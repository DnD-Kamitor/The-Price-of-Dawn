This document is the master spec for every scene in *The Price of Dawn*. It tells you — or any AI model — exactly what goes in each field, what quality it needs to reach, and what it should look like when done. Every session scene is built from this template. When the result is weak, something in this template was skipped or underfilled.

Copy the template block at the bottom of this page for each new scene. Fill every field before running.

---

## What This Campaign Demands

Before filling any template, know these rules. They apply to every scene, every field.

**Stakes in every scene.** Every scene must have something to gain and something to lose. If players can walk in, do the thing, and walk out without cost or consequence, the scene should be cut or redesigned.

**NPCs want things, fear things, and lie about things.** Every named NPC in a scene has a goal they're working toward, a fear that shapes how they behave, and at least one thing they're not saying. If you don't know all three, you're not ready to run the NPC.

**Five senses, every scene.** The amber light of Varenhold is always present. So is the smell of lamp oil, the distant sound of a city waiting for something. Sensory detail anchors players before plot moves.

**The scene has one peak moment.** There is one specific moment in every scene that players will quote afterward. Build toward it. Name it in your prep. Everything else serves that moment.

**Failure is interesting.** If a player fails a roll, the scene does not stop. It changes. Know what changes.

## Field-by-Field Rules

### Scene Header

**Format:** `## Scene [N] — [Name] ([Type])`

**Name:** Short, evocative, not a summary. "The Restorer Ambush" not "Combat Encounter With Cultists." The name should communicate the scene's *character*, not its plot function.

**Type:** One of: Combat / Puzzle / RP / Exploration. If it's more than one, it's two scenes.

**Example from this campaign:** `## Scene 1 — The Restorer Ambush (Combat)`

---

### Opening Lines

**"Players know"** — List only facts they actively carry into this scene from previous events. Not background lore — things that happened in play. Keep to 3–5 bullet points max.

**"Players don't know"** — The specific thing this scene will reveal or move. One line. This is the scene's information payload — name it here so you never lose track of what the scene is actually for.

**"Confirm before running"** — The checklist of physical facts that must be true before you open your mouth. Prop in hand. NPC position known. Handout printed. If any of these are false, stop and fix them. A scene that starts without this confirmation falls apart mid-run.

**Example:**
> Players know: hired to investigate the ritual, heading to the Archive, letter of introduction in pocket.
> Players don't know: someone already knew they were hired before they left the building.
> Confirm before running: sealed note visible on Z4's belt (amber wax) · Edoran 30 ft east in alley · Jaret's dispatch in second pouch.

---

### Quick-Reference Card

This is the entire scene compressed to one scannable block. Fill it in last, once you know the whole scene. A GM who has prepped this session should be able to run from this card alone without reading anything else.

**Purpose** — One sentence explaining why this scene exists in moral or narrative terms. Not what happens — why it matters. Ask: what does this scene *change* about how players understand the campaign?

> Weak: "Players fight cultists on the way to the Archive."
> Strong: "Establishes that someone was already watching before the investigation started — and that the Restorers aren't simply villains."

**Key reveal** — The one fact that must survive this scene regardless of what players do or fail. If a session is a chain of information the players accumulate, this is one link in that chain. State it in plain English.

> Example: "The opposition had the players' route before they knew it themselves — which means the Chancellor's office is not secure, or someone in it is not what they appear."

**Key mechanic** — The number or answer a GM will need without digging for it. DC, puzzle solution, combat condition for victory, whatever the scene turns on mechanically. Put it here so it's instantly findable mid-scene.

> Example: "Puzzle answer: CONSENT — spoken aloud to the door."

**Beats 1/2/3** — Three moments, not three paragraphs. Beat 1 is the setup. Beat 2 is the moment everything pivots. Beat 3 is the exit. Beat 2 is the most important — name it specifically. "Mid-fight, a zealot crouches and holds the folded paper to the lantern flame" is a Beat 2. "Things escalate" is not.

**XP / reward** — Exact numbers. No ranges, no "approximately." If there's a bonus condition, state it. GMs should never calculate at the table.

> Example: "450 XP (2×75 cultists + 3×75 archers) + 100 bonus XP if sealed note is recovered."

**If they skip** — One sentence naming where the key reveal still reaches them. Not "they'll find out eventually." Name the specific scene, NPC, or object.

> Example: "If the note burns, Jaret's dispatch is still on the body. If both are missed, Sera Voss confirms Restorer faction split in Scene 4."

**Three clues** — Three independent paths to the key reveal. They must be genuinely independent: different actions, different skills, different triggers. If two of the three require the same move (e.g., both require searching a body), they aren't independent.

> ① Primary: player examines the wax seal → recognizes Restorer sun-ring symbol
> ② Secondary: Persuasion DC 14 on the captured enforcer → he confirms who sent him
> ③ Passive: the Restorer pamphlet already in their hands uses the same sun-ring symbol on its header — no action required, they're holding it

**NPC positions at scene start** — Not "located in the archive." What the NPC is literally doing with their body right now. Sorting papers. Standing 30 ft east in an alley, watching the junction. Sitting at a table with their back to the door. This is the living dungeon principle: NPCs have schedules, not map positions.

### Scene Description Toggle

**What this section is for:** The GM's context. Everything in here is GM-only — if players could hear it, it goes in Read Aloud instead.

**What is happening** — A factual summary of the situation before players walk in. Not narration. Facts. What exists in the fiction right now that players are about to step into. 2–3 sentences. This is the frame; the read-aloud is the picture.

**Why players are here** — The specific in-fiction reason this party arrived at this moment. Not "the plot requires it." The causal chain from their last action to this location.

**Why they should care** — Personal stakes. Which characters have a reason to care about this scene beyond plot obligation? If you cannot name a specific character connection, the scene may be too abstract. This campaign's moral questions work because they're personal.

**Story relevance** — Where this scene's information or consequences pay off later. Name the scene or session explicitly. "Establishes Edoran's observational role — essential when players encounter him directly in Scene 5." This keeps scenes from feeling like filler.

**Emotional target** — The feeling players carry when they leave. One phrase. Not the event, the emotion. "Watched." "Complicit without meaning to be." "Hopeful about the wrong thing." This is what you're calibrating toward with every decision about the scene.

### Read Aloud Block

**What this section is for:** The words you say out loud at the table. Every word here is player-perceivable. No word outside this block is.

**Rules for writing read-aloud text in this campaign:**

- Open with the senses before the plot. Players need to be in the room before they hear what matters. Amber light. Specific smells. Sounds that belong to this district.
- Use present tense. "The junction is busier than it should be" not "The junction was busier."
- Describe NPCs physically, not psychologically. "She is standing at the east wall with one finger tracing the star chart, not looking up when you enter" not "She seems nervous."
- Do not reveal GM knowledge. "Four figures who are not in the queue, not moving toward it" is correct. "Four Restorer cultists waiting to ambush you" is not.
- End the read-aloud when players have enough to act. Do not narrate their decisions.

**Variants:** If how the read-aloud starts depends on player behavior (cautious vs. fast, combat-ready vs. distracted), write both. Label them clearly. Do not make GMs choose mid-sentence.

**CSS class:** Use `<div class="scene-open">` for the block. Variant labels use `<span class="variant-label">`.

### Environment Toggle

**What this section is for:** Everything in the physical space that a player might interact with. This is your improvisation fuel. If you haven't thought through what happens when they touch something, you'll fumble it.

**Tactical layout** — Dimensions, exits, cover positions, landmarks. Enough for a GM to describe the space without drawing a map. Include: approximate size, number and type of exits, significant objects and where they sit, lighting quality. An ASCII sketch is acceptable and often better than prose.

**Interactive objects** — Every object a player might pick up, open, search, move, climb on, or destroy. One line each: the object, the possible action, the consequence. "Market crate, center of junction: players can overturn it for half cover. Creates difficult terrain in a 10 ft radius."

**Five senses** — Write at least sight, sound, and smell for every scene. This campaign has a consistent sensory vocabulary: amber light (warm, constant, sourceless), lamp oil smell (omnipresent in the Lowmark), the sound of a city under pressure (food queues, coughing, muffled argument). Use it. The moment Varenhold starts sounding like any other city, the campaign loses texture.

**Hidden details** — Every skill check should reveal something specific and useful. "You notice the room is old" is not a skill check result. "Perception DC 14: the eastern bookshelf has been recently moved — there are fresh drag marks in the dust, and the wall behind it is a slightly different shade of stone" is a skill check result.

### NPC Awareness Toggle

**What this section is for:** The schedule and internal state of every named NPC in or near this scene.

**The living dungeon principle:** NPCs are not waiting in their rooms. They are doing something. If players never trigger an NPC, that NPC goes somewhere else and does something else. Know what that is.

For each NPC, fill in four things:

**Right now** — Exact physical action at scene start. The specificity here determines how real the NPC feels. "Standing behind the counter" is not enough. "Standing behind the counter with both hands flat on the wood, watching the door, not the person she's talking to" is enough.

**Knows** — Their complete information state. What they've heard. What they suspect but can't confirm. What they're actively hiding. This is what shapes their dialogue. An NPC who is hiding something will phrase every sentence differently than one who isn't.

**Triggered by** — Specific things that change their behavior. Not "if combat starts" — "if players mention Jaret's name, she stops moving for exactly one breath." These are the moments that make NPCs feel like they have inner lives.

**If never found** — Where they go after this scene if players don't interact with them. They must go somewhere. A well-designed scene has NPCs whose absence in this scene becomes visible in the next one.

---

### Three Clues Toggle

**What this section is for:** Redundancy. Every important revelation in this campaign has three independent paths to it. This section is where you build and verify those paths.

**The rule:** Three clues, truly independent. If ① requires the player to search the body, and ② also requires the player to search the body, you have one clue with two steps, not two clues. Independence means: different player action, different skill, or different location.

**Clue ①** — The primary path. What happens when players do the most obvious thing. No roll required if possible. This is the clue that rewards players who think clearly.

**Clue ②** — The secondary path. A different approach, a different skill, a different NPC. This is the clue that catches players who approach differently than expected.

**Clue ③** — The passive path. This is the hardest clue to write and the most important. It is something already in front of players that confirms the reveal without any deliberate action. A prop they're holding. A phrase in ambient dialogue they'll hear regardless. A visual detail in the read-aloud. The passive clue is often the one players remember two sessions later — "wait, that was in the pamphlet the whole time."

If you cannot write three genuinely independent paths, the revelation is not ready to run. Add paths before the session.

---

### If They Skip Toggle

**What this section is for:** The escape valve. No scene should be a single chokepoint for critical information.

**Likely skip scenarios** — The two or three most plausible ways this scene is bypassed. Players go to Sera before the Archive. They flee without searching the body. They never approach the restricted stacks. Name the actual scenarios for your actual players.

**Where the reveal still reaches them** — Name a specific scene, NPC, or object. Not "they'll figure it out eventually." "If they skip Scene 3 entirely, Sera Voss in Scene 4 will name the distributed anchor mechanism directly if asked how the ritual worked. If they don't ask, Edoran alludes to it in Scene 5."

**What is genuinely lost** — Be honest here. Some things don't recover. If players skip Scene 1's note-recovery, they lose Edoran's signature and the faction-split reveal. They can still learn about the Restorers, but they won't have the evidence of internal disagreement until Session 2. Name the real cost so you can make sure it shows up later.

---

### Encounter Details Toggle *(Combat and Puzzle scenes only)*

**What this section is for:** All mechanical reference for the scene. Stat blocks here, not in an appendix. Numbers you will look up during play, within reach.

**Stat blocks** — Use standard 5e format. Put CR and XP at the top of each block, not buried inside. Abbreviate where possible. A GM running combat does not read full stat blocks — they read AC, HP, and the attack they're about to roll. Put those first.

**Initiative** — Pre-roll NPC initiatives before the session. Write the order here. At the table, insert players where they land. No calculation during play.

**Round-by-round guide** — This is a fallback, not a script. It describes what NPCs do when players don't surprise them. Round 1 is setup. Round 2 is where the designed peak moment fires — name it here and commit to triggering it. Round 3+ describes the retreat or escalation condition.

**Tactical AI** — One line per NPC: what changes their default behavior. "Archers hold position and fire unless more than two players close to melee range, at which point they move to the rooftop access north." This prevents NPCs from doing tactically absurd things because you didn't think it through.

**Secondary objective** — The non-combat goal that runs alongside the fight. This is the most important line in a combat scene. If the only way to win is to kill everyone, the scene is too simple. The secondary objective is what separates a fight from an encounter. In this campaign: protect a document, capture instead of kill, buy time, prevent a specific action. Name it here and make sure it's visible to players from the start of combat.

**For puzzle scenes** — State the solution in plain text. Don't bury it. Then write the hint progression: what additional information players receive after each failed attempt. Then write the bypass path: how a player who refuses the puzzle still gets through or gets the information.

---

### Check on Basics Toggle

**What this section is for:** A pre-session checklist specific to this scene. Run through it before the session begins.

**Rules for the checklist:**
- Every item is checkable in under 10 seconds.
- Every item is scene-specific. "NPC cards ready" is generic. "Edoran's two-line yield speech memorized" is scene-specific.
- The handout line names the prop and the exact moment it transfers to players.
- The transition line is the actual sentence you'll say to move from this scene to the next.
