*Copy this entire page for every new session. Works for any TTRPG campaign. Every placeholder describes what goes in that field — remove the description once you've filled it. Everything is inside a toggle except the Quick-Reference Card, which stays always visible at the top.*

*Guy Sanders / theGreatGM principles are built into every section: stakes in every scene, NPCs with want/fear/lie, secondary objectives in all combat, five senses at every scene open, the encounter beat, the 15-minute prep method, fallbacks for every player decision.*

<details>
<summary>Current Player State — Where the Party Is Right Now</summary>

*Not where they'll end up — where they are at minute zero. This determines how the session opens.*

**Physical location**

*Where the party is standing when the session begins. Specific: district, building, road, time of day, weather if relevant. Enough detail to narrate the opening line from.*

**Party condition**

*HP, spell slots, resources, outstanding conditions, equipment. What they used last session they haven't recovered. What they're short on. This affects how they approach every scene.*

**What they want right now**

*Their active goal — the immediate next step they intend to take. Not the campaign-level goal. "Find the fence who sold the amulet." "Get into the locked tower before dawn." This is where the session begins.*

**Pressure on them**

*What's pushing them forward. Urgency, threat, obligation, moral debt, curiosity. Why they're moving tonight and not resting. A session without pressure is a session that drags. Identify the pressure before you sit down.*

**Relationships under tension**

*Any NPC relationship that shifted last session. Who trusts them more now. Who they've damaged. Who's waiting for them. These are the emotional stakes that make mechanical outcomes matter.*

</details>

<details>
<summary>The Truth Behind the Session — What's Actually Happening</summary>

*GM eyes only. The full picture — the world as it actually is, regardless of what players discover. Filling this lets you improvise from reality instead of from plot.*

**What is actually going on**

*The situation in full. The villain's real plan. The NPC's actual motivation. The conspiracy without the players' distorted lens on it. Write this clearly so that whatever players do, you know what they're bumping up against.*

**NPC schedules — what they're doing if players never show up**

*The living world principle: NPCs don't wait. They have agendas. What is each key NPC doing tonight regardless of player involvement? If the party sits in a tavern all session, what happens in the world?*

- *[NPC name]: [what they're doing and why, in their own time]*
- *[NPC name]: [schedule]*
- *[NPC name]: [schedule]*

**What changes if players do nothing**

*If the party delays, avoids, or fails completely — what is the world like at session end? One paragraph. This is the cost of inaction. It should be real and visible, not punishing.*

**The session's central tension**

*The conflict pulling the fiction apart. Two forces, two people, two ideas in opposition. Players walk into the middle of it. Everything else in the session radiates from this.*

Do not narrate their decisions.*

<details>
<summary>NPC Appearance & Presence</summary>

*Every named NPC gets a full block. Players build mental images from physical specifics, not descriptions. One memorable detail per NPC is worth more than a paragraph. These feed directly into your Read Aloud narration and improvised descriptions throughout the scene.*

**[NPC Name]**

- **Look:** *Age range, build, coloring — 2–3 words each, not a sentence. "Late forties. Compact. Dark-complexioned with close-cropped grey." This is the quick mental picture.*
- **Wearing:** *Specific clothing with one detail that reveals character. Clothes are biography. "Plain merchant's coat, but the boots are military issue and still well-maintained." "Noble-cut doublet, but the cuffs are mended — badly, in a hurry."*
- **Posture and movement:** *Their psychology made physical. How they hold themselves and how they move tells players everything. "Never turns her whole body toward you — always a quarter-turn, like she's ready to leave." "Keeps both hands visible on the table at all times." "He's completely still. Not relaxed — controlled."*
- **One memorable detail:** *The single thing players will describe to each other after the session. A habit under stress. Something incongruous. Something that doesn't fit. Specific enough to belong only to this person. "His voice is calm. His hands aren't." "She keeps touching her left coat pocket without looking at it." "He smells like smoke, and not from a fireplace."*
- **First impression line:** *1–2 sentences, second person, present tense. The words you read when players first see this NPC. Contains posture and the memorable detail. Creates impression without explaining it. "The woman at the far table isn't watching the door — she's watching whoever comes through it. She's been here long enough to have a preferred seat."*

- **Look:** *[Age, build, coloring]*
- **Wearing:** *[Clothing + one revealing detail]*
- **Posture / movement:** *[Personality made physical]*
- **One memorable detail:** *[The thing players describe afterward]*

<summary>Scene Description — GM Context</summary>

*GM eyes only. If players could hear it, it belongs in Read Aloud instead. This is your frame; Read Aloud is the picture.*

**What is happening**

*2–3 sentences. Facts. What exists in the fiction right now. Not narration — the facts behind the narration.*

**Why players are here**

*The causal chain from their last action to this location. Why now. Why them.*

**Why they should care**

*Personal stakes. Not "the plot requires it" — what does this offer or cost a specific character. If you can name a player or character type for whom this hits harder, do it. A scene with no personal hook is texture, not drama.*

**Story relevance**

*What this scene sets up. Name the scene or session where it pays off. "Establishes X — becomes the pivot point in Scene 4." This prevents scenes from feeling like filler.*

**Emotional target** *(Guy Sanders: every scene has an emotional destination)*

*One phrase. The feeling players carry out — not the event, the emotion. "Watched." "Wrong about someone they trusted." "Responsible for something they didn't choose." Every decision about this scene serves this landing.*

</details>

<details>
<summary>Handouts — Props for This Scene</summary>

*Handouts are the most powerful tool at the table. A physical prop makes information tangible. Players hold the evidence. Every prop must earn its place — it should reveal something, raise a question, or deepen a relationship.*

**Handout: [Name]**

- **What it is physically:** *Describe the object before the content — material, condition, size, how sealed or stored. Players look at it before they read it. "A folded square of parchment, sealed with black wax and a tower stamp. Expensive paper — whoever sent this expected it kept." "A small iron key, old, number scratched into the bow: 7. Filed mark on one edge."*
- **Trigger moment:** *The exact in-scene event that puts this in players' hands. Specific enough to execute without deciding at the table. "After combat, if players search the body — inside the left boot, under the lining." "When players successfully open the iron door." "The innkeeper sets it on the table before they can speak."*
- **Handoff narration:** *One sentence, in the scene's tone, spoken while sliding the prop across. "You find it folded beneath the coin purse — placed deliberately, not casually." "She doesn't say anything when she hands it over. She watches your face instead."*
- **What it reveals without checks:** *The information payload — what players learn from reading or examining it normally.*
- **What a skill check adds:** *DC [N] [Skill]: [additional layer — something about the person who made it, or something hidden in the object itself]*
- **How it connects forward:** *The future scene, NPC, or revelation this feeds. "The symbol on the seal matches the one on the second document in Scene 3 — if players connect them, they know the same person sent both."*

</details>

<details>
<summary>Three Clues — Information Architecture</summary>

*Three independent paths to the key reveal. Independent means different action, different skill, different NPC, different location. Two clues that both require searching the same object are one clue with two steps. If you can't write three genuinely independent paths, the scene is fragile — add paths before running.*

**The reveal:** *[The one fact in plain English. One sentence.]*

---

**Clue ①** — Primary *(obvious action — ideally no roll)*

*What happens when players do the most natural thing. This rewards players who think clearly and act directly.*

---

**Clue ②** — Secondary *(independent of ①)*

*A different approach reaching the same fact. Survives if ① is skipped or failed. Must not share a precondition with ①.*

DC [N] [Skill] — or condition: *[what triggers this path]*

---

**Clue ③** — Passive *(no roll, no deliberate action)*

*Something already in front of players that confirms the reveal without them having to do anything right. A prop in their hands. Ambient dialogue they hear regardless. A visible detail in the Read Aloud. Players may not understand it now — they'll recognize it when the reveal lands.*

</details>
