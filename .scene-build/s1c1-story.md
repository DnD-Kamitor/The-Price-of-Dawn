## Why This Scene Exists

Scene 1 is not an action tutorial. It is the first sentence of the campaign's real argument: *power is already being exercised over the Dawnborn, by people who believe they're doing it for good reasons, before the players know what the Dawnborn are.* The ambush happens before the players know enough to have an opinion about it. That is precisely the point. By the time they have an opinion, they will remember that they were already being moved by forces with incomplete information — and so were the people trying to stop them.

Everything in this scene is a seed. The note is a seed. Edoran's absence is a seed. The food queue is a seed. The Enforcer's silence is a seed. None of them sprout in Session 1. The GM's job in this scene is not payoff — it is planting with enough craft that the players, two sessions later, feel the roots when they hit them.

## 1. What This Scene Pays Off

### Archetype Hooks — Immediate Activation

Each of the six archetypes from the Player's Guide has a specific hook that Scene 1 touches on contact.

**The Investigator** was hired under false context — they were told the documents were stolen property, not the key to ten people's deaths. Scene 1 immediately confirms that whoever briefed them omitted something significant: people are already willing to use force to stop this investigation. An investigator's first instinct is to ask *who benefits from warning us off?* That question is not answerable yet, but it is the right question, and Scene 1 is what makes it urgent.

**The Believer** came because of religious obligation, ideological commitment, or a personal promise. The ambushers are Restorers — Auris-faith adjacent, driven by grief that has organized itself into theology. Scene 1 places the Believer in the position of being attacked by people whose faith may not be entirely unlike their own. The question it opens: *is the difference between a zealot and a believer just the desperation of their circumstances?*

**The Survivor** has someone they love declining from grey sickness. They came for the city, for the people in the queue. Scene 1 gives them the queue — fifty shuffling people who are the living embodiment of what the twilight costs — and then forces them to fight in front of it. The note in the zealot's pouch is theologically gentle. The Restorer who tries to burn it is not. That dissonance is the Survivor's whole arc in miniature.

**The Scholar** believes that information, correctly understood, leads to correct action. Scene 1 presents information being actively suppressed — the note-burning attempt is an attempt to destroy a clue before it can be read. The Scholar's background hook (corresponding with Theron, being recruited without knowing it) connects directly: Theron left things findable on purpose. Someone else is trying to make them unfindable. The Scholar is now in the middle of that contest.

**The Mercenary** has a job: locate the ritual documents. Scene 1 tells them the job is more complicated than they were briefed. People with organization behind them know the players' route. A mercenary who has been ignoring signs that something was wrong now has a very loud sign. The question Scene 1 opens for them: *at what point does completing the contract become a choice you're responsible for?* That is also the campaign's question for Sessions 4 and 5.

**The Dawnborn-Adjacent** came because Sera asked. Sera is the kind of person who runs toward burning buildings, who notices specific people, who has been waiting for the right moment to say something out loud. Scene 1 happens in Sera's district. The food queue is Sera's community. The ambush is in the street she walks every day. When players reach her at the Dawnhall in Scene 4, they will already know what she is protecting — because they have just fought in the middle of it.

### From the Chancellor Briefing

The Chancellor told players three things: the Archive contains Corven's documents, the city has three good harvests left, and she is trusting them to find the truth she cannot look for publicly. What she did not say: someone is already trying to stop exactly this investigation. What she let them infer: the political landscape is complicated. What Scene 1 begins answering:

- *How organized is the opposition?* Organized enough to have the players' route on the day they start. This is not a spontaneous reaction to their presence — it is a prepared response. Someone knew they were coming.
- *Why would the Chancellor claim ignorance of the cost?* Because people who look into this get warned off. She has read enough to know the warning is credible.
- *What is this investigation actually about?* The Chancellor framed it as locating documents. Scene 1 reframes it as walking into a contested space where the stakes are already high enough to justify violence.

The Chancellor briefing raised the question: *is this a job, or is it something else?* Scene 1 answers: it is something else. The Mercenary archetype knows this feeling.

### The Campaign Logline in Miniature

*"Ten people are carrying the sun inside them. The city needs it back. Whose choice is that to make?"*

Scene 1 is this question at the scale of thirty seconds of combat. The zealots have decided the choice belongs to the ritual. Edoran has decided the choice belongs to the theology of willing hearts. The players have been hired by someone who has decided to remain strategically ignorant of whose choice it is. And then the zealots try to burn the note that contains the only evidence — so far — that Edoran believes in consent at all.

*Whose choice is it to make?* In Scene 1, it is the note-burner's choice. Unless the players stop them. That is the micro-version of the macro: the question is always whether someone will intervene in the act of unilateral decision-making, and what it costs them to do so.

## 2. What This Scene Plants

### a) The Sealed Note — Session 2 Payoff

The note reads: *"The willing heart gives freely what the bound heart cannot."* This is Auris scripture in Edoran's handwriting.

**When it matters:** Session 2, Scene 4, Theron's confession. The players now know the Dawnborn are Lux Anchors — that completing the ritual means the technical term *extinguished*. In the silence after Theron tells them this, one player will reach for the note. Or the GM should reach for it — have Theron recognize the handwriting.

**The Session 2 beat:** After Theron finishes, when the players are sitting in the aftermath of the worst sentence they've heard in this campaign, the GM says: *"The note is still in your pocket. You read it again. Edoran's handwriting. 'The willing heart gives freely what the bound heart cannot.' He knew. He knew what the cost was, and he was sending people to warn you off, and he still wrote that."*

This does two things at once. It confirms that Edoran knows the ritual kills the Dawnborn. And it confirms that Edoran believes the difference between willing and forced is morally load-bearing — which means he has not, in fact, decided the choice belongs to the ritual or to him. He has decided it belongs to the Dawnborn. Everything else he is doing — the warnings, the intercepts, the ambushes — is his attempt to prevent anyone from forcing the decision before the Dawnborn can make it freely.

That is not the reading of a fanatic. It is the reading of someone who is terrified that events will move faster than consent can.

**If the note was burned:** The handwriting recognition beat is unavailable, but the underlying revelation still lands in Scene 5 of Session 1 — Edoran's words carry the same implication. See section (b) below. The three-clue rule applies: the note is one path to Edoran's position; his words are a second; his willingness to be seen in the alley at all is a third.

### b) Edoran's Words — Session 2 Echo

*"You have no idea what you are looking for. When you find it, you will understand why I did not want you to."*

**When it echoes:** Session 2, Scene 4. The exact moment Theron says the word *extinguished*.

**The GM line:** Let Theron finish. Let the silence run. Then, quietly, without signaling that this is a callback: *"You have no idea what you are looking for. When you find it, you will understand why I did not want you to."* Do not attribute it. Let the players place it.

They will. Because Edoran was right. He did not want them to find this — not because he wanted to suppress it, but because he knew what finding it would cost them. He has known for long enough that his grief has calcified into theology. He organized it into a movement. He sent people to stop the investigation. And he was still standing in that alley, thirty feet away, watching, saying something that turned out to be true.

That is not a villain's speech. It is the speech of someone who has been carrying the same information as Theron for years, and made entirely different choices with it — and still landed on *willing heart* as the operative moral principle.

The callback does not redeem Edoran's methods. It complicates them, which is more useful.

### c) The Food Queue — Session 3 Weight

Fifty people shuffling forward in the amber lanternlight. A vendor calling out quantities. Bundled figures. A child somewhere in the line.

**What it means in Session 3:** Session 3's faction choice is the moment players must decide how far they are willing to compromise toward a path that might not be clean. Isolde Maren's transfer apparatus offers a possibility — but with mortality risk. The Inversion path requires unanimous consent. The ritual-as-designed kills ten people.

When players reach that decision, they are deciding in the abstract: ten lives against a city. The abstraction is easy to work with. The fifty people in the queue are not abstract. The GM's job, in Session 3, is to collapse that abstraction back to the specific.

*"You remember the food queue. Fifty people. The vendor calling quantities. The child somewhere in the line. Three good harvests remaining, the Chancellor said. Those fifty people have three good harvests remaining. So does the child. Whose choice is it to make?"*

The moral weight of the Session 3 decision is real because the fifty people were real. Not named, not spoken to, not given individual stories — but real, present, sensory. Five senses. The cost was in the room before the players knew they were being asked to calculate it.

### d) The Enforcer's Silence — The Truth That Lands Later

**What players think it means:** The Enforcer is loyal, well-trained, professionally disciplined. He will not name his employer because he has been instructed not to. This is a competent operation. Standard interpretation: Restorer zealotry with better organization than expected.

**What it actually means:** The Enforcer is silent because naming Edoran would expose the Restorers' knowledge of the players' route, which would expose the network that provided it. The Enforcer is not silent out of personal loyalty — he is silent because the thing he would have to explain is more damaging than the ambush itself.

**When the truth lands:** Session 2, Scene 1. The Reckoning moves on the Archive on the same day the players return. The Reckoning also knew the players' timeline. Two different organizations — Restorers and Reckoning — both had advance intelligence on the players' movements. Either the players have a leak, or both organizations have someone watching the same source.

The source is the Chancellor's office. The Enforcer's silence in Scene 1 is the first indication that the information network underlying this conflict is more sophisticated than an ambush suggests. Players who revisit this in Session 2 will understand: the Enforcer wasn't protecting Edoran. He was protecting the shared infrastructure.

This is not something players need to decode in Session 1. But the GM should note it, because when the Session 2 realization lands, the Enforcer's silence retroactively explains itself.

### e) The Route Intelligence — Edoran's Network

The zealots had the players' route. The players left the Chancellor's offices not long before the ambush. That is the specific thing to hold.

**What it tells us about Edoran's network:** He has either someone inside the Chancellor's operation, or someone watching the office's exits closely enough to track new contractors on day one. Either possibility means the Restorers are not simply a theological movement with pamphlets and Sevenday meetings. They have institutional reach.

**When that network shows up again:** Session 2, the Reckoning arrives at the Archive the same morning the players do. Session 3, Edoran finds the players before they reach him — or has sent a contact in advance who knows to expect them. Session 4, Edoran appears at exactly the moment the players need him, before they've told anyone where they're going.

Each of these beats should feel, in retrospect, connected to the same fact established in Scene 1: this organization tracks people who are investigating the ritual. They have been tracking this for long enough that it is operational muscle memory, not reactive improvisation.

When players finally reach Edoran in Session 1, Scene 5, and he says *"You have no idea what you are looking for"* — part of what that means is: *I know exactly what you have been doing since you left the Chancellor's offices this morning. I have people who told me. That's how worried I am.*

## 4. Three-Clue Rule — Information This Scene Provides

Scene 1 is primarily a clue delivery mechanism for one core piece of information: **Edoran is not simply a fanatic — he believes in consent.**

The campaign depends on players being able to reach this conclusion before Session 4. If they dismiss Edoran as an antagonist and stop thinking about him, the moral complexity of Sessions 3-5 is diminished. Scene 1 gives three separate routes to the same conclusion:

---

**Clue 1: The Note**
- *What it contains:* Auris scripture in Edoran's handwriting. *"The willing heart gives freely what the bound heart cannot."*
- *What it points to:* Edoran theologically prioritizes consent. He is not directing violence toward the Dawnborn — he is directing it toward investigators. The distinction matters.
- *How players get it:* Retrieving the note before round 2 ends, before the second zealot burns it.
- *Backup path if missed:* The Factions Guide's GM-only section confirms Edoran's position. Jaret (Scene 4 of Session 1, in the Dawnhall approach) will tell players that Edoran believes the willing heart is the only valid ritual mechanism, if asked directly. Theron in Session 2 will reference "a Restorer leader who kept writing to him arguing that the Dawnborn must choose freely."

---

**Clue 2: Edoran's Words**
- *What they contain:* *"You have no idea what you are looking for. When you find it, you will understand why I did not want you to."*
- *What they point to:* Edoran knows what the cost is. He did not want this investigation to succeed — not to suppress the truth, but because he understands the weight of what finding the truth will require from whoever finds it.
- *How players get it:* Reaching the alley in Scene 5 before Edoran decides the conversation is over. DC 12 Perception or active scanning of the street.
- *Backup path if missed:* Any Restorer contact in later sessions will convey that their leader "watches everything" and "has known the cost for years." Jaret specifically will say: "Brother Edoran has carried this longer than the Chancellor has."

---

**Clue 3: The Competence of the Intercept**
- *What it contains:* The zealots had the players' route on day one. This is logistical intelligence, not spontaneous reaction.
- *What it points to:* Edoran's organization has been watching this investigation's parameters long enough to prepare for it before it started. This is not the behavior of a movement that wants to destroy the evidence — it is the behavior of a movement that wants to control when and how the evidence is used.
- *How players get it:* By thinking about it. No roll required. The information is in the setup text the GM reads aloud: *"Edoran sent them to warn off anyone investigating the ritual."* Warn off — not silence permanently. Not kill. Warn.
- *Backup path if missed:* Session 2, Theron confirms the Restorers have been "active around the Archive perimeter for weeks." Session 3, a Desperate contact mentions that Edoran's people knew about the players before the players knew about Edoran.

---

**Clue 4 (Secondary): The Zealots' Restraint**
- *What it contains:* The zealots' goal is to grab one player as a hostage, not to kill. The Enforcer goes still, hands up, saying nothing, when brought to half HP. No one dies in this scene unless players cause it.
- *What it points to:* Edoran's organization is operating under orders that preserve human life. This is a warning operation, not an elimination operation.
- *Backup path if missed:* Scene 5's second ambush has the same restraint signature. Three zealots and an Enforcer whose specific instruction is to keep people away from Edoran, not to kill them. The pattern of non-lethal intent is the clue.

## 5. If Skipped or Failed Completely

### Minimum Information for Sessions 2-5

If Scene 1 is entirely skipped (players teleport past the Lowmark, the GM cuts it for time, the players ignore the encounter entirely):

The session functions, but players arrive at Scene 2 without knowing the investigation is already being contested. They will discover this anyway — the Archive's restricted stacks have been tampered with (a detail Theron mentions), and the route intelligence will surface in Session 2 when the Reckoning moves on the same timeline.

The critical missing element is **Edoran's identity as someone who believes in consent**. This must be recovered before Session 3's faction choice.

### Recovery Paths by Clue

**If the note is burned:** Jaret, at the Dawnhall approach (Session 1, before Scene 4 or at the session break), will mention that "Brother Edoran keeps a particular scripture close — the one about the willing heart. He says it to anyone who asks why the Restorers won't simply force the issue." This is the recovery path. Use it if the note is gone.

**If players never reach Edoran in Scene 5:** Edoran finds them. He sends a message through Jaret between sessions — a brief note in the same handwriting as the (possibly burned) note. It says only: *"When you understand what you have found, I am in the Wounded temple Sevenday evenings."* He is not hiding. He never was. The ambushes were delay tactics, not a permanent barrier.

**If players take the wrong lesson (Restorers = bad guys, Edoran = villain):**

Do not correct this in Scene 1 or Scene 2. Let it sit.

The correction comes in two movements:

**Scene 4, Session 1 (Sera at the Dawnhall):** Sera mentions, without naming him, that there is a Restorer leader she has "complicated feelings about." She doesn't think he's wrong about what the city needs. She thinks he's wrong about his right to manage the process. She says: *"The people I trust least are the ones who are certain they're protecting me from information I couldn't handle."* She is talking about Edoran. She is also describing the Chancellor. She is also, in a different way, describing what the players are doing to her in this scene.

This does not tell players Edoran is sympathetic. It tells them Sera — the person they're falling in love with, the person whose face they will see when Theron says *extinguished* — has nuance about him. That is enough.

**Session 5, Edoran's final appearance (if players have pursued him):** If players have spent the campaign treating Edoran as an antagonist, his final appearance should be the moment they realize he is the only person in the conflict who held the theological line on consent from the beginning. He is not there to be right. He is there to have been consistently right about one thing in a campaign where no one gets to be simply right. The Dawnborn who chose freely — the ones who gave the word — those are Edoran's argument, vindicated.

Do not hand this to the players. Let them arrive at it. Edoran does not explain himself in Session 5. He just looks at the Dawnborn at their stones and nods, once, like a man watching something he has been waiting for.

## 6. Moral Weight Analysis

### The Micro-Choice as Campaign Architecture

The three choices Scene 1 offers — retrieve the note, pursue Edoran, capture or release the Enforcer — are structurally identical to the choices of Sessions 4 and 5, compressed into the space of a single combat encounter. Retrieving the note is the choice to pursue information at cost. Pursuing Edoran is the choice to seek a direct conversation with the person who would rather you didn't have this information. Capturing or releasing the Enforcer is the choice about what you do with someone who was acting under orders they didn't fully understand, who will not tell you who sent them, and who poses no further threat once the operation has failed.

These are the same questions the campaign asks at scale: *Do you have the right to the information? Do you seek out the person who is withholding it? What do you do with people who were tools in someone else's moral decision?*

The players will answer all three of these questions in Session 1 before they know they're answering them. That is the function of the micro-choice. It establishes character position — this player pursues the information at all costs, this player releases the Enforcer, this player goes for Edoran while the rest fight — before the stakes are high enough to feel like a test. By Session 5, those established positions are the ground the characters are standing on when the actual test arrives. The GM should remember what each player chose in Scene 1. So, if possible, should the players.

### Consent as Structure, Not Theme

The Archive door in Scene 2 asks players to name CONSENT as the answer to a riddle. That is the campaign's central word arriving in capital letters, unmissable, at the end of Session 1's first hour. But the scene that actually embeds consent as a structural concern is Scene 1, not Scene 2.

When the second zealot tries to stamp the note into the lantern flame, that is an act of withholding. It is not Edoran's act — he didn't order the burning, he ordered the interception. But his organization, acting on his behalf, is attempting to destroy a document that contains evidence of his own theological commitment to the willing heart. The zealot burning Edoran's words about consent is one of the campaign's cleanest structural ironies. The most charitable reading of Edoran's operation — that he genuinely believes in the Dawnborn's right to choose — is being suppressed by his own people, in his name, without his direct instruction, in front of the players.

This is what organizations do to the moral positions of leaders. The Restorer Fanatics (Jaret's wing) will do this to a much greater degree in Sessions 3 and 4. But it begins here, in Scene 1, with a burning note. The answer to Scene 2's riddle — CONSENT — is what Scene 1's note was about. The answer was always present. Someone was trying to burn it before it could be found.

### The Cost Must Be Real Because the People Were Real

The campaign's central moral weight — ten lives against a city — is an abstraction that the campaign earns over five sessions by refusing to let it stay abstract. The Dawnborn become individuals. Sera becomes someone specific, someone the players will see at the moment Theron says *extinguished*. The city becomes the food queue, the grey sickness, the vendor calling quantities, the child somewhere in the line.

Scene 1 is the first act of that process. Fifty people in amber lanternlight, shuffling forward. The players fight in front of them. The crowd presses behind them. The five-senses opening — lamp oil and old bread, boots on stone, the dryness of a city indoors for fifty years — is not atmosphere. It is the cost being made embodied before the players understand there is a cost to calculate.

When they reach Session 3 and must decide whether the Transfer path's mortality risk is acceptable, whether the Inversion path's requirement for unanimous genuine consent is achievable, whether the ritual-as-designed is something they can permit — they are not deciding in a vacuum. They are deciding in reference to fifty people in the Lowmark and a food vendor and a child and the particular dryness of fifty years of amber air. The decision will be made in their bodies as well as their minds, because Scene 1 put the cost in their bodies first.

That is why Scene 1 begins in the food queue rather than in the Chancellor's office or the Archive. The Chancellor's office is where the job begins. The food queue is where the campaign begins.
