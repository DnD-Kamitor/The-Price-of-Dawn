## Scene Function and Tone

This is the session's pivot scene. Everything before this — the combat, the riddle — was approach. This is the door opening. The box is not a prop that players interact with and move past. The box is the scene. Plan accordingly.

The pacing target is 20–30 minutes of table time. A group that tears through the puzzle in five minutes is possible — and if they do, the scene is not done. The puzzle is not the point. The puzzle is the ceremony.

## The Ceremony of Opening

Theron has been waiting eleven years for someone to open that box. He put it on the desk himself. He stood in this room waiting for the players. He has been carrying this alone long enough that the act of someone else speaking those names has accumulated enormous weight.

When players are working through the puzzle — examining documents, discussing the names, cross-referencing the colophons — do not hurry them. Let the room be quiet. Let Theron be present in his corner. The silence is part of the scene.

When a player speaks a correct name for the first time: slow down. Describe the indentation warming. Describe the glow. Do not rush to "okay, what's the next one." Let the players sit with the first correct name for a beat. Someone named Corven helped perform an act that changed ten lives and created the central problem of this entire campaign. Saying his name aloud, with the box responding to it, is a moment.

If the table is in a hurry, they are in a hurry. Do not force ceremony on reluctant players. But if the table is quiet when the first name is spoken, let that quiet stand.

## Pacing by Group Type

**Investigator-heavy group:** They will find the names in the documents quickly. Let them. The puzzle itself is not the obstacle for this group — the conversation is. After the box opens, stay in the room. Theron has things to say. So does the anonymous sentence at the bottom of page 12.

**RP-first group:** They may spend the first ten minutes asking Theron questions before even looking at the documents. This is correct behavior. Do not nudge them toward the documents prematurely. Let them exhaust their questions. The documents will still be there. The puzzle will wait.

**Action-first group:** They may reach for Thieves' Tools immediately. Let them try. Note the DC. If they succeed, the scene still happens — the consequence of the scorched edges and Theron's reaction is its own story. If they fail, they take the fire damage and someone is now invested in solving the puzzle properly. Either way, move forward.

**Mixed group:** The most common case. Someone is reading documents, someone is asking Theron questions, someone is examining the box. This is ideal. Let all three things happen simultaneously. The documents-reader will find the names; the Theron-questioner may get the secondary objective unprompted; the box-examiner will notice the warmth.

## The Secondary Objective

Someone must ask Theron: *"Why did you never open it yourself?"*

This is the scene's real content. The puzzle is the method of entry. The secondary objective is the revelation about what kind of man Theron is, and what the box has cost him.

Do not push this question. Do not have Theron volunteer it. If the entire table is focused on the puzzle and no one asks, Theron will not offer it. That is fine — it means they get a cleaner, more mechanical scene, and the revelation comes later in Scene 4 (The Confession). But if someone asks, give the answer fully. Do not abbreviate it.

When Theron says "I could not say their names. Not out loud. Not here." — pause. Let that sit. Then, if the player follows up:

*"I pressed the first indentation. Six months after I sealed it. I stood here, in this room, with my hand on the lid, and I knew what to say. I knew all three names. I knew the order. I stood there for — I don't know. Four minutes, perhaps. I put it back on the shelf."*

If they ask about the second attempt: *"Three years ago. Anniversary of the ritual. I thought the distance would have made it easier. It was worse, if anything. I understood more by then."*

If they ask what he understood: *"That the names were not the problem. The names are just names. What I could not do was say them to the box, in this room, and make it real. Saying them means being the person who knew and acted. I didn't want to be that person alone."*

That last sentence is the secondary objective payoff. Players who hear it get something the box does not contain.

## If Players Solve It Too Fast

Fast solutions happen. If the table identifies the colophon names immediately and speaks them into the box in two minutes of table time, the puzzle is over. Do not manufacture obstacles. Do not have the box malfunction or add additional requirements.

Instead: the box is now open. The documents are in front of them. Use the remaining time on what is inside — particularly the key passage ("the completion requires their release") and the anonymous sentence at the bottom.

Read the key passage aloud, slowly. Pause at "release." Let the word sit. Ask: "What does your character understand 'release' to mean?" The players may have already met Sera or Tomas or Lira. If they have, this question will produce different answers from different players, and that conversation is worth more than the puzzle that was supposed to take longer.

The anonymous sentence is always worth lingering on: *"He told me what it meant. I don't think he knew I would write it down."* Ask Theron about it. He doesn't know who wrote it. That mystery is planted here to grow.

## If Players Miss the Conversation Entirely

Some groups will solve the puzzle, take the documents, and move toward the door. No secondary objective. No discussion of why Theron never opened it himself.

This is valid. Let them go. The revelation carries forward — Theron's inability to say the names will surface again in Scene 4, when he gives his full confession. The players just lost a layer of texture, not a load-bearing story element.

What you can do without being heavy-handed: when the players are leaving, Theron says, quietly, to no one in particular:

*"I should have done that ten years ago."*

He doesn't explain. He doesn't look at them when he says it. Players who pick up on it can ask. Players who don't are carrying the documents out the door. Both are fine.

## Theron After the Box Opens

He stays in the study. He does not follow the players out. He is going to sit with this room for a while before he goes anywhere. If players leave without Scene 4 happening here, it will happen in a different location and a different format — but it will happen, because Theron has now passed the burden and needs to complete the handoff.

If Scene 4 (the confession) happens immediately after the box opens — meaning Theron volunteers his fuller explanation in this room, because the box being open broke something loose in him — let it. The scene division in the session outline is a planning convenience. If the scenes merge, they merge.

## Technical Notes

**The narrow window:** afternoon amber light enters from the north. This means the study gets indirect light, not direct. The room never becomes bright enough to eliminate shadow. The lamp at the landing behind the players is the primary light source. This keeps the study slightly dimmer than the reading room below. Appropriate for what is about to happen.

**The shelf gap:** once the box is on the desk, the gap on the east-wall shelf is visible. If anyone looks at the shelf during the scene, describe the gap. The shape of an absence is part of what the room communicates. The box was there for eleven years. The shelf remembers it.

**Temperature:** the study is cooler than the reading room, which had the fireplace and the body heat of the combat. No heat source up here except the lamp and the box itself. The warmth of the box is more noticeable in the cool room than it would be elsewhere. That contrast is worth playing.
