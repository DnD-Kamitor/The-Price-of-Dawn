## The Shift After Scene 1

Scene 1 ended with soldiers. Scene 2 begins with paper.

Do not rush this transition. Give the room a moment to settle after the last combat beat. The players may be checking injuries, looting bodies, or asking about doors — let them. Then Theron emerges from the alcove.

He does not announce himself dramatically. He does not explain himself before he acts. He walks to his desk, unlocks the bottom drawer, and sets three small slips of paper on the nearest reading table. Then he sits down across from them.

The silence before he speaks is the first piece of craft in this scene. Let it exist. The players will fill it or they won't. Either way, the slips are on the table.

> *"I encoded them myself. The cipher is simple enough that someone who wanted to find the answer could find it. That was intentional."*

That is the opening. Nothing before it. That contrast — the sound of a fight, then the sound of one man's careful sentence — is the scene's first emotional note.

---

## Reading the Slips Aloud

Read them at a normal pace. Read them as if they are slightly odd but not obviously coded. Do not pause meaningfully on "third circuit" or "willing vessels" or any other flagged phrase. The players should notice those on their own.

**Recommend providing a handout.** The puzzle is designed to be solved in writing, and players who are working from memory will miss it or spend unnecessary table time trying to reconstruct the text. If you use a VTT, drop the text in chat. If you play at a table, print the three slips or write them on index cards and pass them over when the players ask. Theron, in-fiction, slides the physical slips across the table when the players show interest.

If players ask to hear them read again, Theron reads them aloud himself — slowly, precisely, without inflection.

---

## Theron as the GM's Primary Instrument

The puzzle is the door. Theron is the scene.

While players are working on the cipher, Theron is sitting across from them and doing almost nothing. That almost-nothing is the performance. He is:

- Watching. Not hovering — watching. The way someone watches a door they have been waiting eleven years for someone to walk through.
- Not filling silence. When players discuss approaches, he does not comment, correct, or guide except through the formal hint system. He has a lot of practice being quiet.
- Present in his body in a way that players' Insight checks will reward. His stillness is different from indifference. If players succeed on DC 12 Insight, they read that he wants this. He is hoping.

When players are stuck, resist the urge to have Theron speak. His silence during a pause is more interesting than a reassurance. The reassurance, when it comes, is: *"Keep trying. The pattern is simpler than it looks."* Use it once per failed attempt.

## When to Offer Hints

Only after a complete, committed attempt has failed — not after a partial fumble or a stray idea.

A complete attempt is: a player presents a method, applies it to the text, and reads what they get. It does not produce the answer. That is a failed attempt.

A player saying "what if it's every other word?" and then immediately saying "no wait, let me try something else" has not made a full attempt. Let them finish their second thought.

Dead ends matter. The experience of ruling out sentence-initial letters, ruling out acrostics, ruling out initials — this builds the players' relationship with the text. They are spending time with Theron's mind. That is the scene.

## The Moment of Decoding

When a player reads "THE DAWNBORN ARE THE VESSEL THE ENERGY LIVES WITHIN THEM" aloud — stop.

Do not have Theron respond immediately. Ask the table to hold for a second. Let the sentence exist in the room alone.

Then ask what Theron does.

His response is: *"Yes."* One word. Pause. Then: *"I've been saying that word in here for eleven years."* His hand goes to his chest, not his head.

Do not move past this quickly. If a player responds immediately — asks a question, makes a comment — hold it. Let the "Yes" land first. One breath. Then resume.

This is the campaign's first declarative truth. The players will remember where they were when they heard it.

---

## What If No One Decodes It

The puzzle failing is not a dead end. Theron wanted them to decode it, but he was always going to tell them. The encoded slips were the ceremony, not the only possible path.

Third-attempt recovery initiates when Theron reaches for the slips and the players hold onto them (or refuse to return them). He sits back. His hands go flat on the table. He says *"All right,"* and tells them in plain language. Move directly to Scene 4 RP — skip Scene 3 (the resonance box) or resequence it to happen after the confession, whichever flows better.

The players who didn't decode it will know this. They will carry that into the rest of the session. That is fine. The information reaches them either way. The ceremony of decoding it is a bonus, not a requirement.

## The Scene's Function in Pacing

Session 2 structure:

- **Scene 1:** Combat (high energy)
- **Scene 2:** Puzzle (medium energy, intellectual)
- **Scene 3:** Evidence (low energy, revelatory)
- **Scene 4:** Confession / RP (low energy, emotional weight)
- **Scene 5:** Decision point (escalating)

Scene 2 is the decompression after Scene 1 and the preparation for Scene 3. It should feel slower. That slowness is correct. The players are coming down from adrenaline and moving toward understanding. The puzzle gives their hands something to do while their minds catch up.

If the table is restless after Scene 1 and the puzzle feels like a gear-shift problem — normalize it. The combat was what the Reckoning wanted. This, the paper, the silence, the old man across the table, is what the Reckoning does not know about. That framing helps players who run hot understand why this matters.

---

## Timing

Average time for this scene: 15–25 minutes.

- 5 minutes: Theron emerges, sets slips, delivers opening line. Players read or receive slips.
- 10–15 minutes: puzzle work. One or two failed approaches, hints, final solve.
- 3–5 minutes: the "Yes" moment and immediate aftermath before Theron says "There's one more thing."

If the table solves it in under 10 minutes (History background knowledge + good deduction): let it happen. The speed does not diminish the scene. Move through the aftermath with the same weight.

If the table is still working at 25 minutes: initiate the third-attempt recovery. This is not a failure condition. It is the alternate path.
