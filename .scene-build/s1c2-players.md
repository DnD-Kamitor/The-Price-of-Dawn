## 1. Per-Archetype Engagement

This scene has no combat. Every beat here is observation, deduction, dialogue, or puzzle. Each archetype arrives at the Archive door with a different frame on what they are looking at — and the riddle answers differently for each of them.

---

### The Investigator
*Rogue / Ranger / Wizard / Bard — truth-seeker, possibly operating under false employer context*

**FIRST NOTICE**
The notation beside the door, not the door itself. The Investigator's instinct in any unfamiliar space is to read the room before touching anything in it, and what the Archive antechamber offers first is information: the carved inscription, the quality of the stonework, the fact that the door has no visible lock mechanism — only the notation, only the question. A door with no lock but a puzzle is a door that someone chose to make philosophical rather than physical. The Investigator wants to know who made that choice, and why.

**INSTINCTIVE APPROACH**
Treat the riddle as an interrogation subject. The Investigator reads the phrasing the way they would read a deposition — looking for what the language is doing rather than what it is saying. "Given only when no one compels it" — that is a legal framing, not a philosophical one. "Forced, I become something else entirely" — that is a taxonomy. Someone who thinks in terms of what a thing becomes under duress wrote this. The Investigator will want to parse each line before attempting an answer, not because they are slow but because they know that the wrong answer to a riddle, stated confidently, is worse than silence.

**LIKELY QUESTION TO GM**
*"Before I answer anything, I want to understand the mechanism. What happens when someone gets it wrong? Is there a visible consequence, a sound, anything that tells me what the wrong-answer state looks like?"*

**GM ANSWER THAT SHINES**
Give them the professional read they are asking for: *"On the first wrong answer: nothing mechanical. The door stays shut. But the notation shifts slightly — not the letters themselves, but something about the spacing, the way it sits on the stone. It looks exactly as it did before. Except that it is slightly more patient. Like someone waiting for you to catch up. On the second wrong attempt, before you have time to try a third, you hear boots on the upper stair: measured, deliberate, not hurrying. Someone in this building has been notified."* The Investigator now knows the riddle is monitored. That is the information that matters most to them — not the answer, but the system behind it.

---

### The Believer
*Cleric / Paladin / Druid — faith-driven, may know Auris or Morthis tradition*

**FIRST NOTICE**
The theological weight of the riddle itself. For the Believer, this is not an abstract puzzle — it is a doctrinal statement carved in stone. "Given only when no one compels it" is the definition of willing sacrifice in Auris theology. "Forced, I become something else entirely" is the line that distinguishes the Open Hand canticles from the Binding rites. "The difference between a gift and a theft" is the entire argument the Wounded faction has been making for fifty years about what the Dawnborn are owed. The Believer recognizes that they are standing in front of a door that has a theological position on consent, and that theological position is coherent, considered, and old.

**INSTINCTIVE APPROACH**
Engage with the riddle as a text, not a lock. The Believer will want to read the inscription aloud — not to perform it, but because sacred texts are meant to be spoken, and speaking them is a form of engagement. They may pause mid-reading, because the second line lands differently when heard than when seen. They are not in a hurry. They know what this is about. The answer arrives from somewhere practiced, not somewhere clever.

**LIKELY QUESTION TO GM**
*"This riddle sounds like it's from something. Like a liturgy or a formal teaching document. Does my character recognize the tradition — is this Auris, Morthis, something else? Who commissioned this door?"*

**GM ANSWER THAT SHINES**
*"The framing is closest to Auris — specifically the Willing Hand tradition, which predates the current Wounded/Penitent schism by about a century. But there is something in the final line — 'what word names the thing that makes the difference' — that feels less like a catechism and more like a philosophical challenge. Like someone who had absorbed the theological tradition and then asked a harder question. The Archive was built before the schism. Whoever commissioned this door was asking this question when no one had yet decided which answer was correct."* The Believer now understands the door as older than the campaign's current divisions — which means CONSENT is not a faction position. It is a foundation.

---

### The Survivor
*Fighter / Barbarian / Rogue — Varenhold local or has lost someone; possibly had prior Restorer contact*

**FIRST NOTICE**
The door's age. The Survivor has been in the city long enough to read wear patterns — the stone around the inscription is smoother where hands have rested, rougher where they haven't. People have stood here before. Many of them. They have stood here and read these words and some of them have answered correctly and some have not and the door is still here, unchanged. Whatever is on the other side of this door is worth protecting from people who cannot answer this question. The Survivor finds that sobering rather than interesting. They know what it means to be protected from information "for your own good."

**INSTINCTIVE APPROACH**
The Survivor's instinct is to think about who this riddle was written for — not what the correct philosophical answer is, but what kind of person gets to walk through this door. "Given only when no one compels it." In the Lowmark, everything is compelled by circumstance. You take the food allocation because you are hungry. You sign the registry because the alternative is worse. The Survivor knows the difference between freely given and given-under-duress from lived experience, not theory. They may get to the answer before anyone else — not because they are clever but because they have lived it.

**LIKELY QUESTION TO GM**
*"I've been in this city a long time. Has anyone I know or have heard of tried to get through this door and failed? Is there a reputation around the Archive's restricted section?"*

**GM ANSWER THAT SHINES**
*"There are stories. The Archive has a reputation in the Lowmark for being... selective. People say the restricted section is where the truth about the ritual lives, and the reason most people don't go looking is not that it's forbidden — it's that the door asks something of you that most people discover they can't answer. The stories aren't about the wrong word. They're about people who stood at that door, read the inscription, and walked away. Not because they were stopped. Because they understood what they were being asked to honestly examine, and they didn't want to."* The Survivor hears this differently from anyone else at the table. They have stood in that position — at the edge of a truth they weren't sure they could handle — more than once. The door is not asking them to be clever. It is asking them to be honest.

---

### The Scholar
*Wizard / Artificer / Bard — Spire fellowship, may know Theron Waide by correspondence*

**FIRST NOTICE**
The notation system. Before the content of the riddle registers, the Scholar clocks that the lettering style and the surrounding notation are not standard civic inscription. The Archive's public floors use administrative-format stone carving — clean capitals, standard spacing, functional. This inscription uses a different register: closer to academic philosophical notation, the kind used in Spire fellowship documents for formal propositions. Someone with scholarly training authored this, and it was carved with care. This door was not designed by the civil administration. It was designed by someone who expected it to be read by people who could read it correctly.

**INSTINCTIVE APPROACH**
Decompose. The Scholar will parse the riddle as a formal proposition: each line is a definitional constraint on the unknown term. "Given only when no one compels it" — the term's existence requires absence of compulsion. "Forced, I become something else entirely" — under compulsion the term transforms into a different concept (what concept? coercion, theft, violation). "The difference between a gift and a theft" — the term is the distinguishing criterion between two acts that might be physically identical but morally distinct. The Scholar may arrive at CONSENT by formal analysis faster than any other archetype — but they will want to verify their reasoning, and they will find the verification meaningful rather than redundant.

**LIKELY QUESTION TO GM**
*"Theron Waide has been corresponding with me. Did any of his letters reference this door, this notation, or the concept the riddle is asking about? Is there anything in what he sent me that was pointing here?"*

**GM ANSWER THAT SHINES**
This is the Scholar's specific payoff moment. Give it to them: *"Think back through what Waide sent you. The documents he chose to share — the ones that brought you here. There is one letter, not the others, in which he wrote: 'The archive holds more than records. It holds the question that must be answered before the records can be used rightly.' You thought it was flourish. Standing here, you understand it was a map. He knew you would reach this door. He wrote you a letter that told you what the door was asking — without ever naming the answer — because he needed to know if you were the kind of person who would find it themselves. The choice of what to send you, and what to withhold, was itself a demonstration of the answer."* The Scholar now understands that Theron's correspondence was a test of this exact question — and that they have been, without knowing it, already proving their answer for months.

### The Mercenary
*Fighter / Rogue / Ranger — hired job, advance paid, possibly without full context*

**FIRST NOTICE**
The practical problem. There is a door. It is shut. The objective is on the other side. The Mercenary's read of the situation is efficient: what is the fastest legitimate path through, and what are the fallback options if that fails? The riddle is a mechanism to be solved, the notation is instructions. The Mercenary will also notice, with the professional eye they applied to the ambush in Scene 1, that the door has no guard because the riddle is the guard. Whoever designed this was confident the philosophical challenge would do the work of a portcullis. The Mercenary finds this either impressive or annoying, depending on their disposition — but in either case, they recognize it as a choice that says something about the people who built it.

**INSTINCTIVE APPROACH**
Parallel-process. While someone else is working out the philosophical angle, the Mercenary is checking the physical alternatives: the door's hinges (on which side?), the frame's construction (how old?), whether there are other entry points to the restricted section. They are not undermining the puzzle — they are ensuring the party has options if it fails. This is good practice, not cynicism. In a real operation, you do not rely on a single approach. The Mercenary is the person who has already identified the backup while the scholars are still parsing line two.

**LIKELY QUESTION TO GM**
*"What's the actual cost of a wrong answer? Not philosophically — what happens to us, practically, if we get it wrong twice and Theron shows up? Is he going to throw us out, or is he the kind of person we can work with?"*

**GM ANSWER THAT SHINES**
Give them a practical read that also reveals character: *"From what you can tell — and you've been reading people in professional contexts for long enough to have a working model — Theron Waide is not a threat. He is a complication. If he arrives on the second wrong answer, he does not come to eject you; he comes to manage you. He has been managing people who want access to this section for thirty years, and he has developed a practiced warmth that keeps everyone moving in the direction he wants without anyone feeling pushed. He is, in professional terms, good at his job. The question is whether his job and your job are the same job — and right now, you don't know."* The Mercenary now has a tactical read on Theron before meeting him, which is how they prefer to operate.

---

### The Dawnborn-Adjacent
*Any class — knows a Dawnborn personally, likely Sera Voss; came because Sera asked or because of personal relationship*

**FIRST NOTICE**
The emotional resonance of the riddle's subject matter. The Dawnborn-Adjacent has a specific, personal relationship with the idea of something freely given. Sera — or whoever their Dawnborn connection is — is a person who has been giving, continuously, for fifty years, in a way that the rest of Varenhold benefits from without asking whether it is freely chosen. The riddle's opening line — "given only when no one compels it" — lands with a weight the Dawnborn-Adjacent was not prepared for. They are not thinking about the answer yet. They are thinking about the person they came here for.

**INSTINCTIVE APPROACH**
Hold it. The Dawnborn-Adjacent is the archetype most likely to fall quiet at this door, to take a beat before engaging. Not because they don't understand the riddle — they understand it better than most, because they are living adjacent to its stakes — but because the question being asked here is one they have been asking about the Dawnborn, privately, since the investigation began. Whether Sera chose this. Whether any of the ten Dawnborn chose this. Whether "given" is the right word for what happened to them. The riddle is asking that question formally, in carved stone. The Dawnborn-Adjacent has been asking it informally, in their chest, for much longer.

**LIKELY QUESTION TO GM**
*"I need to ask this carefully. The campaign is about the Dawnborn and whether the ritual can be undone. This riddle is asking about consent. Is this door asking whether we have the right to investigate — or is it asking something about the Dawnborn themselves, about whether they consented to what was done to them?"*

**GM ANSWER THAT SHINES**
This deserves a pause before the answer. Let it breathe: *"The door is old enough that it predates the current investigation by decades. It was here before you were hired, before Edoran's organization, before the Chancellor's commission. Whoever built it was asking this question when the ritual was still recent history — when the people who commissioned it were still alive. You don't know yet what they concluded. But the fact that the door to the records about the ritual is guarded by a question about consent suggests that someone who built this Archive believed the two things were connected. Whether they were right — that's what the restricted section might tell you."* The Dawnborn-Adjacent now carries this question forward not as an abstract philosophical point but as a personal one: if this door has always asked about consent, what does it mean that the city has been walking past it for fifty years without stopping to answer?

## 2. Ability Interactions

These are the spells and class features players will reach for when the door doesn't open and the notation is unclear. Adjudicate each one directly and narratively — the riddle cannot be bypassed magically, but magic enriches the encounter rather than short-circuiting it.

---

### Comprehend Languages on the Notation

**What it does:** The notation beside the door is written in Common with academic philosophical notation marks — Comprehend Languages is technically not required, but a player who casts it to be thorough is engaging consciously, not redundantly.

**What they learn that others don't:** The notation marks around the inscription are not decorative. They are arguments. The superscript marks beside "compels" and "forced" are scholarly qualifiers indicating that the author considered alternative phrasings and rejected them — the word "compels" is marked as preferred over "forces," "coerces," and "requires," all of which are annotated in a smaller hand alongside. Similarly, "becomes something else entirely" is marked as preferred over "ceases to exist" and "transforms into its opposite."

The author of this inscription chose these words very carefully. They were writing a proposition, not a riddle in the folk-puzzle sense. The answer CONSENT is not the punchline to a trick — it is the conclusion to an argument.

**Deliver to the player who casts it:** *"The notation around the inscription is analytical shorthand — the kind you've seen in advanced philosophical treatises from the Spire archive's oldest section. The author considered and rejected at least four alternative phrasings for each key term. Every word that remains is the word they were most certain of. Whoever built this door was trying to be precise, not clever."*

---

### Legend Lore or Similar Knowledge Spells on the Door

**What it does:** Legend Lore requires the target to be a legendary item, place, or person. The Archive's restricted-section door does not qualify as legendary in the technical sense — it is significant within Varenhold's history but not a figure of legend across the wider world.

**Ruling:** The spell fails to trigger its full effect. However, the attempt itself — the reaching out for legendary resonance — produces a faint response: the door has been touched by people of consequence, and the spell registers that weight as a diffuse impression rather than coherent information.

**What the casting character perceives:** *"The door is not legendary. But it has been stood before by people who were. The spell does not give you names or events — it gives you the sensation of depth, the way a shoreline gives you the sense of ocean behind it. Whatever is on the other side of this door has been important enough for very serious people to stop here, take a breath, and answer a question before going further."*

**Secondary note:** If a player later asks Theron Waide whether anyone famous has passed through this door, the answer is yes — Archmagister Corven himself passed through it to deposit the ritual documents. The spell did not lie. It just could not articulate what it found.

---

### Bardic Inspiration on the Riddle-Solving Attempt

**The question:** Can you Inspire someone who is answering a riddle?

**Ruling: Yes — but it changes the nature of the roll.**

The riddle has a fixed answer. If the party already has CONSENT, no roll is needed — the word simply opens the door. Bardic Inspiration applies if the party is uncertain and the GM calls for a DC check to see if a character reaches the correct answer through reasoning rather than knowledge. In that case, Bardic Inspiration adds the die to the Intelligence (Arcana, History, or Investigation) check used to puzzle through it.

**The narrative:** What does inspiration look like for a riddle? The Bard is not adding rhetorical flourish — they are providing a frame. An observation, a question, a way of looking at the problem sideways that opens space. The Bard might say: *"Think about what its opposite looks like. What word names the thing when it isn't present?"* That is inspiration in the literal sense — a breath that moves something.

**If Inspiration pushes a borderline roll over the DC:** *"The word arrives the way right answers sometimes do — not from the front but from the side, when you stopped pressing and let the question breathe. You say it aloud before you've decided to."*

---

### Divination Wizard Portent — Can They Use It on a Riddle?

**Ruling: Yes — with a specific adjudication.**

Portent replaces a d20 roll made by any creature the Diviner can see. If the GM calls for a roll to determine whether a character solves the riddle through reasoning (Intelligence check), Portent can replace that roll.

**The specific case:** The more interesting use of Portent here is pre-emptive. If the Divination Wizard has a high Portent die and declares it before any roll is called — *"I am using my high Portent die on whatever check determines whether we answer correctly"* — the GM should honor this as a mechanical statement of confidence that has narrative weight.

**What this looks like at the table:** The Diviner is essentially saying: *I have seen this answer. I know it. Let me put my foresight on it.* If they have a Portent die of 15 or higher, tell them: *"The word you are reaching for is already present in whatever dream-fragment gave you that number. You do not need to puzzle it out — you need to trust what you already know."* This makes Portent feel oracular rather than mechanical.

**Edge case — low Portent die:** If the Diviner uses a low Portent die (suggesting they have seen a failure), let them use it as a warning: *"Something in my vision said this answer would not open the door. I'm going to save that die and try a different approach."* This is legitimate play and rewards the class fantasy.

---

### Paladin's Divine Sense Near the Door

**What Divine Sense detects:** Celestial, fiend, or undead presences within 60 ft., and the presence of consecrated or desecrated ground.

**At the Archive door:** The door itself is not a magical object in the celestial/fiend/undead sense. However, the stone was quarried from the same source as the Auris temple district foundations — a limestone vein from the Reaches that the early Varenhold founders considered sacred. This is a historical fact, not a mechanical one.

**What the Paladin perceives:** Divine Sense does not fire dramatically. But the Paladin notices an absence of wrongness — nothing here is desecrated, nothing is corrupted. Whatever is on the other side of this door, it has not been touched by the kind of evil that leaves a Divine Sense signature. That is its own form of information.

**Deliver quietly:** *"Nothing triggers. The stone is old and clean. Whatever the Archive holds — whatever is on the other side of this door — it has not been corrupted by the kind of force your sense is tuned to. That is either reassuring or worrying, depending on how you define the things that could go wrong here."*

**If the Paladin has the Oath of Devotion (Sacred Weapon, etc.):** The door does not react to the Paladin's sacred nature. It is not a divine threshold. It is a philosophical one. The Paladin's oath does not grant passage — their understanding of the riddle does. That is a meaningful distinction for a Paladin to sit with.

---

### Warlock's Eldritch Sight or Detect Magic Through Invocation

**What Detect Magic reveals:** The door has a faint aura of Divination magic — old, stable, and precise. This is not a trap or ward in the combat sense. It is a sensing mechanism: the door knows when an answer has been given, and it knows whether the answer is correct. The magic is passive, not reactive — it does not push back, it simply pays attention.

**Secondary aura:** The notation beside the door carries a separate, fainter aura of Transmutation — the letters will rearrange themselves on successive wrong answers (players may not have seen this yet). The Warlock who detects this is the first to understand that the riddle is not static.

**Eldritch Sight (Devil's Sight):** In the dim light of the Archive antechamber, Devil's Sight reveals no hidden text, no invisible marks, no demon-script beneath the inscription. The door is exactly what it appears to be — philosophically, at least. What Eldritch Sight does give the Warlock is a clear read of the antechamber's shadowed corners, and in one of those corners: a worn patch of floor where many feet have stood, centered precisely in front of the door. The riddle has been answered many times. Most of those people got in.

**Deliver:** *"Divination and Transmutation — old, quiet, working. The door is paying attention. And something in the notation will shift if you're wrong. Also: that floor. People have been standing exactly where you are standing for a very long time."*

---

### Ranger's Primeval Awareness (Irrelevant — But Players Ask)

**The question players ask:** *"Can I use Primeval Awareness to sense whether there's anything dangerous on the other side of the door?"*

**The honest ruling:** Primeval Awareness detects the presence of aberrations, celestials, dragons, elementals, fey, fiends, and undead within range. The restricted Archive stacks contain none of these. The ability is not applicable here.

**What Theron says if present and the Ranger asks:** Theron observes the Ranger's attempt with the careful attention of someone watching a process they have seen before. He says, mildly: *"The Archives have had several visitors over the years who tried various approaches to the door before asking me. A ranger once spent twenty minutes with their ear pressed against the stone. I did not interrupt him. Eventually he stood up and said, 'There is nothing in there that is not human.' I told him: 'That is correct. That is precisely the problem.'"* Theron then waits. He is not unkind. He is very patient. He has been waiting for someone to simply answer the riddle for thirty years.

**Alternative delivery (if Theron is not yet present):** The Primeval Awareness yields nothing. The Ranger can truthfully report: "Nothing in there is supernatural." That is its own form of useful information — the restricted section does not contain magic or monsters. It contains documents. The question is what those documents say.

---

### Cleric's Guidance Cantrip on Knowledge Checks About the Riddle

**What Guidance does:** Adds 1d4 to an ability check. It can be applied to any ability check a creature is about to make.

**Can Guidance apply to "solving a riddle"?**

Yes — if the GM calls for a roll. The canonical use here is an Intelligence (Religion, History, or Arcana) check to identify the riddle's conceptual territory and narrow toward the answer. Guidance on that check is entirely appropriate.

**The theological angle:** If the Cleric is applying Guidance to another character's roll, the act of invoking divine assistance on a question about consent is worth noting at the table. The Cleric is asking their deity to help someone understand what freely given means. If their deity has any position on consent, sacrifice, or willing acts — which most D&D deities do — this is a moment of genuine thematic alignment between character ability and scene content.

**Deliver:** If Guidance pushes the roll over the DC, acknowledge the assist: *"The cantrip works exactly as it always does — a small clarity, a steadying of what you were almost sure of. In this case, what becomes clear is that the riddle is not asking what CONSENT is. It already knows. It is asking whether you know. The Cleric's blessing did not give you the answer. It confirmed that you were reaching for the right thing."*

## 3. Player Choice Map

This scene has no combat, but it has three genuine choices with real costs. None of them is obviously correct.

---

### Choice 1: Attempt the Riddle vs. Wait for Theron to Arrive and Guide Them

**Context:** Theron appears on the first wrong answer. On the third wrong answer (or at GM discretion after notable struggling), he becomes a guide. Players who have failed twice and are on their third attempt know that Theron is coming — the door's notification system has already been triggered.

**Option A — Attempt the riddle independently:**

- *Gives:* The satisfaction of having found CONSENT without help, which means something in this campaign. The door opens on their terms. When Theron arrives (because the first wrong answer will almost certainly trigger him), he meets them on the other side — or entering as they are succeeding — and the dynamic of the relationship begins differently. They are colleagues, not students. Additionally, every clue found independently in this campaign accumulates toward a specific understanding of the ritual's ethics; players who puzzle out CONSENT on their own are more likely to apply it correctly when it matters in Sessions 4 and 5.
- *Costs:* If they are wrong twice before getting it right, Theron has been watching them fail, which sets a different tone. More practically: wrong answers cost time and may trigger social complexity with an NPC they want access to.

**Option B — Wait for Theron:**

- *Gives:* Theron's guidance. He will not simply give them the answer — he will ask questions that lead them to it, in the way of a teacher who believes the student is capable of arriving themselves. His questions reveal his character before they reveal the answer. Players who wait for Theron learn more about Theron than players who solve it independently.
- *Costs:* They have not answered the question themselves. When CONSENT appears as a theme in later scenes — and it will, repeatedly, explicitly — there is a version of that encounter where the players remember that they found the word, and a version where they remember that someone walked them to it. The campaign does not adjudicate this distinction mechanically. But the players will feel it.

**The real stakes:** This choice is about whether they want to be people who found CONSENT, or people who were shown it. The campaign will ask that question again, at much higher stakes, in Session 5.

---

### Choice 2: Ask Theron Directly for the Answer vs. Solve Independently

**Context:** Theron is present (triggered on the first wrong answer). He is standing nearby, available, watching with the patient attention of someone who has seen this before. Players can ask him for the answer outright.

**Option A — Ask Theron directly:**

- *Gives:* Theron's response, which is not the answer but is more valuable: *"I never give the answer. I have been standing at this door for thirty years and I have never given the answer. Not because I do not know it — I have known it since I was twenty-three — but because the door is not testing whether you know the word. It is testing whether you have thought about what the word means. If I tell you, the door opens, but the test has been administered to me, not to you. That is not what the person who built this door wanted."* Players who ask Theron directly receive this speech, and it tells them more about Theron than any Insight check would.
- *Costs:* They still have to solve it. The ask has not moved them closer to the answer mechanically. But they now know something about Theron: he has been here, at this door, thinking about CONSENT, for thirty years. That is significant.

**Option B — Solve independently (with Theron present but unhelpful):**

- *Gives:* The experience of being watched by someone who knows the answer while they work toward it. This is its own pressure. Players who are aware of Theron watching may ask him questions that he answers in ways that do not give the answer but illuminate the territory: *"Is the answer a noun or a concept?"* / *"It is both."* / *"Is it something everyone already knows?"* / *"Everyone knows the word. Not everyone knows what it requires."*
- *Costs:* Nothing mechanical. But knowing Theron is watching while they think changes how people think, for most people. The self-consciousness of being observed by an expert who is choosing not to help is a specific kind of discomfort.

**The real stakes:** Whether they ask Theron or don't, they cannot avoid the question he represents: he has spent thirty years knowing the answer and not acting on it. When the players finally solve the riddle and walk through the door, they are walking into the consequences of his three decades of knowing. That transition is different for players who asked him directly vs. players who found it themselves.

---

### Choice 3: Satisfy Curiosity About Theron vs. Push Straight to the Restricted Stacks

**Context:** The riddle is solved. The door is open. The restricted stacks are beyond it — Corven's documentation, the ritual's true cost, everything they came here for. Theron is in the antechamber, clearly willing to talk, clearly holding something back. Players who push straight through get the documents sooner. Players who pause get Theron.

**Option A — Push straight to the restricted stacks:**

- *Gives:* Immediate access to the documents. If the session has run long or the players are eager to resolve the investigation's first concrete lead, this is the right call. The information in the stacks is the objective of the scene.
- *Costs:* The relationship with Theron begins on a transactional footing. He is a resource, not a person. He will still help — it is his job to help — but the warmth in his manner adjusts to match the players' investment. If they treat him as a door mechanism and then a stacks guide, he treats them as researchers and the scene loses its human weight.

**Option B — Pause and engage Theron:**

- *Gives:* Theron. Not his secret — he will not reveal what he knows in this scene without trust that hasn't been earned yet — but the beginning of a relationship that pays off dramatically in Session 2. Players who take time to see him as a person before using him as a resource will find Session 2's confession scene lands with far more weight. Additionally: Theron knows things about Corven's notation system that will save the players time in the stacks. Engaging him now reduces the investigation difficulty later.
- *Costs:* Time, in the session's pacing sense. The players know the stacks are through that door. Choosing to sit with Theron is a choice to delay the payoff in favor of the person.

**The real stakes:** The restricted stacks are what the players came for. Theron is what the campaign is about. The scene is asking whether they can tell the difference.

## 4. Q&A Pairs

Ten player questions the GM should have ready. Deliver each as a GM response, not a skill check result — these are things the characters encounter or can observe without rolling.

---

**Q1: "Is this the right place?"**

*GM answer:* "Yes. The antechamber is clearly marked above the inner gate: RESTRICTED ACCESS — CIVIC REPOSITORY, SECOND FLOOR. Below that, in smaller text, a notation in the formal scholarly style: 'Entry by answered question only. Master Archivist to be notified on first failure.' The door is set into the wall at the far end — old stone, heavier than the surrounding construction, with the inscription carved directly into the lintel arch rather than on a plaque. Someone built this door to be permanent. The inscription is meant to last."

---

**Q2: "What does the notation say?"**

*GM answer:* "The inscription is carved in an arc above the door in clean capital letters. You read it aloud or to yourselves:

*'I am given only when no one compels it.*
*Forced, I become something else entirely.*
*I am the difference between a gift and a theft.*
*What word names the thing that makes the difference?'*

Below the final line, there is a single carved oval — a shallow depression in the stone, roughly mouth-sized, at speaking height. The oval is smooth with use. That is where the answer goes. You say it aloud, into that space."

**Q3: "Has anyone else tried to open this? What do wrong answers look like?"**

*GM answer:* "The floor in front of the door has a worn patch exactly where someone would stand to read the inscription — centered, two feet back. Many feet, over a long time, in exactly that spot. The stone around the carved oval shows handling: the edges are worn smooth. As for wrong answers — there is no visible consequence mechanism, no scorching, no alarm bell you can see. The door either opens or it does not. On the first wrong attempt, the notation seems to settle slightly, in a way you cannot articulate — like something that was listening has made a note. On the second wrong attempt, you hear footsteps above you, measured and unhurried, beginning to descend."

**Q4: "Does Theron know the answer?"**

*GM answer (if Theron is present):* He does not confirm this directly, but he does not deny it either. When asked, he says: "The door has been here for the entire thirty years of my tenure, and for at least forty years before that." A pause. "I have always found it easier to help researchers understand what they are looking for than to stand at the door and watch them guess."

*GM answer (if Theron is not yet present):* "You don't know yet. But the notation mentions that the Master Archivist is notified on first failure — whoever installed this system, they expected the Archivist to be involved. Whether as enforcer, guide, or something else, you don't yet know."

**Q5: "What's the answer?" (asked directly to the GM)**

*GM answer:* "The riddle is in-world. Your characters are standing in front of the door, not outside it. If you want to tell me what your character says into the oval — the word they believe is correct — that is how this works. I can tell you that the answer is a single word, and that the riddle's lines are each a different way of approaching the same concept. If your character knows the answer, your character speaks it. The door responds to the word, not to me."

*If the player is genuinely stuck and asks for an out-of-character hint:* "What makes the difference between someone giving you something and you taking it? What word names the quality of the act when the person who gives it is not under compulsion? It is a word you know. You have known it since you were old enough to understand that there are some things you cannot simply take."

**Q6: "What happens if we try to force the door physically — break through, pick the lock, find another way in?"**

*GM answer:* "There is no lock to pick — the mechanism is not mechanical, it is magical, and it is built into the stone itself. Forcing the door would require the kind of structural force that would bring the Archive staff and very possibly the City Watch. The door is also, you realize as you examine it, significantly heavier than the frame suggests — it was built to be impassable by anything short of siege equipment, and this is a library in a civic building in a city full of people. More practically: you are here on the Chancellor's commission, and the Master Archivist is a man you need to work with. Forcing this door is the fastest way to make the next several days much harder."

**Q7: "When Theron shows up after the first wrong answer — what does he look like? What's his energy? Is he angry?"**

*GM answer:* "He is not angry. He descends the stair at the same pace he would descend any stair — he is not hurrying, which means he was expecting this. He is sixty-something, wearing a scholar's plain dark coat with ink on the cuff, carrying a small lantern that he clearly forgot to leave upstairs. He looks at you, then at the door, then back at you, and his expression is neither disappointed nor surprised. It is the expression of a man who has made this trip before and has decided the trip is worth making. He says, mildly: 'I see you've found the second floor.' A pause. 'Most people find it difficult.' He sets the lantern on a bracket, folds his hands, and waits. He is not going to tell you the answer. But he is going to help you find it, if you let him."

**Q8: "Can we research the riddle — pull books from the public floor, check Auris scripture, look it up?"**

*GM answer:* "The public floor is accessible — the Archive's general collection is open to any Varenholder. The riddle's subject matter — consent, willing action, the difference between gift and theft — appears in several traditions represented there: Auris devotional texts (particularly the Open Hand canticles), civic legal documents from Varenhold's founding charter period, and a section of philosophical ethics from the Spire fellowship's public-facing publications. None of them will give you the word. All of them are exploring the same concept from different angles. An hour of research (and a DC 14 History or Religion check) will confirm that the concept has been central to Auris theology, Varenhold civic ethics, and Spire natural philosophy for at least two centuries — and that no one, across any of those traditions, has found a word that does the work better than the obvious one. The research is not a shortcut. It is confirmation."

**Q9: "After we solve it — what does the door opening feel like? Is it mechanical, magical, something else?"**

*GM answer:* "The word leaves your mouth and the stone receives it. There is a half-second of nothing — the particular silence of something about to change — and then, without grinding or spectacle, the door opens. Not swinging dramatically: simply opening, moving inward on balanced pivots, as though it had been waiting to be asked politely. The air that comes through is cool and dry with the specific smell of very old documents — parchment, dust, a trace of the wax used to seal document boxes. The restricted stacks are dark beyond the threshold; the amber lanterns inside have been waiting for occupants. As you step through, they warm. The door remains open behind you. It does not lock again until you leave."

**Q10: "The Scholar specifically — Theron corresponded with me. What does he make of meeting me in person after our correspondence?"**

*GM answer:* "He looks at you for a moment longer than he looks at the others. Not with surprise — with assessment. He says, finally: 'You are shorter than I imagined.' A pause. 'Most correspondents are.' His manner shifts very slightly: still careful, still deliberate, but with the particular quality of someone who has been building a model of you for months and is now correcting it against reality. He knows your handwriting. He has read your questions. He chose what to send you based on what those questions suggested you were ready for. Meeting you in person is a recalibration, and you can watch it happening behind his eyes. He does not offer his hand. He says: 'You found the door. That is further than most people who write to me get. Come. There are things I should have told you in letters and could not.'"
