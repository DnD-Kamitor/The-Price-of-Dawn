# Session 1, Scene 2 — The Riddle: The Archive Door: GM Build File

<!-- SCENE HEADER BLOCK — place above <details> tabs -->

---

**PURPOSE STATEMENT:** This scene exists to make players speak the campaign's central word aloud — in a room built to receive it, to a door that has been waiting eleven years to open. It is a compressed moral argument presented as a puzzle: knowledge that was sealed is about to become accessible, and the mechanism that seals it is consent itself. The riddle does not teach the players about consent. It demands they produce the word from their own minds, in their own voices, before they know what it will cost them.

**EMOTIONAL TARGET:** Players should feel the weight of a long silence being broken — not excitement, but something quieter and more complicated. The Archive has been sitting with this door closed for eleven years. The players have been in the city for less than an hour, most of it fighting. When the door opens in response to their word, the feeling should be: *we unlocked something that cannot be re-locked.* There is no triumph in it. There is only the specific gravity of a thing that has been waiting too long.

**DRAMATIC QUESTION:** Are you willing to open a door that has been sealed for eleven years — knowing that the person who sealed it is standing behind you on the landing, watching you try?

**SURPRISE:** Theron is not a gatekeeper. He is a man who has been alone with this secret since Year 39, who has been slowly, methodically trying to work up the courage to give someone the key, and who just found out that the door is answering on its own. His appearance on the first wrong attempt is not authority asserting itself. It is relief trying very hard to look like composure. Players who read him correctly find not an obstacle but a man who has been waiting for them to arrive — and who is terrified now that they have.

**DESIGNED PEAK MOMENT:** The moment a player says CONSENT aloud and the door moves.

Everything in this scene builds toward that single beat. The riddle is written to be solvable without a roll, without Theron's help, by any player who reads it carefully. When someone at the table says the word — not in game text, not paraphrased, but the actual word spoken by an actual person — the door responds. The notation carvings shift. The restricted stacks become visible.

That is the campaign's central word, spoken for the first time, in a room dedicated to locked knowledge, by a person who does not yet know what the word is going to cost the people it applies to.

The GM should let that beat land before saying anything. One full breath of silence. Then the narration: the carvings moving, the door swinging inward, the amber half-dark of the restricted stacks beyond. Do not rush past it. The players will feel whether the GM understands what just happened.

**PREP vs IMPROVISE:**

Must prep:
- The riddle carved on the door — write it on a physical index card and place it on the table when the scene opens. Players should read it as an object, not hear it as narration.
- Theron's physical state on the landing: coat askew, ink on sleeve, breathing slightly fast — he ran up the stairs, but he is performing calm. This detail must be delivered precisely on his first appearance.
- The exact line Theron says after the third wrong attempt: *"Perhaps I should show you what I know before you try again."* This is not an offer of charity. It is a man who has been waiting eleven years finding, finally, an excuse to speak.
- The door's response to CONSENT: carvings shift first (a slow movement, not a snap), then the door swings inward, then the smell of the restricted stacks reaches them before they can see inside clearly. Prep the order of these three beats.

Safe to improvise:
- The junior clerk's exact manner and name (they are texture and threshold — apologetic, slightly awed by the sealed door, gone once they've delivered the players to the landing)
- The physical description of the restricted stacks visible beyond the door (rows of bound folios, amber half-dark, the brass star-wheel of the Scene 3 puzzle visible at the far end — this is connective tissue, not architecture)
- How many wrong attempts before the players start looking at each other differently — whether that silence is two beats or five is a read-the-table call
- Theron's precise dialogue during the RP that follows the door opening — his confession arc is Session 2; what he gives in this scene is willingness, not revelation

## 1. Guy Sanders Techniques — Applied to This Scene

---

### SECONDARY OBJECTIVE: Making the Intercepted Restorer Note Feel Urgent Without Explaining It

The secondary objective in this scene is not the riddle. The riddle is the primary puzzle. The secondary objective is Theron's intercepted note.

When Theron appears on the landing after the first wrong attempt, he is holding something. Not conspicuously — it is in his coat pocket, or tucked under his arm with a sheaf of reference papers. He does not mention it. Players who look for it (Perception DC 11, passive — he is not trying to hide it, he simply has not volunteered it) see a folded paper with a broken wax seal. The seal is Restorer iconography: the inward-ray Auris sun, same as the badge inside the zealots' collars in Scene 1.

**Do not name it. Do not say "clue." Describe only what a character in the scene would see.**

The delivery technique is the same as Scene 1's belt-pouch plant: show Theron noticing that a player has noticed, let his hand move across his pocket once in a small, deliberate gesture, then let the conversation continue without addressing it.

What this tells players: the Restorers contacted the Archivist. Before the players arrived. Today, or recently enough that he has not put the letter away.

What it means, if players press: Theron deflects. He is not ready to explain this. He will explain it in Session 2. In this scene, the note functions as a question mark — it tells players that Theron is not simply a neutral repository of knowledge. He is a man receiving correspondence from the people who just tried to stop the players reaching this building.

**Why it feels urgent without explanation:** The note makes Theron's involvement in the situation structurally ambiguous at the exact moment players most want to trust him. They have been attacked. They have solved the door. They are about to enter the restricted stacks with this man as their guide. And he is carrying a note from the people who attacked them. Players cannot confront him about it yet — they do not know enough. But they know something is wrong, and they cannot name what, and that dissonance will drive them into Session 2 wanting answers they can now specifically formulate.

If players ask about the note directly: Theron looks at it. He looks at them. He says: *"Later. There is something in here I should have shown someone eleven years ago. That matters more."* He opens the restricted stacks. He is not lying. He is choosing the order of his confessions.

### FIVE SENSES — Timing Table

| Sense | Content | When to Deliver |
|---|---|---|
| **Sight** | Stone stairwell narrowing toward the third floor landing; the door at its end iron-banded, older than the rest of the Archive, notation carvings visible across its full face like a text someone wrote without spaces | **Approach narration** — as players climb from the second floor, before they are standing in front of the door. Give them the visual from below, ascending. The door should feel like something they are walking toward. |
| **Sound** | Silence on the third floor landing — not the reading-room hush of the floors below but a different quality of silence, the silence of a room that has not had foot traffic in a long time. The junior clerk's voice sounds slightly too loud up here. | **On the landing** — deliver this the moment players step off the stairwell and onto the third floor. The sound contrast with the occupied floors below is the sensory signal that they have entered a different category of space. |
| **Smell** | Old parchment and the specific cold-stone smell of a room that gets no direct light; underneath it, faintly, something else — a mineral trace, like the air before lightning, that does not belong to any archive material. This is the door. The notation carvings have a faint charge that eleven years of closure has allowed to concentrate. | **When players first touch or closely examine the door** — or when Theron appears on the landing, whichever comes first. His arrival brings a draft from the stairwell that stirs the air and makes the mineral smell suddenly clearer. |
| **Touch** | The carvings under fingertips if a player touches the door: smooth stone with edges that feel slightly warm despite the cold room, warmer than the surrounding stone by a few degrees. The notation has been active this whole time, waiting. | **If a player specifically touches the door** — deliver this unprompted if they do not ask. It is information the door is giving them. It changes the scene's register from "old locked thing" to "thing that is still operating." |
| **Taste** | The same dust-and-cold-air of the rest of the city, the dryness of fifty years without open sky — but here there is also something almost sweet, like heated metal, that arrives when the door begins to respond to the correct answer. | **The peak moment** — when CONSENT is spoken and the carvings shift. The taste is the only sense that belongs to the door's response rather than the room's existing state. It arrives with the answer. It should feel like the Archive exhaling. |

**Rule for this scene:** Sight and sound belong to arrival. Smell belongs to Theron's entrance (he carries the draft that reveals it). Touch belongs to investigation. Taste belongs only to the answer — it is the door's one sensory gift, held back until the word is spoken.

### NPC QUICK REF — Theron Waide

**WANTS:** To have someone else open the door so that he does not have to be the one who did it. He has had the notation key for eleven years. He knows the riddle's answer. He has never spoken it to the door himself, because speaking it felt like a decision he did not have the right to make alone. He wants the players to make the decision so that he can then tell them what they have decided to find.

**FEARS:** That they will solve the riddle before he reaches the landing — that the door will open without him, and he will arrive to find it already done, and he will have to reckon with the fact that he could have done this himself at any point in the past eleven years. This fear is correct. The door has been solvable since Year 39. He did not try.

**LIES:** That he came up the stairs because he heard the warning tone and thought there might be structural damage to the mechanism. This is technically possible — the tone can indicate that. He does not believe it. He came because the tone told him someone was trying, and he needed to see who it was, and whether they would find the answer, and what he would do if they did. He has been rehearsing this moment in his head for a decade. The rehearsal did not prepare him for how it would feel.

**Voice note:** Rapid, soft speech. Lots of qualifications — *"I believe," "perhaps," "it's possible that."* When he stops qualifying, pay attention. The moments Theron speaks without hedges are the moments he is telling the exact truth. In this scene, the only unhedged thing he says is: *"This door has not opened in eleven years."* He says it to the door, not to the players.

## 2. The Encounter Beat — The Pivot

The encounter beat for Scene 2 is not the door opening. The door opening is the designed peak moment — the one beat players will quote after the session. The encounter beat — the pivot that reframes everything in the scene — is what Theron does in the moment after the door opens.

He does not enter.

The door swings inward. The restricted stacks are visible beyond — the amber half-dark, the folios, the star-wheel at the far end. Players will move toward it. Theron stands on the landing with his hand on the door frame, not following, not speaking. He is looking at the opening he has stood outside of for eleven years.

If no one addresses him, he says: *"I should tell you something before you go further."* He does not continue until someone acknowledges him. This is not dramatic withholding — he genuinely does not know how to begin what he is about to say, and he needs a beat of human contact before he can start.

What he says next is not the full confession — that is Session 2. What he says is: *"I found something in there eleven years ago that I told no one about. I told myself I needed more time to be certain. I am now very certain, and I have run out of excuses for the time."*

Then he enters. He becomes the guide.

**Why this is the pivot:** Before this moment, Theron was the gatekeeper — the inconvenient authority who appeared after the wrong answers and watched the players try. After this moment, he is a co-conspirator. He has just told the players that he has been sitting on something for eleven years, that he has excuses rather than reasons, and that the door opening forced his hand. The relationship has inverted. He is no longer a man the players must get past. He is a man who has been waiting for them to arrive so that he could stop being alone with what he knows.

**When to deliver the pivot:** Immediately after the door opens, before players have moved more than a step inside. The pivot must land before the scene's geography shifts into the restricted stacks — once players are inside and oriented on the star-wheel puzzle, the window for this beat closes. Keep Theron in the doorframe until he has spoken.

## 3. Scene Transitions

---

### IN — Closing Scene 1 (Junction Aftermath), Opening Scene 2 (Archive Approach)

Use this after the Enforcer yields and the queue re-forms:

*"The Archive is fifteen minutes east from the junction. It is a stone tower in the Spire Quarter, four floors, lanternlit at every landing — the only building in this quarter where the lights are kept up past the second floor at all hours. The Civic Repository. You can smell it before you can read the sign: old paper and cold stone and the particular mineral trace of documents that have been kept in the same rooms for a very long time.*

*A junior clerk meets you at the door. Third floor landing, restricted stacks, she says — the Chancellor's authorization carries, there is no difficulty with the public floors. She walks ahead of you up the stairs. At the second floor she lowers her voice, though there is no one nearby. 'The door at the top hasn't opened since I started here,' she says. 'Seven years.' She says it the way someone reports a fact they have been asked not to think about too much.*

*The third floor landing is at the top of the stair. The door is there, iron-banded, older than the Archive that surrounds it. The carvings across its face are tight and dense — notation, not decoration. And beneath everything, something you can almost smell: a mineral trace, like the air before lightning, that has been sitting in this room for a long time."*

**Technique:** The junior clerk's line — *"The door at the top hasn't opened since I started here"* — is delivered as texture, not as a clue, and it arrives in lowered voice without explanation. It tells players that the sealed state of this door is known to the building's staff and treated as normal. That normalization is the first signal that something has been actively left this way, not accidentally. The mineral smell arriving before they see the carvings means the door is already present as a sensory fact before it is a visual one.

---

### OUT — Closing Scene 2 (Door Open, Restricted Stacks Visible), Opening Scene 3 (Puzzle)

Use this after Theron's pivot moment has landed and players move inside:

*"The restricted stacks smell different from the rest of the Archive — denser, colder, the accumulated breath of documents that have not had a reader in a long time. The shelves run floor to ceiling on both sides of a central aisle, bound folios in amber-dark, labels you cannot read at this distance. At the far end of the aisle, a brass mechanism set into the central column: three rotating rings engraved with constellation symbols, each as large across as a cart wheel.*

*Theron follows you in. He does not look at the folios. He looks at the mechanism at the far end. Then he looks at you. 'Corven designed the organization system himself,' he says. 'He did not want anything in here to be findable by accident. He wanted someone to have to understand the sky on the night the ritual failed in order to reach his final notes.'*

*He pauses. 'The notation key fragment you will need is on shelf 4-17-3. I know where it is. I have known for eleven years. I have been waiting — I have been waiting for someone who was going to do something with it.'"*

**Technique:** Theron's final line is carefully broken — the self-correction mid-sentence (*"I have been waiting — I have been waiting"*) is the moment his prepared deflection fails and the true statement replaces it. Do not smooth this in delivery. The stutter is Theron telling the truth by accident and then deciding to let it stand. It is the first crack in his composure that points toward the full confession in Session 2.

## 4. Improv Anchors

---

**Anchor 1: Players Ask Theron for the Answer Directly**

This will happen. Someone will look at him and say some version of: *"You know what it is, don't you?"*

He does know. He has known for eleven years.

Do not let him answer. This is not a withholding-for-drama move — it is true to his character. Theron's goal in this scene is to have someone else open the door. If he speaks the answer himself, he loses the thing he has been protecting: the claim that he did not choose to open it. He will say: *"If I tell you the answer, I'm the one who decided to open this door. I've been the only one who could open it for eleven years. I chose not to. I need someone else to make that choice."*

This is the most honest thing Theron says in Session 1. It is also the scene's argument in miniature: consent means the choice belongs to the one who makes it, not to the one who knows the outcome. If players press him further — *"But you could just tell us and we could say it"* — he goes very still and says: *"Yes. And then who opened the door?"*

Let that sit. It is not a riddle. It is the campaign.

If players are completely stuck and have genuinely run out of ideas (not just frustrated — actually stuck after five or more minutes of real table time), Theron can offer a narrowing hint without giving the answer: *"The riddle describes something that cannot exist if it is compelled. Think about what changes when force enters the picture."* This points toward consent without naming it. It is his limit of help. He will not go further.

**Anchor 2: Players Try to Go Around the Door (Force, Magic, Find Another Entrance)**

Let them try. Take each approach seriously and let it fail with consequences that feel real rather than arbitrary.

*Force:* The door is iron-banded stone, Archive construction, Archive age. It is not a puzzle-box — it is a door. Forcing it would require sustained effort over hours and would be audible throughout the building. Theron, if present, says quietly: *"The door is not locked in any mechanical sense. It is sealed by notation. The notation responds to the correct answer. There is no hinge to remove, no lock to pick. There is a question, and it wants an answer."* If players insist on force despite this, the door does not respond to damage. The carvings do not crack. The stone is cold and does not care.

*Magic:* Knock, Dispel Magic, and similar spell effects interact with the seal but do not override it. A Knock spell creates the correct auditory resonance to shift the carvings — they move, the mechanism engages — but the door stops just short of opening. A thin line of light appears along the frame and fades. The seal is not a lock; it is a recognition system. It knows the difference between the sound of the word and the word. What a player learns from a failed Knock: the mechanism is responsive, it is listening for something specific, and whatever it is listening for is a word with specific phonemic content. This narrows the answer considerably for a smart player.

Dispel Magic targeted at the door has no effect — the carvings are not a spell. They are notation engineering, fifty-year-old craft. Theron: *"Corven did not trust spells. He trusted language. The seal is linguistic, not arcane."*

*Another entrance:* There is one. A maintenance access hatch on the exterior of the building, third floor, accessible from the roof via a rope and significant athleticism (DC 16 Athletics to descend safely to the correct window). The hatch opens from the outside with a standard iron lock (thieves' tools DC 14). This brings players into the restricted stacks from the north end, bypassing the door entirely. If players reach the stacks this way, they find shelf 4-17-3 without Theron's guidance, without the peak moment of the door responding, and without the pivot beat of Theron deciding to stop waiting. They get the contents of the shelf but not the scene's thematic payload. When they eventually emerge and meet Theron, he will have been standing on the landing for whatever time they spent inside. He will be very still. He will say: *"You found another way in."* A beat. *"I should have guessed someone would."* This is not recrimination. It is Theron recognizing that the door was not, in the end, the only gate — and that the choice he had been protecting himself with was less protected than he thought. This is still a meaningful beat; it is simply not the designed one. Run it.

**Anchor 3: Players Solve the Riddle Immediately on the First Attempt — No Theron Intervention Needed**

This is the best outcome. Design for it. Hope for it.

If a player reads the riddle and says CONSENT on the first try, no tone sounds, Theron does not appear, and the door opens without an audience. The carvings shift. The door swings. The restricted stacks are revealed. The players have done the thing the scene was built toward without needing any of the scaffolding.

What to do: let it be quiet. Give the five senses in order — the carvings moving, the door swinging, the smell of the restricted stacks, the taste of heated metal in the air. Then pause.

Then Theron appears at the top of the stairs — not because he heard a tone, but because the door opening produced a different sound: a deep resonant shift in the building's acoustics, a sound the Archive staff recognize because it has not happened in eleven years. He comes up the stairs fast. He arrives at the landing. He sees the open door.

His face: the GM should describe this carefully. He is not surprised the players found the answer. He is surprised that it happened today, in this hour, to him, that he is standing here watching the thing he has been guarding resolve itself in the hands of strangers while he was downstairs with ink on his sleeve.

He says: *"You got it on the first try."* Not a question. He is filing something. Then, after a beat: *"I could have opened it on the first try. For eleven years."*

This is his confession arriving ahead of schedule, in its simplest possible form. The players have not asked him anything. He has just told them the most important thing about himself because the open door has made the truth unavoidable.

Run the pivot beat exactly as designed from here — he stands in the doorframe, he tells them there is something inside he should have shown someone eleven years ago, he enters the restricted stacks as their guide. The sequence is identical to the version with wrong attempts. The only difference is that the players earned it clean, and Theron's admission lands without the structure of wrong answers to soften it. It is more exposed this way. That is the reward for solving it immediately.

## 5. Pre-Scene Resource State

**Assumed entering Scene 2:** Party at 70–85% HP, 1–2 first-level spell slots expended, no higher slots used. The ambush was hard but not punishing. Players are alert and slightly wrong-footed — the ambush told them this investigation is contested before they even reached the Archive.

The resource state affects Scene 2 less than Scene 1 — this is a puzzle and RP scene, not a combat. But it affects pacing and player psychology in ways worth tracking.

| Resource Level | What to Watch | Adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| Party at full HP, no slots used | Players may rush the riddle — they feel capable and are not yet recalibrated toward careful attention | Slow the approach narration; give the Archive building more sensory texture before the door is reached. Players who are wound up from a hard fight need decompression; players who skipped or bypassed the fight may need to have the scene opened more slowly. |
| Party at 70–85% HP (standard) | Standard pacing. Players have been rattled and are alert. The riddle will feel appropriately weighted. | No adjustment needed. |
| Party below 50% HP, multiple spell slots used | Players may be impatient or anxious; the puzzle format can feel frustrating when physical resources are depleted | If the table's energy is flat after a difficult Scene 1, consider letting a DC 10 passive Arcana or Religion check on the carvings give one of these: the word has four letters, or the riddle describes an act of will rather than an action. These are narrowing hints, not answers. They bring depleted parties back to engagement without removing the solve. |
| One or more party members unconscious (they made it) | If anyone went to 0 HP in Scene 1 and was stabilized, the scene opens with that person still recovering. | Let them participate in the riddle from a seated position on the landing. Theron, if present, will arrange this without being asked — he produces a stool from somewhere, which tells players he has been dealing with people in various states of need in this building for a long time. It is a small humanizing detail. Use it. |

**Party state note:** Players arriving at Scene 2 carrying the note from Scene 1 should feel the weight of it differently when they encounter the riddle. The note reads: *"The willing heart gives freely what the bound heart cannot."* A player who reads the riddle and then thinks about what they are holding — a document about what giving freely means, written by the man who sent people to stop them — has all the information they need. The note is the answer. The GM should not say this. But if a player reaches for it mid-scene, let them read it again. Let them make the connection themselves.

## 6. "What They Remember" Test

**The sentence players quote after the session:**

*"I said 'consent' and the door just opened."*

Or, in the version where they arrived having just fought through the Scene 1 ambush: *"We'd just been attacked for investigating. And then the door to the Archive asks us: what's the difference between a gift and a theft? And we said consent. And it opened."*

**Why it works:** The line is quotable because it contains the entire campaign's argument in three beats — attack, question, answer. Players will not know, in the moment they say it after the session, that they are describing the shape of Sessions 1 through 5. They will know that something clicked. The word was produced by them, from their own minds, in response to a riddle that the campaign designed to receive it. They were not told the answer. They found it. That finding is the thing they own, and it is the thing they will carry into Session 2 when they discover what the word costs.

**How to deliver it:** The GM's job at the peak moment is to stop. The riddle has been on the table as a physical card. Someone says CONSENT. The GM does not immediately describe the door opening — there is a half-beat of quiet while the word sits in the air, and then the narration begins, measured and unhurried:

*"The carvings shift. Not suddenly — slowly, the way something moves when it has been still for a very long time and is remembering how. Each notation character repositions itself, following a logic you cannot read but can see resolving. Then the door moves. Inward, on old hinges that barely make a sound. The air from inside reaches you first — cold, dense with old paper, and underneath it something almost sweet, like heated metal. The restricted stacks are beyond. Rows of bound folios in amber half-dark. Something brass glinting at the far end."*

Then silence. One full breath.

**If it is not landing:** The beat fails if the GM is tracking multiple things at once when the word is spoken — if initiative is still up on the table, if there is a mechanical question unresolved, if the scene opened without the physical card on the table and the riddle was read aloud instead. The peak moment requires the table to be in the same place at the same time. If players are scattered across the scene's logistics, pull focus before the delivery: *"Hold on — someone's about to try the door. This is what happens."* The interruption is the signal. Then let the player speak the word. Then deliver the narration.

**What would make it stronger:** Have the GM write CONSENT on a small card face-down on the table before the session. If a player produces the correct word, turn the card over without saying anything. Let the physical reveal confirm what they said. It tells the table: the GM knew. The answer was always there. The question was whether they would find it. The card is the door on the table.
