## 1. Scene Purpose

This scene must accomplish three distinct things, and the GM should hold all three at once throughout.

**Mechanical goal:** Players solve the star-wheel mechanism, retrieve the documents from shelf 4-17-3, and leave the Archive with the notation key fragment and Corven's public notes in hand. The puzzle has a clean answer — Vael's Crown, Shepherd's Eye, Trailing Light, west to east, as the chart says plainly on the east wall — and every element of the scene is designed to deliver that answer to attentive players without a roll. The mechanism is not a test of cleverness. It is a test of patience.

**Emotional goal:** Theron Waide has been carrying eleven years of a decision. When shelf 4-17-3 rotates open, the players are witnessing the moment he has been building toward without knowing he was building toward it. This is Session 1's most human beat. It is quieter than the street fight and subtler than the riddle door, but it is the one players will still be talking about three sessions later: *the archivist who spent eleven years in a room he could have opened.* The scene must land that moment cleanly.

**Campaign goal:** The phrase "living conduit mechanism" is in those documents. Players do not yet understand what it means. They should leave the Archive with the phrase lodged somewhere in their minds — not explained, not alarming, just present. The mystery deepens here. It does not resolve. Session 1 ends on the widest possible question, not an answer.

## 2. The Three Beats

### Beat 1: Entering the Stacks

Players have just come through a door that responded to a word they spoke. The restricted stacks should feel like the room that word opened into — not a reward room, but a space that has been waiting in a particular kind of silence.

Use the five-senses entry narration from the Environment tab before anyone touches the mechanism. Give them the skylight. Give them the amber light that sits flat and does not move. Give them the cedar smell from the far end of the room and the brass constellation plaques at eye level and the star chart on the east wall — that last one specifically, because the chart is the answer and it should be visible from the moment players enter. Don't highlight it. Don't say "you notice a chart." Put it in the narration the same way you'd put a window in a room description: it's there, it's part of the space, they can cross to it whenever they want.

The central column and its three rings should also be described as part of the room, not called out as the puzzle. Players who have been paying attention since Scene 2 — Theron mentioned Corven's organization system in his pivot beat — will make the connection themselves. Let them make it.

Give Theron a moment here. He goes slightly ahead of the party, to the center of the room, and stops near the column. He does not explain. He looks at the east wall, where the chart is. If you delivered the Scene 2 transition line correctly, he has already told them that Corven mounted the chart himself on the day he finished the system. This beat is the callback: here is the chart, exactly where he said it would be, still there after fifty years.

Do not rush from entry to puzzle engagement. Let the room land. Players who have been through a combat and a door riddle in the last hour need approximately ninety seconds of atmospheric establishment before the next puzzle problem is put in front of them. The room earns that ninety seconds.

### Beat 2: Working the Mechanism

The puzzle is designed to be solved without assistance. The chart is on the wall. The annotation reads *"Rise order, west to east. As above, so the key turns."* A player who reads that annotation and looks at the three-ring mechanism has everything they need. No roll. No check. Just reading.

Your job as GM is to run this puzzle in a way that lets attentive players feel the satisfaction of arriving at the answer themselves, while having Theron available as a resource for any table that gets stuck without making his presence feel like remedial tutoring.

**If players go straight to the chart:** Get out of their way. Let them read. The chart is legible — the annotation is in plain common script, the three key stars are in reddish-brown ink where everything else is dark blue, and the labels are large. A player who says "I read the chart" gets all the information they need as a straightforward information handoff. That player should feel very competent right now. They walked into the room, saw the tool Corven left for exactly this purpose, used it. That is the correct response to this room. Validate it.

**If players go to the mechanism first:** Let them turn a ring. The rings click and feel deliberately mechanical — each position is a decision. A player who turns Ring 1 to something random and looks at the room is getting sensory data. Nothing happens until all three are set and the pin is engaged. If they ask what the symbols mean, the answer is right there on the plaques at eye level: these are constellation names. Now the chart becomes an obvious tool rather than decoration.

**Theron while the puzzle is being worked:** He stands to one side of the column, at an angle where he can see both the mechanism and the chart. He is not hovering. He is not watching the players with the vigilant attention of a man waiting to intervene. He is watching the room he has been inside eleven years and never engaged — watching the players do what he did not. He brings the lamp closer to the chart if the light is poor. He nods once, to himself, when a player reads the annotation correctly. He flinches when a wrong configuration sounds. Keep him present as a physical fact without making him the scene's focus until he needs to be.

**If a correct configuration is entered on the first attempt:** Clean. The lower tone sounds, the shelves rotate, the scene moves to Beat 3. Theron says nothing. His expression says everything. Keep the momentum.

**If players enter a wrong configuration:** The shelves move — dramatically, methodically, the 4-17 section rotating away and blank wood coming around — and the room is different when the rotation finishes. This is not a punishment. It is the mechanism doing its job. Describe the rotation fully the first time: the low rumble in the floor, the fifteen-second duration, the rolling ladder sliding to a new position. Let players understand what the machine does before they try again.

After the wrong configuration, the thirty-minute reset clock starts. This is real time pressure — not because something bad happens at thirty minutes, necessarily, but because the Archive is occupied and the mechanism's sound carries. Use the time not as a countdown but as texture: players have this window to examine the chart more carefully, to look at what Theron does during the wait, to notice the dust patterns on the rings (DC 13 Investigation to see that only three symbols show brass wear, which effectively gives them the answer without the chart). Theron, during a reset, finds small tasks. He brings the lamp. He checks a label. He does not look at the players. He is managing the same anxiety he has been managing for eleven years by keeping his hands occupied.

**If players enter a second wrong configuration:** Footsteps from below. Do not announce this as a threat. Deliver it as information: someone is on the stairs, moving with purpose, not browsing. The footsteps are almost certainly Theron's colleague — or Theron himself if he stepped out — and this is the moment when the room's ambient tension spikes. The puzzle that felt patient now has stakes attached to it. Something changes when there are footsteps on the stairs. The players feel it. Use it. This is also the point at which Theron straightens and something in his posture shifts from watchful observer to man-who-has-made-a-decision.

### Beat 3: Discovery

The correct configuration produces a sound different from the failure tone. Lower. It comes from the floor rather than the column — felt in the soles before it is heard. There is a pause of about three seconds that is slightly longer than the failure pause. Players who have heard a wrong answer once will notice the difference in that pause. Then the shelves rotate, and this time the labeled faces come around instead of the backs, and shelf 4-17-3 is at the top of the accessible face when the ladder settles into position.

Give this its due. The cedar smell comes around with the shelf — sharp and slightly sweet, the only part of the room that smells like something was cared for. The document tubes in their slots. The brass clasp of the smaller case catching the amber light. Everything exactly where Corven placed it, undisturbed, waiting without urgency.

Then give Theron his moment. Move to Section 3 of this tab for the craft of it. But the structural point is: do not rush to the documents. The shelf opening is the scene's peak moment, and the peak moment belongs to Theron before it belongs to the players. Let him have the five seconds. Let the table be quiet. Then let the players go to the ladder.

When a player retrieves the smaller case and opens it: the cipher fragment is inside, Corven's name and date in plain script at the top, the dense structured writing of a man who thought by writing filling both sides, and at the bottom of the second page in plain common: *"When you have all three, begin at the first mark."* Deliver this without emphasis. The phrase "living conduit mechanism" is on the public notes document in the same row — let the player who reads it have the phrase land naturally, without the GM underscoring it. The phrase does not need help. "Living" is doing all the work.

## 3. Theron as Live Wire

Theron is the scene's emotional engine. Everything in the s1c3-npc tab applies here in full; these are the specific craft notes for running him in this room at this moment.

**He will not touch the rings.** This is not a rule the GM needs to enforce — it is a character truth that the player running Theron should know before the scene starts. Even when a player is about to set a ring to the wrong position. Even when the footsteps are on the stairs and time is short. Theron does not reach for the mechanism. His hands stay at his sides or find other tasks. If a player watches him during a tense moment, they will notice his hands are deliberately occupied with something else — the lamp, a label, a ring notation he is checking without touching — because if his hands are free he might reach for the rings, and he has decided he will not do that.

**His two modes:** Most of the scene, Theron runs in watchful silence. He is present, he is oriented toward the room, he is doing small tasks. Occasionally he shifts his weight. Between wrong attempts, he looks at shelf 4-17-3 from a distance — not continuously, but in the particular way of someone looking at something they have been avoiding looking at for a long time. The second mode is oblique observation: a quiet comment framed as a question or a wondering aloud, not addressed to anyone specifically. *"I wonder if that's the rise order or the current position."* Not a hint. Not a direction. Something that might be useful if the player receiving it knows what to do with it. Players who have been watching Theron will hear these moments differently than players who have been focused on the mechanism.

**The "I know" moment:** If a player pushes Theron with genuine frustration rather than analytical inquiry — *"People might be dying because of what's in that shelf, just tell us"* — he goes still. Not defensive still. Just still. He looks at the player. Then: *"I know."* Nothing else. The procedural deflection is gone. The layer he has been presenting — the helpful-archivist professional register — has come off. What is visible now is a man who has been sitting next to the door for eleven years knowing exactly what the cost of not opening it might be. The player named it. He acknowledged it. He does not give the answer. But something has changed, and players who see it will carry that change into Session 2.

**How his emotional state shifts with the solve:**

The cleaner the solve, the more relieved Theron is — and the more exposed. A party that reads the chart on entry and sets the correct configuration on the first attempt gives Theron his peak moment without him having contributed anything, which is exactly what he needed: proof that it did not require him, that the door was always openable, that he cannot hide behind complexity as an excuse for eleven years of inaction. He is more cracked open by an easy solve than a hard one. The hard solve at least gave him something to witness. The easy solve gives him nothing except the arithmetic.

A party that struggled, that took two wrong attempts and heard the footsteps and then found the answer under pressure — Theron is steadier in that version. He watched them work. He held himself to his own standard. He found his limit and stayed inside it. There is something that resolves in him through a hard solve that a clean first-attempt solve leaves unresolved. Both outcomes are valid. Know which one you're running as the scene closes.

## 4. If Players Are Struggling

The puzzle is designed to be solved. Corven mounted the chart on the wall because the answer was always meant to be findable. These interventions exist for tables where something has gone wrong — decision paralysis, misread annotation, real-world fatigue — not for tables that are working steadily through the problem.

**Read the table before intervening.** Puzzle-solving silence looks different from disengaged silence. If players are quiet and working — moving between the chart and the mechanism, asking each other questions, trying configurations with some logic behind them — they are fine. Do not intervene. Give them room.

**Option 1 — Environmental, zero GM intrusion:**

Theron glances at the chart. A small, involuntary look when a player is examining the mechanism rings without having checked the chart. He does not say anything. He looks at the chart and then back at the player. This is behavior the player can notice or not notice. A player who notices and asks "what are you looking at?" gets the answer: the chart. That is all they need.

Alternatively: a player with passive Perception 13 or higher notices, during a moment of stillness, that the three constellation symbols on the ring positions showing brass wear from previous use are the same three that appear in reddish-brown on the star chart. Corven used this mechanism. He used it to the same three positions repeatedly, every time, for years. The wear pattern is the fingerprint of the correct answer.

**Option 2 — Theron's oblique observation:**

*"I wonder if that's the rise order or the current position."*

This line is worth understanding. It sounds like musing. It is actually a surgical intervention: it tells players that there are two possible orderings (rise order vs. current position), that the distinction matters, and that the mechanism cares about one of them. A player who goes back to the chart with this question — which order? — will find the annotation: *"Rise order, west to east."* The oblique observation has pointed them at the correct reading of the chart without giving them the configuration. Theron has done something without appearing to do anything, which is characteristic.

Deliver this line as though it just occurred to him. He is looking at the chart. He says it the way people say things they are half-thinking. Then he looks at something else. He is not waiting for a response.

**Option 3 — Theron's direct direction under pressure:**

Only when: second wrong configuration has sounded, footsteps are on the stairs, and no one at the table has a clear path to the answer. This is the line that costs Theron something. He straightens. His posture changes — the lean goes out of him and he stands properly, the posture of someone who has made a decision.

*"The star chart. Start from the left edge of the Dawnmark grouping. Not the brightest star — the one that rises first. Corven always worked from first principles."*

This is not the answer. It is the direction to the answer. A player who follows this observation finds the annotation, reads the rise order, and has the configuration. Theron has pointed at the place on the chart where the solution lives. He has not solved the puzzle. He has refused to let people fail in front of footsteps on the stairs.

His face after he says this: something has resolved in it. He is not ashamed exactly, but he is no longer neutral. He has crossed a line he has been holding for eleven years and he knows it and he is not going to pretend he does not.

## 5. Table Energy

This is a puzzle scene at the end of a session that opened with a street fight and a door riddle. The pacing risk is real. Know what your table needs.

**Players leaning in, engaged with the puzzle:**

Get out of the way. Give the room. The five-senses narration and Theron's silent presence are the only atmospheric fuel you need. Do not add complications. Do not introduce texture that creates questions the players have to track. They are solving a puzzle — let them solve it. Your job is to respond clearly to every action they take on the mechanism and the chart, confirm information when they read correctly, and deliver the payoff clean when they get there.

**Players frustrated — either with the puzzle itself or with the session's puzzle density:**

Lean on Theron. He is the lifeline in this mode. The oblique observation (Option 2) is the right tool: it redirects frustrated players toward the answer without the GM visibly lowering the difficulty. Theron offering a wondering aloud does not feel like the GM breaking down. It feels like the NPC being human. Use that.

You can also redirect frustrated energy toward the room itself. The cedar smell from the far end of the room — why only that shelf? The rolling ladder on the east track — where does it go when the configuration is wrong? The skylight showing the actual stars above Varenhold — the same stars Corven charted. These are observation prompts that do not solve the puzzle but give players something to engage with while the primary solve regains momentum. Sometimes a frustrated player needs to look at something else for thirty seconds before they come back to the problem fresh.

**Players disengaged — checked out from the puzzle, interested in the room or in Theron:**

This is not failure. A player who is paying close attention to Theron during the puzzle-working phase may see more emotionally significant content than a player who solved the mechanism in two minutes. Let them watch him. Give them what they see: the deliberate hands, the glances at shelf 4-17-3, the way he is older in this room than he seemed downstairs. These players are doing work that will matter in Sessions 2 and 3, even if it doesn't advance the mechanism.

If players want to ask Theron questions while others work the puzzle, run that conversation. Theron's answers in this scene are limited — he is managing his own emotional state carefully — but the questions players think to ask him here are diagnostic. What do they want to know about him? What are they noticing? Let them ask it.

**Players who bypass entirely — use the override key:**

Valid. Theron has an override key. If players ask him to simply open shelf 4-17-3 directly, he can do it — at the cost of logging the access under his name, which the Senior Archivist reviews weekly. This is a real cost, not a punitive one. Let players make the trade. When they do, Theron opens the section with the key, notes it in the access log, and says nothing about the mechanism. The paper trail exists. Whether it matters depends on what the Senior Archivist does with it in Session 2.

What this costs the scene: the star-wheel mechanism is one of Corven's most beautiful design choices — the room organized as a map of the sky, the answer literally overhead through the skylight — and players who bypass it miss the experience of moving through Corven's mind. They still get the documents. They still get Theron's moment when the shelf comes open. They just didn't earn the space the way Corven designed it to be earned. Note this for yourself as GM. It is not a wrong choice. It is a different relationship to what the Archive was built to be.

## 6. The Peak Moment

When the correct configuration is entered and the lower tone sounds from the floor, stop moving. Whatever else is on the table — initiative tracker, the footsteps thread, the players' notes on what they've already retrieved — set it aside. This moment gets the full room.

**The narration (from the Environment tab, adapted):**

The lower tone: felt in the soles before it is heard. The three-second pause that resolves rather than shifts — longer than the wrong-answer pause, and a table that has heard a wrong answer will notice the difference in those three seconds. Then the shelves rotate, smooth and slow, and this time the labeled faces come around. Cedar arrives with the 4-17 section as it turns — sharp, slightly sweet, the preserving smell of documents stored with care. The rolling ladder settles into position on the east track. Shelf 4-17-3 is at the top of the accessible face: seven document tubes, one map case, one smaller case set slightly apart, its brass clasp catching the light.

**The silence:**

After that narration, say nothing. Hold it for five full seconds. This is not a dramatic pause — it is space. The room has just done the thing it was built to do, for the first time in eleven years, and the scene needs to breathe before anyone speaks.

**Theron:**

He does not move immediately. His hands are at his sides and not fidgeting, which is unusual — he fidgets when anxious, and the absence of fidgeting means he is past anxiety into something that does not have a name yet. He looks at the shelf. He exhales once, controlled, almost silent.

*"Eleven years."*

He says it quietly. Not to the players — he may not be saying it to anyone. He says it the way people say things that have been true for a long time, finally saying them aloud. It is not dramatic delivery. It is the flat, factual tone of a man acknowledging an arithmetic. Do not add anything to it. Do not explain what it means. The players know what it means by now.

He does not go to the shelf. He waits. If players move toward the ladder, he stays where he is and watches them go. If they look to him first — waiting for him to lead — he shakes his head once and opens his hand toward the shelf: *yours, not mine.* He has been waiting eleven years for the right to say that. Let him say it with the gesture.

**Do not rush to the documents.** The players will want to go to the ladder. Let them go when they choose. The five seconds of silence after the narration, Theron's line, the gesture if it's needed — these are the moment. They deserve to happen before the scene moves into retrieval logistics.

## 7. Session Close

Players leave the Archive with the documents. Theron is changed in ways he will not fully process until tomorrow.

**What to carry out of this scene:**

The notation key fragment is Fragment 1 of 3. Players cannot decode Corven's cipher yet — they can see the structure, can recognize it as intentional encoding, can read Corven's name and the date and the instruction at the bottom (*"When you have all three, begin at the first mark"*). What they cannot read is what it says. That wait is deliberate. Let them want to read it.

The public notes document uses the phrase "living conduit mechanism" without defining it. The phrase is in Corven's hand, present tense, technical and precise — and the word "living" is doing work that players should feel without being able to articulate yet. If a player reads the document aloud at the table, deliver the phrase without inflection. Let it land in the silence after without your voice emphasizing it. Players who hear it will feel that "living" is wrong somehow, or more than they expected, or both. That is the correct response. They are right to feel that. They do not yet know why.

**Theron in the aftermath:**

He will walk them out. He will be quieter than he was in the reading room downstairs, and the distance between his two states — the professional archivist who met them downstairs and the man who stood in the restricted stacks while eleven years resolved — is visible to anyone who was watching both versions of him. If players ask him anything directly as they're leaving, he answers fully. He is past the point of calculated omission. The shelf is open. That changed his relationship to what he knows.

He will also, before they leave the building, say something about the other fragments. Not a speech. A sentence: *"There are two more fragments. When you come back — I want to show you where they are."* He has decided this is his investigation now too. Let that sentence be the last thing he says to them.

**The threads leaving Session 1 open:**

The notation key needs two more fragments, which are elsewhere in the Archive, which means a Session 2 return. The footsteps on the stairs — whether they were Theron's colleague, a junior archivist, or someone who should not have been there — are a thread that players either investigated and found inconclusive, or ignored and left dangling. The access log that Edoran's people may be monitoring. Theron's Restorer correspondence, visible in his coat pocket in Scene 2, still unexplained.

And the phrase "living conduit" sitting in the public notes, doing its slow work.

**How to end Session 1:**

Do not tie it off. Do not give the table a summary of what they learned or a sense of completion. The correct end is the players stepping back into Varenhold's amber permanent dusk with a cipher they cannot read and a phrase in their heads that does not quite add up. They know more than they did three hours ago. They do not know what it means yet. That gap is the engine of Session 2.

Leave the table hungry. A satisfied table comes back because they want more of what they had. A hungry table comes back because they need to know what the phrase means and what Theron is not saying and who was on those stairs. Hunger is the better reason.

The session ends. Shelf 4-17-3 is open, somewhere behind them, in a room that has been waiting fifty years to be read.
