## What This Scene Offers

This is a movement puzzle inside a combat. The enemies are not the main obstacle — the fireplace is. The soldiers are pressure that prevents the players from simply walking over to the tables and picking up the documents. The scene works when players feel the split-focus problem in their bones and make a choice that costs them something.

**Immediate stakes:** Visible from the opening image. No investigation required to understand what's happening.
**Visible objective:** Three bundles on three tables. A fire at the far end. Soldiers moving toward the tables.
**Embedded problem:** Players cannot save all three bundles and defeat all soldiers in the same action economy. Something will be traded.

---

## Primary Engagement: The Split-Focus Problem

The scene's core tension is not "can we win?" but "what do we prioritize?"

If all players focus on combat: soldiers fall, but at least one bundle enters the fire during the fight.
If all players focus on bundles: they secure documents but get overwhelmed by four Veteran-stat soldiers.
If they split: the math works — barely. Someone has to be in danger so someone else can be at the table.

The right play is inherently messy and collaborative. Players who have never coordinated tactically will be forced to. Players who do coordinate well will still feel the cost of the bundle they couldn't reach.

---

## Secondary Engagement: The Capture Opportunity

The Lieutenant knows things. He has already read the documents. Capturing him alive provides information that the bundles alone do not:
- Confirmation that the Reckoning has known about the documents for three weeks
- Confirmation that the Commander already has his own copy
- The phrase "secondary phase" (he won't explain it, but players will hear it)

Capturing him costs approximately two rounds of action economy from whoever commits to it. That is two rounds where a bundle may go unsecured. Players who think to capture him will naturally be in tension with players who are trying to protect the documents. This is a good problem for a table to have.

**Let players feel the temptation.** The Lieutenant is not hiding that he has information. His dialogue implies it. A player who asks "what are you burning?" gets a real answer — briefly, midstream, because he is genuinely curious whether these players understand what they've walked into.

## Player Type Matrix

### Combat Players

Soldiers prioritize bundles over fighting. A combat-forward player who charges in will find the soldiers frustrating opponents — they don't behave like creatures that want to win a fight. They're trying to complete a task. A soldier being attacked will take the hit if throwing a bundle means the task advances.

**The trap:** Winning the combat feels like the right move because it's familiar. The cost only becomes visible when a bundle enters the fire mid-fight. Let that moment register before moving on.

**The corrective:** Give them a moment to see the bundle in the fire. Then continue. The sunk cost is real — make it felt, don't dwell on it, move forward.

### Puzzle Players

The document prioritization problem is the puzzle. Bundle A is the highest-urgency target but the hardest to reach. Bundle C is the easiest to grab but the lowest-urgency. Bundle B contains the institutional thread (Keseph Vyne's name) but players don't know that yet.

**The real puzzle:** Which bundles do you save when you don't know what's in them? The puzzle players will want to assess before acting. Reward that impulse by making the observation round legible — they can see the labels, see which bundle the Lieutenant reviewed longest, see which is closest to the fire.

**The tension:** Puzzle players who stop to assess lose time. One observation round is valuable. Two observation rounds means Bundle A is gone.

### RP Players

The Lieutenant is the opportunity. He is not performing cruelty. He is doing a job he finds efficient. He will pause for a genuine question — briefly, once, during the combat — if a player engages him directly rather than attacking.

**If a player asks "what are you burning?" mid-combat:**
> He looks at them. Not with contempt. With the particular attention of someone who has just learned the situation is more complicated than they expected. He says: *"Something that was already read by the people who needed to read it."* Then he signals the soldier.

This is a real answer. It tells players that the burn is not about preventing knowledge — it's about controlling access to who knows, and when, and in what context. If they hear that and understand it, they're one step closer to understanding what the Reckoning is doing.

### Cautious Players

The observation round is a valid choice. If players hold the doorway for one round to assess — watching rather than entering — they see Bundle A being moved toward the fire. They have complete information about soldier positions, bundle locations, and the Lieutenant's role.

**What they see in that round:** Soldier 1 picking up Bundle A. Soldier 4 moving to block the entrance (he will close to attack range if players stay in the doorway into Round 2). The Lieutenant checking Bundle B.

**What they should feel:** Urgency, not panic. The situation is bad. It is not yet unrecoverable. One round of assessment is free. Two rounds costs Bundle A.

## Frustration Handling

If players feel overwhelmed mid-combat — surrounded, bundles moving, no clear path:

**Give them a break:** A soldier fumbles Bundle B while backing toward the fireplace. The bundle lands on the floor — a DC 10 Athletics check as a bonus action to scoop it up while moving past. Keep options open. The scene should be pressured, not impossible.

**If two bundles are already burned and players are struggling:** The Lieutenant makes a quiet decision. He signals his soldiers to withdraw — they've completed two-thirds of the objective. This is not charity; it's in-character. He did not come here to harm investigators. He came to burn documents. If two are gone, the operational value of continuing declines sharply.

**Never make players feel stupid for not knowing the right answer.** There is no right answer. There is only what they chose and what that cost.

---

## The Question Players Should Be Asking

By the time this scene ends — regardless of outcome — every player should be carrying one question:

**"They already knew. How long have they known? What else do they know?"**

The Reckoning did not discover these documents today. They had a list. They had a floor plan. They had a burn order. Someone read these documents weeks ago and filed them for destruction. That means there is a timeline running in parallel to the players' investigation — one that started before the players got involved.

That question is the bridge from Scene 1 to Scene 2. Theron, in the alcove, has part of the answer.

## Spotlight Rotation

In a scene with this much simultaneous action, spotlight can fragment. Use these as natural hand-off points:

| Moment | Hand to |
|---|---|
| Soldier 1 picks up Bundle A | The player nearest the east table — what do you do? |
| Lieutenant checks Bundle B | The player with highest Insight passive — you notice something about how he's reading it. What? |
| First bundle hits the fire | Full table pause. Let it land. Then: what does your character feel in that moment? |
| Lieutenant speaks during combat | The social/face player — he's talking to you. What do you say back? |
| Alcove door creaks (Complication 2) | The most observant player — you heard something behind that door. |
