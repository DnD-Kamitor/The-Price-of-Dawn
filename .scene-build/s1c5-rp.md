# S1C5 — The Confrontation: Scene Flow

<details>
<summary>🗣️ RP Scene</summary>

## Full Scene Flow

The scene has seven beats. They run in order as a default. Players can push beats forward or backward by how they engage. Beat 6 (THE CRACK) is conditional — it only triggers if a player asks the right question, and it should not be prompted.

The primary mode of this scene is conversation, not combat. Combat is a failure state: players who fight through Edoran reach the end of Session 1 without understanding the consent question, which is the thing this scene exists to deliver.

The secondary objective: get close enough for a real conversation. Players who approach and stay have won the scene, regardless of what they say or argue.

---

### Beat 1 — THE WAIT

*Players see him at the alley mouth. He doesn't move toward them. They have to choose.*

The Dawnhall door closes. Edoran is visible from the doorway — fifty feet south, at the alley mouth, in dark robes, standing slightly aside from the lane's center. He is not blocking anything. He is visible and still.

The four zealots are shapes in the alley shadow behind him.

He does not move toward the players. He does not call out. He waits.

::: {.prop-alt-ink}
**GM note:** Do not rush this beat. Let the players see him and make a choice. If players move toward the western passage at thirty feet — the one that exits to the market street — he lets them go, and says from fifty feet:

> "The woman you just met knows what she is. She chose. You don't know yet what she chose within."

He says this quietly, not a shout. It is audible at thirty feet if the lane is quiet. He does not repeat it.

If players stop and look at him, he acknowledges it: a single deliberate nod. Not a greeting. An acknowledgment that he sees them considering. He continues to wait.

If players approach — walk toward him — he watches them come and does not move until they are within ten feet. Then:
:::

He watches them cross the fifty feet without moving. When they are close — within ten feet — he speaks first.

---

### Beat 2 — THE OPENING

*"You have no idea what you are looking for."*

> "You have no idea what you are looking for."

He says it without preamble. Not aggressively. Not dismissively. It is an observation delivered by a man who has watched people look for this particular thing for a long time, and who finds the gap between what they are looking for and what they have found both precise and important.

He lets it sit.

::: {.prop-alt-ink}
**GM note on delivery:** This line is not an insult and must not read as one. It is an honest assessment of the players' current information state, delivered by someone who has more context. The tone is quiet. He is not impressed by himself for saying it. He has waited all day to say it, and it is not a performance — it is what he came to say.

After the line: he does not continue immediately. He gives players the space to respond. If they are hostile or dismissive, he remains patient. If they are curious, he continues. If they ask who he is:
:::

**If players ask who he is:**
> "Brother Edoran. I led the people who inconvenienced you this morning. I apologize for the method. I needed time."

He says this with neither pride nor excessive contrition. It is factual.

**If players are immediately hostile:**
> "I'm not here to fight you. I was here this morning and I could have — I didn't. I'm still not. You can hear what I have to say or you can go. Either decision is yours."

**If players ask what he wants:**
> "To tell you one thing before you report back to whoever sent you. One thing. After that you can do what you like."

---

### Beat 3 — THE QUESTION

*He asks what they found in the Archive. Not interrogation — calibration.*

After the opening, and after players have responded in whatever way they respond, Edoran asks:

> "What did you find in the Archive?"

::: {.prop-alt-ink}
**GM note:** This is not an interrogation. He is calibrating what players know — establishing a shared information state before he says the next thing. He is not extracting intelligence. He is asking in the same way a teacher asks a student to demonstrate their understanding before the lesson proceeds.

If players are guarded or refuse to share:
:::

**If players decline to say:**
> "You found the phrase 'living conduit.' Whether you want to tell me or not, I can see from how you're standing that you found something in the restricted stacks and it wasn't what you expected."

*(He does not press further. He states what he observes and waits.)*

**If players confirm what they found — "distributed sympathetic anchors," "living conduit mechanism":**

He nods. One, slow.

> "Good. Then we're starting in the same place."

**If players describe meeting Sera Voss:**

He is still for a moment. Not surprised — he knew they would. The stillness is acknowledgment.

> "She told you she knew. That she chose."

*(This is not a question. He knows she would have said this.)*

> "Then you know two things simultaneously: what she is, and what she's chosen. That's the whole question. In one room, with one person. You have it now."

---

### Beat 4 — THE LIVING CONDUIT BEAT

*He confirms what "living conduit" means. Obliquely, precisely.*

This beat comes naturally after Beat 3 if players have mentioned the Archive language. If they haven't, he introduces it:

> "You found the phrase 'living conduit.' You think it's a mechanism. It's not a mechanism."

He pauses.

> "It's ten people. Ten people who have been carrying something for fifty years that was supposed to have lasted a single night. You're not investigating a ritual failure. You're investigating what happened to the people who survived it."

Then, with the specific care he gives to every word about the Dawnborn:

> "The question is not whether you're here to ask them or to inform them. The question is whether you understand the difference."

::: {.prop-alt-ink}
**GM note:** This beat shifts the frame. Players have been thinking of the Dawnborn as a mechanism — ten anchors, a living conduit system, an engineering problem to be solved. Edoran is telling them that the people are not components in the mechanism. They are the mechanism, in the specific sense that no approach that treats them as components will work.

This is partly practical (Corven built consent into the design as an engineering requirement) and partly moral (he is, in his way, defending the Dawnborn's personhood, even as his ultimate position is that they should complete the ritual). Both are true simultaneously. He would not separate them.

If players push on the mechanics:
:::

**If players ask what "the completion requires their release" means specifically:**
> "It means what it says. I won't say it more plainly than that — it is not my secret to give you plainly. But I will tell you this: Corven designed the ritual with consent as an engineering requirement, not an ethical nicety. Without genuine consent, it fails. He was specific about this. The people who want to pressure or coerce the Dawnborn are building a machine that will not run."

He says this with visible frustration at Harran Lecht, though he does not name him directly here.

---

### Beat 5 — THE CONSENT ARGUMENT

*This is where he says what he actually believes.*

::: {.prop-alt-ink}
**GM note:** This is the scene's intellectual core. Edoran's theological position is not simple, and it should not be delivered as if it is. He has been building this argument for twenty years. It is rigorous. It is also, underneath, grief organized into a position. Both are present simultaneously.

Do not rush this beat. Let it be as long as it needs to be. Players who engage seriously get a serious interlocutor. Players who dismiss get patience. He has heard every objection.
:::

If no player has raised the consent question, he raises it himself:

> "I've spent twenty years hearing people argue about the Dawnborn's consent. Whether they should choose. Whether anyone has the right to ask. Whether the cost is too high to be voluntary."

He pauses.

> "None of those arguments start in the right place. The question is not whether the Dawnborn should choose for themselves. Of course they should. The question is what it means that they were born for this, and what the choice looks like when a person understands that."

**If players argue that "born for this" is coercion:**
> "Were you born to breathe? Does the fact that breathing is necessary make your choice to breathe coerced?"
*(He holds up a hand before they can answer.)*
> "I know that's not a perfect analogy. Nothing in this situation has a perfect analogy. But the distinction I'm making is real: the Dawnborn didn't choose to carry what they carry. They do choose what they do with it. That second choice is theirs. The first is not. I don't confuse those two things."

**If players argue that the Dawnborn should simply not complete the ritual:**
> "Then more people die. The grey sickness advances. The city spends another decade in permanent dusk watching its population diminish. And the Dawnborn continue to carry something that was supposed to last a single night, for fifty more years, until they die anyway — and the energy they held dissipates uselessly, and the ritual cannot be completed, and the sun stays down."
*(Flat. Not emotional. He has thought through this outcome and it does not look better to him than the alternative.)*
> "Is that a better death than a chosen one?"

**If players argue that more time is needed to find alternatives:**
> "I have been looking for alternatives for twenty years. If you have found evidence of one — a pathway that does not require what the design requires — I will stand here and listen to you describe it. I will be genuinely glad to hear it."
*(He means this. He has looked.)*
> "But 'more time' is not an alternative. More time is more grey sickness."

**On his theological position — the consent as form, not precondition:**
> "The consent is not the precondition. The consent is the form the act takes. You cannot peel them apart. A Dawnborn who completes the ritual under coercion is not completing the ritual — Corven designed it this way. The energy requires a voluntary release. Without genuine will, the conduit does not open. This is not philosophy. This is engineering."

---

### Beat 6 — THE CRACK

*Conditional. Only if a player asks the daughter question.*

::: {.prop-alt-ink}
**GM note:** Do not prompt players toward this. Do not have NPCs ask them why Edoran built this movement, or whether he has personal stakes, or anything that points toward the daughter. If players find this crack, they found it themselves.

The question takes any of these forms:
- *"Why did you build this movement?"*
- *"What happened to make you care this much?"*
- *"Did someone you know die of grey sickness?"*
- *"Why didn't your daughter get to choose?"* (If players have somehow learned about Mira — they haven't yet, but if they ask the right intuitive question)
- Any version of: the Dawnborn's consent is the form their death takes, but who gave Mira a consent question?

When it lands — when a player says the thing that touches the crack — he stops.

This is different from his normal deliberate pause. This is a man who has been brought up short.

He is still for a long beat. Not defensive, not evasive. Still.

Then:
:::

> "She didn't."

Two words. He does not follow them with anything. He is looking at the players, or looking at the middle distance slightly past them — the gaze of a man who has just heard the asymmetry he carries spoken aloud.

::: {.prop-alt-ink}
**GM note:** Do not let Edoran explain. Do not add a follow-up line from him. The line is complete.

Players who push for more get silence first. Then, if they push again:
:::

**If players push for him to explain:**

He looks at them.

> "She died at seventeen. Of the grey sickness. Three winters before I left the priesthood."

He does not say her name. He does not say that the movement is built on her. He does not say that everything he has argued for twenty years has her as its foundation. Players can see this or not see it. It is there.

Then, after a beat that is slightly too long to be comfortable:

> "That doesn't change the argument. I know that. But you asked."

He does not continue on this thread. He returns to the scene — not immediately, not with false composure. He takes a breath. Then he continues.

::: {.prop-alt-ink}
**GM note:** The scene is different after this beat. He is quieter. Not broken — he has carried this for fourteen years and will continue to — but the precision of his argument has been replaced, briefly, by the grief that built it. Players who found this crack leave Session 1 with a question about Edoran that is not about the ritual. Good.
:::

---

### Beat 7 — THE EXIT

*He steps aside. The session ends here.*

Whether players have been fully engaged, briefly engaged, or hostile and then disengaged — when the scene reaches its natural end, Edoran says:

> "The Restorers will not trouble you again tonight."

He steps aside. Not backward — to the side, in the specific way of a man clearing a lane that was never blocked.

::: {.prop-alt-ink}
**GM note on the phrasing:** "Tonight" is the word. Not "again." Not "ever." Tonight. He is telling them there is more, later, without saying so directly. This is deliberate. Players who catch the word catch a thread. Players who don't will catch it in retrospect.

He does not wish them luck. He does not say the conversation mattered. He does not apologize for the morning. He steps aside and the lane south is open.

The session ends when Edoran is no longer in their path. Do not begin a new scene. Do not extend the session. The session is over.
:::

**If players have one final question as he steps aside:**

He stops. He turns back. He answers it — fully, with the same precision he has given everything else in this scene. Then he goes. One question. It receives its full weight.

**If players try to follow him into the alley:**

The four zealots in the alley shadow shift — not threateningly, but physically. Edoran speaks over his shoulder:

> "The conversation is finished. Go."

The zealots do not engage unless players physically force the issue.
