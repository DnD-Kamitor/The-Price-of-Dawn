## Read-Aloud — Arrival at the Dawnhall

*Read this as players cross the threshold of the Dawnhall for the first time.*

---

> The door opens inward and the warmth hits before your eyes adjust. Forty people at long tables — most of them grey, some deeply, some only recently. Quiet conversation. A child half-asleep against her mother's arm. A man coughing once, softly, from the far corner. No one reacts. The cough is not an event here.
>
> On a hook by the door: a yellow scarf. It is the only yellow thing in the room.
>
> At the northeast corner, a woman in a worn apron has her back to you. She is serving soup from a cauldron. She knows you're there — you can tell by the particular stillness of her shoulders. She fills one more bowl. Hands it to the teenager beside her; he takes it without being asked and turns toward the tables. She removes her cooking gloves and turns around.
>
> She is not surprised to see you. She does not look nervous.

---

## Read-Aloud — "I've Been Waiting to Be Told"

*Deliver this exactly when it arrives in the conversation — after players have told her what they know, after the natural pause.*

*The table should be quiet when you say this. If it isn't, let the quiet arrive before you do.*

---

*She has been listening without interrupting. The last thing one of your group said has settled. She looks at the table for a moment. Then at you.*

> "I've been waiting to be told for a long time."

---

*Stop. Let it sit.*

*She does not add anything. She waits. The room is still. Forty people are eating soup ten feet away and the soup is unremarkable and the woman across from you has just said that someone telling her something she already knows is the thing she has been waiting for for years.*

*The room has not changed. The bowl she was served to the teenager is still in his hand somewhere near the west tables. The man in the corner coughed once and did not cough again. The bell by the kitchen has not rung yet.*

*What do you do?*

---

## Read-Aloud — The Bell

*Use this when the scene has run its natural course.*

---

> A single tone from the bell above the kitchen door. Clear, not loud. Lights-out.
>
> She stands. She doesn't rush it. She looks at each of you — once, briefly, directly.
>
> Then she picks up a cloth from the nearest table and she is already moving toward the far side of the room before she has fully turned away.
>
> The door to the kitchen is still half-open. The soup is still warm. The yellow scarf is still on the hook by the door.

---

## Player Engagement Matrix

---

### The Action Player

This is a quiet scene. There is no fight, no chase, no immediate danger. Some action players will feel this acutely — the absence of something to do.

What to give them: the Insight checks. Frame them as action. *"You've been reading rooms for a long time — what do you see?"* Insight in this scene is not passive; it is the active work of reading a person who is not giving everything away all at once. The DC 10 is obvious. The DC 18 requires sustained attention and presence.

Also: watch them watch the room. An action player who has spotted the yellow scarf, noticed the teenager, clocked the man coughing in the corner — that is engagement. The observation is the action here. Honor it.

**What they need from this scene:** Something to read that is not simple. Sera is not simple. She rewards sustained attention in the same way a complex combat rewards sustained tactical thinking.

---

### The Investigation Player

Three distinct threads to pull, each leading to a different subsequent scene.

**Thread 1: The Ashring Inscription**

She has been there. She could not read it. She knows it is there. This is a gap in her knowledge and in the Archive's knowledge — a detail that is unresolved and unresolvable from this room.

> "There's an inscription at the base. I've never been able to read it. But I know it's there."

This thread opens in Session 4. If players flag it now, it activates then.

**Thread 2: Consent Status of Other Dawnborn**

She will not say. But the shape of her refusal is informative: she says she does not *always* know their answers, which implies she sometimes does. She is not lying — she genuinely does not have full information. But her precision about what she knows and doesn't know is a data point for an investigator.

**Thread 3: Tomas Areth**

> "Tomas thinks too carefully. I mean that as a compliment. He is braver than he knows."

She says this and moves on. The investigation player will file it. By Session 3, when they meet Tomas, they will understand exactly what she meant by *braver than he knows* — and it will land differently than it sounds here.

---

### The Role-Play Player

This scene is built for them. Sera is fully present, fully real, willing to answer anything that is actually asked.

What the role-play player needs to do: ask the questions that aren't on the investigation list. The scarf. What she cooks for herself. What she finds complicated about her faith. What the Ashring felt like.

She gives more to the question she actually wanted to be asked. That question — *"how are you?"* or anything that is genuinely about her as a person — unlocks:

> "I'm all right. Most days."

And if pushed:

> "Ask me again after."

The role-play player who finds this beat has found the scene's emotional center from a different direction than the "I've been waiting" line. Both matter. This one is quieter. It is more hers.

**What they need from this scene:** Sera to be fully available to them. She is. Meet her.

---

### The Problem-Solver

The problem-solver wants the secondary objective stated. Here it is:

The secondary objective of this scene is to ask Sera something you don't need to ask for the investigation.

That is the objective. It is stated. It is not obvious how to execute it, because the problem-solver's training is to identify what is needed and acquire it. The skill this scene is asking for is different: it is asking you to stop acquiring for a moment and be in the room.

If the problem-solver can do this — if they can ask her about the scarf or the kitchen or her faith and mean it rather than using it as a technique for relationship-building — they will find that it is not empty. There is something there. Sera answers the non-investigation questions more fully than the investigation questions, because they are the questions she has been waiting for.

**What they need from this scene:** To discover that the secondary objective is the point. Not a side-task. The actual thing the scene is for.

---

## What Players Do with the Conversation

Sera does not hand players anything physical. She gives them her words, her attention, and three threads that go somewhere.

At the end of the scene, when the bell rings and she goes, players are left at the table with:
- Everything she said, held in memory and notes
- The yellow scarf on the hook, unasked-about (if they didn't ask)
- The soup still being served by the teenager at the far tables
- The knowledge that forty people depend on this building, and the building depends on her

They have not been asked what they are going to do with what they know. They have been given the knowledge. The decision about what to do with it is theirs.

---

## Handling Player Silence After "I've Been Waiting"

If the table goes quiet — genuinely quiet, the silence of people who have just heard something land — do not fill it.

She does not fill it either. She is in the silence with them.

If the silence extends and a player looks like they are about to speak but haven't: wait. They are getting there.

If a player asks "what do you mean?" — they are in the scene. Answer them as Sera, directly:

> "I knew what I was before anyone came looking. I've been the only person in my life who knew for a long time. It's different, when someone else knows."

The silence is not awkward. It is the scene doing its job. Sit in it.
