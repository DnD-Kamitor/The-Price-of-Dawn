## Pacing

This scene runs until the bell. That is the scene's duration — not a timer, not a page count, but the organic conclusion of the service night at the Dawnhall. In real time: 15–30 minutes. Let it breathe. The breathing is not padding; it is the scene doing its work.

Players have spent two scenes in archives. They have been solving puzzles, reading documents, handling information. This scene is different: a person, in a room, doing her work, who already knows what they came to ask. The shift from archive-mode to person-mode is the transition the scene is designed to create. Do not rush it.

---

## Sera Is Not a Quest-Giver

The most important GM note for this scene.

Sera Voss has information players need. She is not an information dispenser. These are not the same thing.

The scene fails — not mechanically, but emotionally — if players treat her as a database to query and she as the interface. She will answer their questions efficiently if that is what they bring. She will answer them more than efficiently if they bring something else. The scene should produce the second result. This requires the GM to model what the second result looks like.

How: When Sera answers a question, give the answer and then let Sera's posture remain. She does not return to neutral between questions. She stays present. She is not waiting to be queried again. She is in a conversation.

Players who feel this will begin doing it too.

---

## The "I've Been Waiting" Beat

This is the scene's emotional center. Deliver it correctly.

The setup: players have told her what they know. A natural pause arrives. Then she says:

> "I've been waiting to be told for a long time."

**What this means:** She already knew. She has known for years. The "telling" she waited for was not her being informed — she informed herself, eight months of Archive work, three years into running the Dawnhall. The "telling" she waited for was someone else knowing. Someone outside her own head. Someone with resources and authority who could actually do something with the knowledge.

She has been alone with this for years. She is, at this moment, not alone with it anymore.

**How to deliver it:** Let the previous sentence — whatever the last thing a player said — settle. Wait for the actual pause in the conversation. Then say the line without buildup, without tone change from the rest of the scene. It is not a dramatic reveal. It is a statement, made into the space of the scene, that happens to carry everything.

**After it:** Do not add anything immediately. She doesn't. The players respond to it. Whatever they say, she receives. This is not a moment that requires managing — it is a moment that requires space.

---

## The Secondary Objective

Every scene in The Price of Dawn has a secondary objective. For this scene:

**Get players to ask something they don't need to ask.**

Any question that isn't investigation-relevant — about the scarf, about the kitchen, about her faith, about the teenager with the ladle, about what she cooks for herself — earns a relationship with Sera that pays dividends in Sessions 3-5.

The GM's job is not to engineer this. The GM's job is to make Sera feel like a person worth asking about. If Sera is rendered with full texture — the cooking gloves, the way she touches the pendant, the specific detail that the Dawnhall was a grain store, the yellow scarf that she never wears inside — players will generate these questions organically.

The secondary objective is not the investigation. The secondary objective is the person.

---

## If Players Are Transactional

Some parties will treat this scene like the archive scenes: information gathering, efficient questions, depart with findings. That is a valid approach and Sera will support it — she will answer their questions clearly, completely, without resistance.

What she will not do: offer more than she's asked. The warmth she has is not indiscriminate. It is extended to people who are present in the room with her. Players who are not present in the room — who are in investigator-mode, treating her as a source rather than a person — receive answers. They do not receive the scarf detail, the rice-with-herbs detail, the pendant-touch pattern.

She is not punishing them. She just doesn't perform warmth that hasn't been met.

**Downstream consequence:** Players who stay transactional do not have a relationship with Sera. They have her name and her words, noted in their investigation. In Session 5, her stone activates last. She is not hostile to them. She simply does not know them.

---

## If Players Try to Convince Her Not to Consent

She will not be argued out of this.

Her response, patient and final:

> "It's not yours to change."

She does not say this with hostility. She is not affronted by the attempt. She simply states the fact of her sovereignty over this decision and does not continue the argument.

If players persist: she is quiet for a moment. Then:

> "I've had years to think about this. I'm not asking you to be comfortable with it. I'm telling you what my answer is."

That is the end of this thread. She will discuss everything else. She will not re-examine her consent on request.

**What not to do:** Do not have Sera become defensive or emotional about this. Her steadiness is the whole point. She has made her decision. The players' discomfort with it does not change the decision. She holds both things simultaneously — their discomfort and her answer — without allowing one to override the other.

## What Theron Said (If Players Raise It)

In Session 2, Theron says of Sera: *"She will take this better than she should. That is the thing I most fear about telling her."*

Players will not have heard this yet — this is Session 1. But if they mention Theron's name in this scene:

> "Theron." She is still for a moment. "He is careful. He has always been careful."

She does not explain what she means by "careful." She means: he has known for years and has been protecting himself from the cost of saying it out loud. She does not say this. But she knows it. And now players have two people whose silences they will understand better when Session 2 arrives.

---

## Tomas Areth — The Aside

When the subject of other Dawnborn comes up, or when players ask who she is closest to, she mentions Tomas. She says he is "braver than he knows."

This is a small seed. Do not over-deliver it. Players will return to this phrase in Session 3, when they meet Tomas and discover that the person Sera called brave is carrying a specific kind of terror about his own consent. The bravery she sees is real. The terror is also real. Both are in the same person, and she knew about both when she said what she said.

---

## The Bell

When the scene has run its course — when the conversation has wound to completion, when a natural breath appears — ring the bell. In fiction, the Dawnhall bell rings for lights-out. In practice, this is the GM's tool for ending the scene at the right moment.

The bell is not a forced ending. If the scene is still alive — if players are in the middle of something real — let it run. The bell is available when it is needed.

After the bell: she stands, looks at each player once, goes. The scene ends with her already in motion.

---

## What Sera Does After Players Leave

She finishes lights-out. She moves through the tables, checking in with the residents settling in for the night. She touches the yellow scarf on the way out — left hand, briefly, at the hook — and walks home to Crestwick Alley, three streets south.

She does not think about the players specifically. She thinks about the conversation in the way of someone who has been waiting for a conversation and now has had it. She thinks: they don't know what they're carrying yet. She thinks: they will.

She is not afraid tonight. She is something closer to relieved.
