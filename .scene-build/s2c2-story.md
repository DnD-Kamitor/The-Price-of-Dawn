## Narrative Function: The Puzzle as Ceremony

Theron could not say this directly. He tried once, eleven years ago, and was administratively suppressed. After that, direct disclosure carried too high a risk — the wrong person receiving the information at the wrong time would bury it again, or act without consulting the people most affected.

So he encoded it. Not to protect the secret — the encoding is simple enough to break — but to create a ceremony of transfer. He gave the players a tool and waited to see if they would choose to use it. The cipher is a form of consent operating in both directions: he consented to disclosure by encoding it to be found; they consent to knowing by choosing to decode it.

This is also, and he knows this, rationalized cowardice. He built himself a door he could leave closed by never finding the right person to hand the key to. He kept finding reasons the time wasn't right. He knows that now. He knew it three years ago when he corrected the third slip — which is when he quietly admitted to himself that the ceremony was something he intended to complete before he died.

The cipher is not a puzzle. The cipher is eleven years of a man telling himself he was being careful when he was being afraid.

## What Changes for the Players

Before this scene, the investigation was historical and abstract. Corven's notation. Archive documents. Distributed sympathetic anchors. The language of a ritual that failed two and a half centuries ago.

After this scene, the investigation is present and specific. Ten people in this city are carrying the mechanism of the Founding Ritual in their bodies. The players know this. Most people in the city do not.

The moral weight has transferred. The players are now in the position Theron has occupied for eleven years: they know something, and knowing it requires deciding what to do with it. They are not bystanders to that decision. They have become, as Theron intended, participants.

This is the campaign's first transfer of burden. The session dilemma — *Do you have the right to decide someone else's fate, even to save thousands?* — has moved from being a question about the past to being a question about the next hour.

---

## Theron's Arc Beat

Theron has been in one position for eleven years: witness. He saw the evidence. He understood what it meant. He kept it. He said nothing usable to anyone who could act on it.

This scene ends that position.

The moment he hands over the slips — the moment the players decode them and he says "Yes" — he is no longer a witness. He is a participant. He chose the players. He chose this moment. He is accountable now in ways he was not before.

This matters in Sessions 3–5. Theron-as-participant is useful. He will help. He will take risk. He will make decisions that witness-Theron refused to make. But he will also carry the weight of eleven years of being a witness, and that weight will shape how he helps and what he is willing to do.

Players who understand this arc — who understood it from the moment he sat in the visitor's chair rather than behind his desk — will have a different relationship with him in Session 4 than players who saw him only as exposition.

## Bridge to Scene 3

The resonance box on the shelf behind his desk.

Theron stands, straightens his pen case, and says: *"There's one more thing. Upstairs."*

The players follow him out of the reading room. The scene transition is simple — no new combat, no obstacle. The Archive's interior stairs. The door to his private study.

The box is visible from the doorway. Theron does not point to it immediately. He lets players see it before he explains what it does. Whatever a player's eyes go to first, when they enter the room, will tell you something about where they are in the story.

---

## Bridge to Scene 4

The resonance box (Scene 3) measures the presence of ritual energy in proximity to a Dawnborn individual. Theron will demonstrate this with a Dawnborn subject — a visit is arranged, or a record is reviewed — and the reading will confirm everything the slips implied.

Scene 4 is the confession.

The slips were the prelude: *here is what I knew.* The box is the evidence: *here is proof it is real.* The confession is the synthesis: *here is what I did not do about it, and here is what I know you will have to decide.*

The three scenes — 2, 3, 4 — are a single movement. Scene 2 gives the players knowledge. Scene 3 gives them proof. Scene 4 gives them the full moral problem.

By the time they leave Scene 4, they will not be able to unknow what they know. They will have to look at ten specific people and carry a question those people cannot yet ask themselves.

---

## What the Table Should Feel

Not triumphant. Not horrified — not yet. The horror comes in Scene 4, when "vessel" becomes "extinguished."

What the table should feel at the end of Scene 2 is: *we are no longer investigating an abstract mystery.* We are investigating something that will require us to look ten specific people in the eye. Something that Theron has been unable to look at directly for eleven years.

The puzzle was solvable. The problem it reveals is not.

That gap — between the solvable cipher and the unsolvable moral question — is the emotional geometry of this scene. Players who feel it are ready for what comes next.
