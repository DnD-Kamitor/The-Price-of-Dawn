## Scene 3 NPC Roster

Three presences in the restricted stacks. One is in the room. One is present only through the objects he left behind. One is a sound.

---

## 1. Theron Waide — In Scene 3

### His Relationship to This Space

Theron Waide has been inside the restricted stacks. More than once. He knows the star-wheel mechanism intimately — every notch of the three rotating rings, the sound each shelf section makes when it rotates into position, the particular shudder the central column gives when the configuration is wrong.

He has never opened shelf 4-17-3.

This is the thing players may not think to ask, and the answer matters. He decoded the notation key eleven years ago. He identified the shelf location from Corven's cipher. He has stood in this room, in front of this mechanism, with everything he needed to reach the final documents — and he turned around and walked out. He locked the door behind him. He went back to his desk.

He has been living with that choice ever since.

**Why he did not operate it himself:** The answer he would give, if pressed carefully in this scene, is procedural — *"It is not my investigation to open. The documents belong to whoever the Chancellor authorizes."* This is technically true and entirely insufficient. The real answer is that Theron decoded enough of the notation key to know what the documents would say, and he could not bear to read confirmation of what he suspected. He has been waiting for someone else to open the door so that the knowledge becomes theirs, not only his. He has been trying to distribute the weight before it crushes him.

He also has a smaller, uglier reason: he is afraid of what would happen to him if the wrong people knew he had read those documents. He has been slowly destroying his own notes. He has not been able to destroy the notation key fragment, and he has not been able to destroy himself, which amounts to the same cowardice from two different directions.

**What this means in Scene 3:** He is present in the restricted stacks as a man who has been waiting for someone to do the thing he could not. He will not admit this. He will describe the mechanism in technical terms. He will offer small observations. He will not touch the star-wheel.

---

### What He Does While Players Work the Puzzle

Theron stands slightly to one side of the central column — not behind the players, not ahead of them, but at an angle where he can see both the mechanism and the star chart without being in anyone's way. He has been here before and his body knows the space.

**His default behavior:**

He watches. Occasionally he shifts his weight. When a player asks him to confirm a reading on the star chart, he crosses to look at it — deliberately, not rushing — and says something that is helpful in an indirect way: *"The Dawnmark rises in a specific sequence. Corven was precise about astronomical order. He always was."*

He does not touch the mechanism. Even when a player is about to set a ring to the wrong position, he does not reach out. He will say *"I wonder if that's the rise order or the current position"* — a question framed as an observation, something that might be wrong or might be useful. He is not being coy. He genuinely finds it easier to approach certainty sideways.

When the shelves rotate on a wrong configuration — the shuddering grind of heavy wood and brass moving to a new position — he flinches. Not dramatically. A small, controlled reflex, the same thing his face does when he hears a document being torn. Noise in a library is viscerally unpleasant to him, and the mechanism's sound is the noise of something going wrong in a space he has spent eleven years protecting.

**Between attempts:** He finds small useful tasks. Brings the lamp closer to the star chart. Checks the notation beside one of the ring symbols and nods once, to himself, at something he already knew. If the party is quiet and working, he is quiet and working beside them — not hovering, not directing, just present. A player who watches him closely during a pause will notice he is not looking at the mechanism or the chart. He is looking at shelf 4-17-3 from a distance, already visible as a labeled position on the shelf face. He looks at it the way people look at something they have been not-looking at for a long time.

---

### Key Lines

**If asked to help directly — "Theron, you've been here eleven years. Just tell us the answer."**

He pauses. A real pause, not a performance of one.

> "I can tell you how the rings are engraved. I can tell you the chart is accurate as of the night of the ritual. I can tell you Corven believed in an audience earning what it found."

Another pause.

> "He left it solvable. I think he wanted it solved. He just — did not want it solved easily."

He will not say the answer. Not because he is being obstructive — because he has decided, in eleven years of sitting with this, that arriving at the answer himself is the only form of honest engagement with it. If the players solve the puzzle, they solved it. He needs that to be true.

*GM note: If players push with genuine frustration rather than asking analytically — "People might be dying because of what's in that shelf, just help us" — Theron goes still. He looks at the player. Then he says, quietly:* "I know." *And nothing else. He does not give the answer. But something in his face has opened slightly — the layer of procedural deflection has come off. He is a man who has been sitting next to a door for eleven years telling himself he had good reasons not to open it. The player just named the cost of that.*

---

**If players hit exhaustion — second failed configuration, footsteps audible, shelves in the wrong position:**

He straightens. Something shifts in how he is standing — he stops leaning and stands properly, the posture of someone who has made a decision.

> "The star chart. Start from the left edge of the Dawnmark grouping. Not the brightest star — the one that rises first. Corven always worked from first principles."

This is not the answer. It is the correct direction to find the answer. He is not circumventing the puzzle — he is pointing at the place on the chart where the solution lives. A player who follows this observation will find it quickly. Theron is not going to watch them flail while someone approaches on the stairs.

---

**When 4-17-3 opens:**

The shelf rotates into position. The configuration clicks — a lighter sound than the previous grindings, something that resolves rather than shifts. The shelf face labeled 4-17-3 is now accessible.

Theron does not move immediately.

He looks at it. His hands are at his sides, not fidgeting, which is unusual — he fidgets with objects when he is anxious, and he is not fidgeting now. He is very still in the specific way of someone who is past anxiety and into something that does not have a name yet.

Then he exhales. One controlled breath, almost silent.

> "Eleven years."

He says it quietly, not dramatically — the way people say things that have been true for a long time, noting them as true. He does not say it to the players. He may not be saying it to anyone.

He does not rush to the shelf. He waits to see what the players do. If they move toward it, he stays where he is and watches them go. If they look at him first, waiting for him to lead, he shakes his head once — *this is yours, not mine* — and gestures toward it with an open hand.

*GM note: This is a personal moment for Theron, not a plot moment. Do not rush past it. Give it five seconds of table silence. He has been waiting for this for eleven years and he does not know yet whether what is inside will absolve him or confirm that he was right to be afraid. Both possibilities are present in his face simultaneously. Players who have been paying attention to him will see this. Players who have been focused on the puzzle may not. Both responses are valid.*

---

### Physical Description in This Scene

He is wearing his working coat — the ink-stained one, not the formal one he wore to meet them downstairs. He brought a hand lantern because the restricted stacks are dim and he knows it. His hair is slightly disordered; he came quickly when the first wrong configuration sounded. He smells of lamp oil and the particular dusty cold of an old stone building — the same smell as the rest of the Archive, so concentrated on him that he has stopped noticing it.

He is older in this room than he seemed downstairs. Not physically — he is the same age he was twenty minutes ago. But the restricted stacks hold eleven years of a decision he made, and it is visible on him here in a way it was not in the public reading room.

## 2. Archmagister Corven — Present Through Objects

Corven died fifty years ago. He is not in this room. He is entirely in this room.

The star-wheel mechanism is his. The astronomical organization system is his. The notation key fragment on shelf 4-17-3 is his handwriting, his cipher, his private thinking made legible only to someone who has earned the right to read it. The entire architecture of this space is an argument — Corven believed that knowledge should require effort to reach, and the restricted stacks are that belief made physical. Players who move through this room are moving through the mind of a man who has been dead for fifty years, and they should feel that.

---

### Personality as Legible Through Design Choices

**The astronomical organization system — a mind that thinks in patterns**

Corven did not organize his life's work by subject or date or access level. He organized it by the sky. This is not whimsy. This is a person for whom the cosmos was the organizing principle of thought — someone who, when asked to file documents, reached instinctively for the most fundamental order he knew. The star chart on the east wall is not decorative. It is how he thought. Players who understand this understand something about why the ritual he designed was astronomical in scope: it was not a clever choice, it was the only kind of choice Corven made.

The system also has a specific quality: it is *learnable*. The chart is in plain view, labeled in common script, comprehensive. He did not hide the key to his organization system. He hid the documents inside it, but he left the door visible. This is a man who believed in the puzzle being solvable — who designed his private spaces for an audience who would come eventually, not for permanent secrecy. He expected to be found. He just insisted on being found correctly.

**The notation key — a man who kept secrets in layers**

The fragment players find on shelf 4-17-3 is part of a larger system. Corven's private cipher is not a code designed to exclude — it is a code designed to *sequence*. To read his personal journal requires all three fragments, assembled. The first fragment alone is partial. This is deliberate: he is not protecting information by obscuring it. He is requiring that anyone who reads his most private thinking do so in order, following his reasoning as he developed it. You cannot skip to the conclusion. You have to walk the path.

This suggests a man with strong opinions about how knowledge should be received — specifically, that understanding must be earned through process, not just access. He is generous with access. He is uncompromising about process.

**The notes' voice — what kind of writer was he?**

Corven wrote in the first person, in the present tense, as if writing to a reader who was there with him. Not to posterity, not to an archive — to someone sitting across the table. His sentences are long. He qualifies. He circles back to correct himself: *"the anchor — no, that is the wrong word, the anchor implies passivity, I should say conduit — the living conduit mechanism requires..."* He was a man who thought by writing, and who trusted his reader to follow the corrections.

He is not cold in his writing. He is precise, which is different. When he writes about the ritual, he writes about it the way an engineer writes about a structure — with admiration for the architecture that is entirely technical and entirely warm. He loved this ritual. Whatever happened, he loved what he was trying to build.

---

### What Fragment 1 of the Notation Key Reveals About Corven's Character

Fragment 1 is a single sheet. It is not the beginning of the notation key — it is a middle section, which suggests the fragment is genuinely incomplete, not the whole of Corven's cipher. What is present:

A list of seventeen symbols, each with a brief gloss in Corven's hand. Most of the glosses are technical — *circle with ascending line: return-point; triangle open at apex: variable cost; interlocking rings: sympathetic bond.* Standard notation elements for advanced ritual work.

But two glosses are different in character from the rest. They are longer. They repeat themselves slightly, as if Corven was working out what he meant as he wrote it.

The first: *"The open hand — not, as in prior notation, 'offering given.' Rather: the particular quality of a thing that is freely decided. The distinction matters. An offering can be compelled in its form while the hand that gives it is not free. This symbol marks what cannot be compelled — not the gift, but the freedom of the giving."*

The second, beside the symbol for the ritual's central mechanism: *"The weight borne willingly is not the same weight as the weight imposed. This is not a philosophical observation. It is an engineering specification. The mechanism will not function as designed if the distinction is collapsed."*

**What this reveals:** Corven was not careless about consent. He was not vague about it. He built it into his notation as an engineering requirement — not a moral preference, not a political consideration, but a *specification*. The ritual fails if the weight is not willingly borne. He knew this. He wrote it down. He put it in his cipher, inside his locked stacks, behind a puzzle no one solved for fifty years.

The players are holding the first piece of evidence that whatever the ritual cost, Corven did not intend to impose it.

---

### "Distributed Sympathetic Anchors" and "Living Conduit Mechanism" — How Corven Writes About Enormous Things

The public notes on shelf 4-17-3 use these phrases without explaining them. This is typical of Corven's public-layer documents — they assume the reader has the technical background and do not define terms.

But the *tone* of these phrases is consistent with the rest of his writing, and that tone is revealing.

**Clinical:** Yes. He does not use metaphor when he can use a technical term. "Distributed sympathetic anchors" is a precise description of a specific ritual architecture — multiple components maintaining a shared effect over a geographic area, held in place by sympathetic resonance rather than direct magical maintenance. He names the thing exactly and moves on.

**Reverent:** Also yes, but quietly. When he writes about the mechanism as a whole — as a complete system, not as individual components — his sentences get slightly longer, his qualifications slightly fewer. A passage like *"the conduit mechanism, once established, becomes self-sustaining through the willingness of the components; it is, in this sense, the most elegant solution to the persistence problem I have encountered in forty years of ritual architecture"* is not the language of someone who is merely performing a technical function. He finds this beautiful. He is proud of it. The pride is visible even in documents he expected only other ritual architects to read.

**Frightened:** Not in the public notes. There is no fear in the documents on shelf 4-17-3. The fear comes later — in his personal journal, which requires all three notation key fragments to decode, and which players will not reach until Session 2. What is present here is the early-stage Corven: confident, precise, in love with his own architecture. The fear came after. It always does.

*GM note: If players ask "what does 'living conduit' mean?" — do not answer them yet. The notation key fragment is suggestive, not explicit. The phrase "living conduit mechanism" beside the symbol for the ritual's central mechanism is the moment players should feel the ground shift slightly under the phrasing. "Living." Not "sustained" or "maintained." Living. Let them sit with that word. Theron, if asked, will say only: "Corven used 'living' in a specific technical sense. I believe it is defined in the later fragments." He is telling the truth. He is also relieved not to have to say more.*

## 3. The Footsteps — If Triggered

**Trigger condition:** Two failed full configurations of the star-wheel mechanism. The shelves rotate twice incorrectly. The noise — a low, structural grinding, followed by the percussive click of shelf sections locking in place — is audible from the floor below.

The footsteps come approximately ninety seconds after the second failure. Soft, deliberate, ascending from below. They stop at the landing outside the restricted stacks.

---

### Who Is It? Three Options

The GM should decide before the session which option applies, or allow the fiction to determine it based on what has happened so far. These are not all equally likely; the GM guide for each option indicates when to use it.

---

**Option A — Theron, coming to check (most likely, especially if Theron is NOT in the stacks)**

*Use when:* Theron was outside the door during Scene 2 or chose to wait at the landing rather than entering with the party.

The footsteps are Theron's. He heard the second configuration fail and came up to see whether he should intervene. He stands at the door. If the door is not locked from inside, it opens slightly — he looks in, takes in the situation, and does not enter unless spoken to.

**What he says from the doorway:**

> "The configuration sound carries to the third floor. I thought — I wanted to see if you needed the chart reading confirmed."

This is an offer, not a direction. He will come in if invited. He will stay in the doorway if not.

*GM note: The tell for players that this is Theron rather than a threat: the footsteps did not hurry. Someone who heard something dangerous would hurry. Someone who heard something familiar and wanted to check on it would move exactly as Theron moved — deliberately, at the pace of concern rather than alarm.*

---

**Option B — A junior archivist who heard the shelves move (low stakes, texture only)**

*Use when:* The party wants a moment of tension without complications; you want to introduce the Archive's human presence without plot weight.

A junior archivist — late twenties, ink-stained apron, holding a lantern in a way that suggests she has never had a reason to hold it as a weapon before — stops at the landing when she hears the door is in use. Her name is not important. What is important: she knows the sound the mechanism makes, because it used to make that sound occasionally when Theron came up here, and then it stopped, eleven years ago. She has never been inside the restricted stacks. The door opens and she sees people in a room she was told was sealed.

**What she says:**

> "I heard the shelves. I didn't know anyone had authorization to —"

She stops. Sees Theron, if he's present. Her expression does the work: *oh. He finally let someone in.*

She does not need anything from the party. She is not a threat. She is the Archive's institutional memory in human form: she knows this room was sealed and for how long, and seeing it open clearly affects her in a way she cannot entirely process in real time. She can be spoken to. She will answer questions honestly. She does not know what is in shelf 4-17-3, but she knows it exists.

*GM note: This option works best as a small human moment rather than a complication. She gives the players a sense of how the Archive experienced this sealed room — as an absence, as a door that was always locked. Her small reaction to seeing it open is a gift to any player who has been watching the room with the same attention she has.*

---

**Option C — Someone who should not be here (raise stakes significantly)**

*Use when:* The campaign needs friction beyond the puzzle; you want to introduce early-stage Reckoning presence or Restorer intelligence reach without a combat scene.

The footsteps belong to neither Theron nor staff. They stop at the landing, and then there is silence — not the silence of someone who left, but the silence of someone who is listening.

If players open the door: a corridor that should be empty. The lamp on the landing has been angled away from the stairwell, reducing visibility down the stairs. Someone has been here recently and is no longer here.

If players move to investigate: faint boot marks in the dust on the stairs, leading down. Too careful to be accidental. Someone moved through this space knowing they were not supposed to be here and took steps not to leave clear traces. They did not entirely succeed.

**What the dust prints tell them:**

| Check | Information |
|---|---|
| Investigation DC 12 | Two sets of prints, one heavier than the other. They came up together and left separately. |
| Investigation DC 15 | The lighter set stopped at the landing for longer — weight distribution suggests standing still, listening, for several minutes. |
| Survival DC 13 | The prints were made after the second shelf rotation, not before. Whoever this was, they were already on their way up when the mechanism sounded. They knew to come. |

**Who it was:** The GM has two options. First: Reckoning scouts, establishing their presence before Session 2. They were not here for the players specifically — they were monitoring the Archive for exactly this kind of activity. Players who investigate will find nothing that identifies the Reckoning by name; what they find is a level of operational sophistication that does not match an opportunist. Second: a Restorer contact, monitoring Theron's access decisions on Edoran's behalf. The implications are different but the physical evidence is the same.

*GM note: Do not resolve Option C in Scene 3. It is a hanging thread. The question of who was here and what they heard is the seed; the payoff is Session 2. If players want to pursue it immediately, let them — they will find the trail ends in the public reading room where the person blended into normal Archive traffic. They know someone was there. They do not know why or who. That is enough.*

---

### What Happens If Players Investigate vs. Ignore

**If players investigate:**

They spend time they might need on the puzzle. The trail gives them information (options above) but costs them approximately five to ten minutes of real-time focus. The footsteps question becomes a secondary thread they are now carrying out of the scene.

Theron, if present, watches them investigate with a particular expression — not anxious, exactly, but alert in a way he was not before. He knows the Archive. If the footsteps were not his and not staff, he knows that is unusual. He does not have a face for unusual things happening in his Archive; it looks like very controlled concern.

*If players ask him directly "do you know who that was?" he says, honestly:* "No. And that is — not a comfortable answer." *He does not elaborate. He returns to the mechanism.*

**If players ignore:**

The puzzle continues. The thread closes for now. The thing about ignored threads in the Archive is that the Archive keeps records. When players return in Session 2, a notation in the after-hours access log will show that someone entered the building after hours on this evening — a detail Theron mentions without emphasis during the Session 2 debrief. He mentions it because it has been bothering him.

Players who ignored the footsteps in Scene 3 and hear about the access log in Session 2 have a choice: go back to this now, or let it sit. The correct answer is: both options have costs, neither is wrong.

## 4. Attitude Shift Table — Theron After Scene 3

Theron entered this scene carrying eleven years of a decision. What happens in the next thirty minutes changes how he carries it — not whether, but how.

| Scene 3 Outcome | Theron's Attitude Shift | How It Manifests |
|---|---|---|
| Players solve the puzzle without assistance (no hints from Theron, chart read independently) | **Relieved and slightly awed.** They did what he could not, without needing him. He did not have to give them anything — they just arrived and did the work. This is the outcome he hoped for and barely believed was possible. His guilt is the same weight, but he is not ashamed in front of them. | In the debrief after shelf 4-17-3 opens, he is voluble — not chatty, but willing to speak beyond what is necessary. He offers to show them the second notation key fragment location. He has decided they are serious. |
| Players need Theron's observation to find the direction (the "first-principles" hint) | **Relieved with a faint edge of shame.** He helped. He did not hand them the answer, but he moved the needle. He can tell the difference between those two things and so can they. The shame is for eleven years of not doing this; the relief is that it is done. | He is quieter after the shelf opens. Not closed off — quieter. He answers questions fully but does not volunteer. He is processing. He needs tonight to sort out what today meant. By Session 2, he is more open, not less. Tonight was the hinge. |
| Players hit both failures, were pushed by a second noise, solved it under pressure | **Shaken, then steady.** The footsteps made it real — this was not an academic exercise, someone else knows something is happening here. He spent Scene 3 trying to be useful without taking over, and the pressure revealed how much he wanted to intervene. He held himself to it. He is steadier than he was going in, because he found out what his limits were and stayed inside them. | He checks the landing himself after the scene. He locks the door to the restricted stacks. He is quieter on the walk down. Toward the end of the evening, if players are still with him, he says: *"If you come back tomorrow — there are two more fragments. I want to show you where they are."* He has decided. This is his investigation now too. |
| Players ask Theron directly what he suspected and he admits it (Insight DC 15 after shelf opens) | **Cracked open.** The admission is not a confession — it is the first honest thing he has said to anyone about this in eleven years. He is frightened of what that means. He is also, quietly, relieved in a way that is close to physical — the specific bodily lightening of something that has been too heavy for too long being slightly lifted. | He tells them about the notes he has been destroying. He does not tell them what the notes said, because he has already destroyed most of them and what remains would only partially confirm what the ritual documents will make clear. He tells them because he cannot not, now. The shelf is open. He is past the point of calculated omission. |
| Players finish the puzzle, take the documents, leave without speaking to Theron | **Intact, but hollow.** He did his job. He guided them to the space, observed without interfering, let them take what they needed. Professionally: exactly right. Personally: he has been waiting eleven years for this moment to mean something to someone, and they left before he could see whether it did. | He sits in the restricted stacks for a while after they go. He looks at shelf 4-17-3, now empty of the documents it held. He leaves eventually. In Session 2, he is correct and helpful and there is a small formal distance in him that was not there before. Players who notice it and ask about it will learn that distance is the thing Theron does when he does not know what he meant to people. |

---

*NPC design note: Theron and Corven are both present in Scene 3, but they occupy different registers — Theron is the live wire, the man whose guilt and relief and shame are available in real time; Corven is the architecture, the mind that shaped the space they are all moving through. The best moments in this scene hold both: a player at the mechanism, working through Corven's design, while Theron watches and does not intervene. Two people honoring the same work in opposite ways. One built it. One protected it. The players are the first ones to walk through it in fifty years.*
