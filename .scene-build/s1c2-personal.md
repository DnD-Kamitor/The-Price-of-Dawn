## Scene 2 — Personal Hooks by Archetype

These inserts are delivered *before* the first riddle attempt, slotted in after the junior clerk says "this door hasn't opened in eleven years" and before any player calls out an answer. Each is addressed quietly, directly to one player. The goal is not to give the answer — it is to make the door feel like it was sealed specifically for the person standing in front of it.

---

## Part 1 — Per-Archetype Sensory Inserts

Deliver each to the relevant player individually, as a brief aside while the rest of the table absorbs the door. These are additions to the scene setup, not replacements for the general narration.

---

### To the Investigator

> The inscription isn't just carved — it's annotated. Look at the notation on the lower left register, under the main riddle text. Someone has cut a second layer of marks into the stone at a slightly different angle — newer chisel-work over older stone. Corven built the lock. But someone else, years later, added something to it. That second layer is in a different hand. Whoever touched this door after Corven sealed it did not open it. They read it, understood it, and left their mark anyway. The door isn't just a puzzle. It was a message to whoever came next. Someone was here and decided not to answer.

*GM note: The Investigator's read is that the door has a history independent of the riddle. The second-layer notation is not a game mechanic — there is no additional check hidden in it. It is texture that their archetype would notice: the sense that someone stood in this exact position before them and made a different choice. Do not elaborate on what the second notation says. It is in Corven's private cipher; Theron can identify the hand as his own, if asked, from eleven years ago.*

---

### To the Believer

> The word carved at the center of the inscription — the one the answer will fill — has had offerings left in it. You can tell because the groove is slightly discolored at the base: old wax, worked in and worn down. Someone lit a candle here. More than once. The scent is too faint to catch now, but the residue is clear if you put your face close to the stone. Someone came to this door and prayed to it. Not Auris-standard — there's no ritual notation in the frame, no consecration marks on the arch. They weren't performing a liturgy. They were asking. The door, the riddle, the sealed word: in the Litany of the Open Hand, consent and prayer are the same act. A request freely made. An answer freely given. The Wounded faction prays to Auris the way they would speak to someone they love who is in pain. They ask. They don't command. This door asks too.

*GM note: If the Believer is Auris-aligned (either faction), they will recognize the Litany of the Open Hand reference in the riddle's fourth line — "the thing that makes the difference" is scripture-adjacent language. The wax in the groove is Theron's, from eleven years ago, when he decoded the door and stood in front of it and still did not answer. He does not volunteer this. He will confirm it if pressed, in Session 2.*

---

### To the Survivor

> The junior clerk said eleven years. You do the math without meaning to. Eleven years ago was Year 39. Year 39 was when grey sickness moved from the Ashfen into the Lowmark. The Archive stacks in the restricted section have been sealed since before [the person you love] got sick. Whatever is inside has been sitting there since before the first round of worst winters. Before the Dawnhall started turning people away for capacity. Before the food registry expanded to cover half the district. Someone sealed this door, and left it sealed, while the city got sicker. Whoever built this lock had an answer. They didn't give it. That is what eleven years of not opening a door looks like from here.

*GM note: The Survivor's read is temporal and personal. Do not soften the math. If the Survivor named their sick person in Session Zero — a parent, a sibling, a partner — let the calculation land with that name implicit. You do not need to say it aloud. The player knows. If they have not shared the name, the general grief of the timeline is sufficient. The point is: the door's eleven-year silence is the same eleven years the city has been declining.*

---

### To the Scholar

> Before the rest of the party has finished reading the inscription, you've already placed the notation style. Third-tier Corven — his later hand, post-Year 4, when he stopped writing for colleagues and started writing for the archive itself. You have seen this style before. Not on these walls, but in letters. Three winters ago, Theron Waide sent you a folio of Corven's administrative correspondence as supplementary context for the research you had asked about. The notation in the margin of that folio, the organizational marks he used to flag documents for his successor — they match this. This door is not just in Corven's style. It is in the same hand as documents Theron chose to send you. He knows this door. He built his correspondence with you around someone who would eventually recognize this door. You were being read.

*GM note: The Scholar archetype has been corresponding with Theron Waide — letters, shared documents, a slowly built research relationship. This insert activates that backstory by making the door legible through it. Theron did not literally teach the Scholar to read this notation. But he sent them examples. He may not have done it consciously. He may have done it because, on some level, he was looking for someone who would eventually stand here and be able to answer. When Theron appears on the landing after the first wrong attempt, he will recognize the Scholar by name. This insert is the reason that recognition lands.*

---

### To the Mercenary

> You read it the way you read any lock: who commissioned it, what are they protecting, what does the protection level tell you about the value of what's behind it. Answer: this is not a physical lock. No mechanism, no keyhole, no pin tumbler. It's a test. Specifically, it's a test designed by someone who wanted only a particular kind of person to get through — not someone with the right key, but someone with the right understanding. That is a very expensive design choice. You don't build a conceptual lock on a door unless the contents are worth more than any physical lock could protect. Whatever is behind this door is not retrievable documents in the ordinary sense. It's something you cannot just steal and walk out with. The security system is telling you what the job actually is: the thing you're being paid to find is the kind of thing that can only be found by someone who already understands why it matters.

*GM note: The Mercenary's read is a security assessment, and it is accurate. The door's design is itself intelligence about the job's real scope. The stated commission — document retrieval — does not match the protection level. This is the Mercenary's Scene 2 version of the Scene 1 realization: the job was never routine. Let this land as professional concern, not alarm. The Mercenary files things away. This goes in the file.*

---

### To the Dawnborn-Adjacent

> Sera mentioned the Archive once, in a letter. Not the restricted stacks — she wouldn't have access to those — but the public reading room on the second floor. She said she came here in Year 34, during the bad fever season, to find anything Corven had written about grey sickness treatment. She sat in the reading room for six hours and found nothing. She wrote about it briefly: *"Corven documented everything except what people needed."* You are now one floor above where she sat. The door in front of you is everything she couldn't reach. And the word the door is asking for — the word that opens it — is the same word that appears in the scripture on the note from Scene 1, if you still have it, and in the Auris passage Edoran quoted when he sent people to stop you from getting here. Everyone in this investigation has been circling the same word. Sera didn't know that when she sat in the reading room for six hours and went home empty-handed. You know it now.

*GM note: Sera's letter about the Archive is invented here as flavor consistent with her character — she is exactly the person who would have spent six hours in a public reading room looking for grey sickness documentation. Do not tell the Dawnborn-Adjacent player that Sera mentioned this in her letters unless you want to confirm it as canonical. If you prefer: deliver this as the character's inference — they know Sera well enough to know she would have come here, and they are standing one floor above where she would have sat. The emotional content is the same either way: Sera tried to find what this door is hiding, and couldn't get through.*

## Part 2 — Knowledge Triggers (Passive)

Deliver each automatically to any character whose passive score qualifies. No roll required; no announcement of the skill name. They simply know.

| Passive Score | Skill | What the Character Knows | How to Deliver It |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Arcana 12+** | Arcana | Notation-sealed doors — doors locked by a conceptual or linguistic key rather than a physical mechanism — were an Archmagister-tier technique, discontinued after the Year 7 Spire regulations on binding-class enchantments. They required ritual literacy to construct and were deliberately designed to resist brute force: no lockpick, no knock spell, no structural attack affects the mechanism. The only way through is the correct answer. There are four known examples in the city. | "You've read about this kind of lock. It's notation-sealed — the mechanism is linguistic, not mechanical. Brute force doesn't touch it. The only way through is saying the right thing." |
| **History 14+** | History | Corven's private cipher, used in his restricted correspondence and in his most sensitive academic work, was partially documented by a Spire historian in Year 12 as part of a review of the ritual's archival record. The review was classified. What leaked: the cipher is not a code. It is a compressed notation system that encodes meaning at three levels simultaneously — surface, sub-textual, and structural. A document in Corven's private cipher means something different depending on which layer you read. The riddle on this door is most likely surface-layer only. The full cipher is somewhere in the restricted stacks. | "Corven's cipher was reviewed by the Spire in Year 12. It's not a simple substitution code — it encodes at multiple levels. What's on this door is the readable layer. There's more inside that you can't decode yet." |
| **Religion 12+** | Religion | "Consent" — the specific word that unlocks this door — is the operative concept in the Auris doctrinal sub-text called the Litany of the Open Hand, which concerns freely-given sacrifice. The litany distinguishes between two kinds of sacrifice: bound (compelled, extracted, taken) and open (given willingly, with full knowledge and no coercion). Bound sacrifice, in the litany's theology, is not sacrifice at all — it is loss. Only the open hand gives. The litany was written before the Ritual of Eternal Dawn and is not specifically about the ritual. But its theological framework maps onto the ritual's design in ways that Auris clergy in Varenhold have been quietly discussing for forty years. | "That scripture — the fourth line of the riddle — it's Litany of the Open Hand language. The difference between a gift and a theft, in Auris theology, is consent. That's the specific word the Litany uses. The Wounded faction quotes it constantly." |
| **Investigation 13+** | Investigation | The door has been touched within the past eleven years. Not opened — the frame seal is intact, and the notation is undisturbed at depth. But the surface of the carved inscription shows the kind of wear that comes from hands: fingertips tracing the text, palms pressed flat against the stone. Decades of untouched stone would show even dust accumulation in the grooves. This stone has been cleaned, repeatedly, by someone who touched it often enough that maintenance became reflex. Someone has been coming to this door for years. Not to open it. To stand in front of it. | "The door hasn't been opened. But it's been touched. Recently. Repeatedly. Someone has been coming here and not going through." |

## Part 3 — Check-Result Narrations

When a player succeeds on one of the scene's active checks, deliver the story of what they perceive, not the mechanical result.

---

**Insight DC 12 — on Theron (after he appears at the landing, first wrong attempt):**

> He appears on the landing, coat slightly askew, and his eyes go to the door before they go to you. That is the tell. A man coming to address intruders looks at the intruders first. He looks at the door the way you look at something you have been worrying about. Then he looks at you, and the expression that moves across his face is not anger. It is something closer to the expression of a person who has been waiting for a knock and is not sure whether they are relieved or terrified that it finally came.

---

**Insight DC 14 — on Theron (deeper read, sustained observation):**

> He knows the answer. You are certain of it within thirty seconds of watching him. He is not puzzling over the riddle — his eyes do not move across the inscription the way someone reads. He is watching *you* read it. He has the particular stillness of someone who has already lived this moment many times in his imagination and is now managing the difference between what he imagined and what is actually happening. He is also afraid. Not of you. Of what it will mean if you get this right.

---

**Arcana DC 12 — on the door notation (general decoding):**

> The notation is three-layered, as Corven's cipher tends to be. The surface layer is the riddle in plain script — legible to anyone. Below that, in smaller marks at the groove-edges, is a structural layer: the notation is self-referential, defining its own terms. The word being requested is defined within the inscription itself, if you know to look for the definition in the sub-marks rather than the surface text. The word is: the condition under which the definition given in line one becomes possible. You can cross-reference that against standard Auris devotional vocabulary. It narrows fast.

---

**Arcana DC 15 — on the door notation (deep decode, Corven's private cipher):**

> There is a fourth line of sub-notation that does not appear in the surface inscription. It reads, in Corven's compressed cipher, as: *"This is what I should have required before I began."* He built a door that says what the ritual should have asked for. He built it after. He built it knowing it was too late.

*GM note: Deliver this only if a player specifically asks to decode the deepest layer and succeeds at DC 15 Arcana. This is a significant narrative revelation that changes how the door reads retroactively — it is not a sealed Archive entrance, it is a confession. Theron, if present, will go very still when this is read aloud. He knew this line was there. He decoded it eleven years ago. He has been sitting with it since.*

---

**History DC 14 — on the cipher (Corven's authorship):**

> The hand is Corven's — unambiguously, if you have any reference material for comparison, and the Archive's public floors have several framed examples of his formal administrative script. What confirms it is not the letterform (which could be imitated) but the notation spacing. Corven placed his sub-marks at a consistent 2:1 ratio to his surface characters, a structural quirk documented in the Year 12 Spire cipher review. No forger would know that detail. No student would have reproduced it unconsciously. This door was made by the same person who made the ritual. The same hand that created what killed the sun built the lock on the room that explains it.

---

**Perception DC 10 — on the door (ambient observation, no specific target):**

> There is a detail no one asks about. The floor in front of the door is clean. Cleaner than the landing behind you, cleaner than the stairwell, cleaner than the rest of the third floor. The Archive is dusty in the way that archives are — a fine, specific dust of old paper and binding leather that settles on everything. Except here. The two-foot radius in front of this door has been swept, recently and regularly, by someone who needed the floor clear in front of it. Whoever comes here comes often enough that keeping the floor clean became automatic. They were not cleaning for company. They were cleaning for themselves.

*GM note: The clean floor is Theron. He has been sweeping in front of this door for eleven years. This is not something he will explain unless directly confronted. If a player points it out in front of him, he looks at the floor for a moment and then back at the players, and says nothing. His silence on this particular observation is the most revealing silence of the scene.*

## Part 4 — Post-Scene Personal Questions

After the door opens — or after Theron's guidance moves the scene to RP — each archetype is left holding a different version of the scene's weight. These are the personal questions Scene 2 plants in each character's arc.

**Investigator:** *Someone stood in front of this door before me, understood it, and chose not to answer. That was eleven years ago. What were they protecting — the city from the answer, or themselves from the cost of giving it?*

**Believer:** *The litany says consent makes the difference between a gift and a theft. The ritual happened without it. The door is asking the question the ritual forgot to ask. Is that justice, or just guilt left carved in stone?*

**Survivor:** *The door has been sealed for eleven years. For eleven years, people in the queue downstairs have been waiting for something to change. Whoever built this lock had the answer and sat on it. I understand waiting. I am not sure I understand that.*

**Scholar:** *Theron Waide corresponded with me for three years. He sent me materials that would help me recognize this cipher. He built the relationship that brought me here. He has known the answer to this riddle for eleven years. What was he waiting for — the right person, or permission he couldn't give himself?*

**Mercenary:** *The security architecture of this door is disproportionate to "documents." The job I was hired for and the job I am actually doing are not the same job. At what point does completing the contract require me to understand what I'm actually being paid to deliver?*

**Dawnborn-Adjacent:** *Sera sat one floor below this door in Year 34, looking for anything Corven wrote about grey sickness, and found nothing. The answer was up here the whole time. She doesn't know that. When I see her next, she is going to ask what I found. I am going to have to decide what to tell her about a door she once almost reached.*

## Part 5 — Callback Hooks (Session Zero Dependent)

If players established specific background details in Session Zero, use these amplifications when delivering the sensory inserts.

**Investigator** — *If the player specified a prior investigation or a particular case that brought them to Varenhold:* When describing the second-layer notation on the door, connect it to the investigative register the player established: *"The marks are consistent. Someone knew what they were looking at and made a record that they had been here, in the same way that [specific reference to prior case or method] — a note left for whoever came next."* The Investigator's profession is pattern recognition. Let the door confirm the pattern.

**Believer** — *If the player specified their Auris faction (Penitent or Wounded):* The wax-in-the-groove detail lands differently by tradition. For a **Penitent**: the wax offering is irregular — Penitents do not petition, they observe. Someone from outside their tradition came here and prayed anyway. For a **Wounded**: this is recognizably Wounded practice — the persistent, tending quality of leaving offerings for something that has not yet responded. The Wounded pray to a god who may be hurt and silent. This door has been treated like that god. For **Morthis** or another tradition: the offering is legible as devotion without being legible as familiar. Someone cared about this door the way a person cares about a grave.

**Survivor** — *If the player named their sick person:* In the Year 39 calculation, say the name once, quietly: *"Year 39. The year [name] first showed symptoms."* Do not pause for effect. Continue immediately. The player will hold it without help.

**Scholar** — *If the player specified particular documents Waide sent them or named their research focus:* When describing the notation match between the door and Waide's correspondence, name the specific document: *"The notation in the margin of the [folio name/subject] he sent in [winter/year]. The same sub-mark spacing. He sent you the key and didn't tell you what the lock looked like."*

**Mercenary** — *If the player named their employer or stated the job's described scope:* When the Mercenary assesses the disproportionate security level, make it specific: *"[Employer's description of the job] doesn't warrant a notation-sealed door. Either [employer] doesn't know what's behind it — or they do."* Do not resolve this. Leave it as a question the Mercenary is now carrying.

**Dawnborn-Adjacent** — *If the player shared details of Sera's correspondence or their relationship history:* When describing Sera's Year 34 visit to the Archive reading room, use a detail from the established correspondence if available: *"She mentioned the reading room once. [Quote or paraphrase from established letter.] She was looking for something Corven wrote about grey sickness. She was one floor below this door."* If no specific letter detail exists, the general Year 34 inference is sufficient. Specificity is the goal; if you cannot be specific, be present.

### Theron Waide Recognizes the Scholar — The Moment of Recognition

This beat happens when Theron appears on the landing after the first wrong attempt. It is a private beat for the Scholar, but the whole table sees it.

Theron comes up the stairs, coat askew, ink on his sleeve. He looks at the door. He looks at the party. His eyes move from face to face with the practiced scan of a man cataloguing visitors — and then they stop.

> *"You're [Scholar's character name]."*
>
> Not a question. A statement. He says it with the particular quiet of someone who has been reading a correspondent's letters long enough to feel like they know the handwriting's owner, and is now confronted with the face.
>
> *"I've been reading your questions for three years. I always thought — when someone eventually came — it would be you. Or someone like you."*
>
> He looks at the door again. Then back at the Scholar.
>
> *"You're going to have better questions for me after you've been through it. Come find me in the reading room when you're finished."*

*GM note: Theron does not explain what he means by "or someone like you." He means: someone whose letters showed they understood that finding the answer would cost something, and were asking anyway. The Scholar's correspondence with him was, in retrospect, an extended interview for this exact moment. He was looking for someone who would arrive at this door and be ready to go through it. The Scholar should feel, in this moment, both chosen and set up — because both things are true.*

*If the Scholar pushes back — "You could have told me what you found" — Theron says: "Yes. I could have." He does not elaborate. He goes downstairs to wait.*

---

*Design note: Scene 2 has one answer and it is not subtle — CONSENT in capitals, the campaign's central word, arriving at the end of the first hour. The personal hooks exist to make sure that word lands differently for each player. The Investigator hears it as a question about who decides. The Believer hears it as scripture. The Survivor hears it as eleven years of not deciding. The Scholar hears it as the thing Waide could not say in letters. The Mercenary hears it as a security assessment with implications. The Dawnborn-Adjacent hears it as something Sera was one floor away from. The answer is the same for all of them. What it costs each of them to say it is not.*
