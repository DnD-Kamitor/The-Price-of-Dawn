# S3C2 — Isolde's Door: Environment & Five Senses

<details><summary>👁 Sight: The Stairwell Approach</summary>

The stairs are narrower than the alley. One person wide, ascending — the person behind you must wait while the person ahead clears the turn. The wood is dark with age and lamp oil, the grain compressed smooth by decades of the same feet in the same path.

The first landing. A window: boarded over, not plastered, not finished — boards nailed horizontally over the frame from the inside. Daylight finds the gaps and throws flat white lines across the landing floor. It is the only natural light in the building. You can see tool marks along the board edges if you look. They're on the inside face.

Second flight. Narrower still. The ceiling drops by two inches at the top stair — either intentional or the building has settled, no way to tell.

The landing at the top: eight feet wide, six feet deep. One door. No windows here. The only light is what comes from below — the slivers from the boarded first-landing window, filtered up through the stairwell.

The door itself: heavy wood, iron-banded at the vertical seams, iron plate at the frame where a keyhole would be. No keyhole. The plate is solid. Continuous. Whatever locks this door, you cannot see it.

And pinned to the door at eye level — precisely, deliberately, at the height where your eyes go first — a card. Stiff paper, the kind used for official correspondence or scientific record-keeping. A single nail through the upper center. The handwriting is small and precise.

::: {.prop-alt-ink}
**GM:** The boarded window on the first landing is a player-accessible discovery, not a hidden one. Any player who looks at the window sees it. The tool marks are readable on a DC 10 Perception. The critical detail: the marks are on the inside face of the boards — someone installed them from inside, planning an exit, not patching a break-in. This is Isolde's escape route. Do not call attention to it. It is there for players who look.

The two-inch ceiling drop at the top stair is purely sensory. Give it as a moment of physical adjustment — someone taller might duck slightly. It makes the landing feel like a distinct space, removed from the building below.
:::

</details>

<details><summary>👂 Sound: What a Building Sounds Like When One Room Is Occupied</summary>

The crowd noise fades as you climb. Not gradually — it drops off in sections, one flight at a time. By the first landing, it is muffled. By the second, it is gone.

What replaces it: the specific quiet of a building that has stopped being used. The chandler's shop below is closed today. No wheel-creak, no scraping of furniture, no voices. The building's sounds are structural — the complaint of old wood under foot, the faint tick of the walls as temperature changes, the sound of your own breathing in a confined space.

Then, at the second-floor landing, you stop moving and listen.

Under the door, at the floor line, something that is not silence. It is very low. A hum — the way metal sounds when it is under sustained vibration, not ringing but resonating. Barely audible. If someone were speaking on the other side of the door you would hear that too. There is no speaking. There is only the hum.

It is steady. It has been going for a while.

::: {.prop-alt-ink}
**GM:** The hum is the Transfer Apparatus, already running. A DC 10 Arcana check identifies it as lux crystal array resonance — lux crystals sustain a sympathetic vibration when charged, and Isolde has been running the array long enough that the crystals are at equilibrium. This is not the lock. This is the lab.

For players who ask what kind of sound it is: *"Like a tuning fork that someone struck a long time ago and forgot to dampen."* That's accurate enough.

The transition from crowd noise to stairwell quiet should be palpable. The crowd scene was dense with sound. Here, the absence of sound is itself information — the building is isolated, the researcher is alone, the problem upstairs has been contained in one room for three years.
:::

</details>

<details><summary>👃 Smell: The Stairwell and What Comes Through the Door</summary>

Old wood. The kind of old that has absorbed everything the building has ever housed — lamp oil, tallow, sawdust, the residue of whatever the chandler's shop has made and stored and sold for thirty years. It is not unpleasant. It is simply the smell of a building that is older than anyone in it.

Damp stone at the landing. The building's exterior walls here, no plaster cover, exposed stone that breathes the city's permanent cold. Moisture has collected in the mortar joints. Nothing wet to the touch, but the smell is there.

And under the door — faint, present, not identifiable without context: something metallic and slightly sharp. The way copper smells after rain. But held, sustained, as if whatever is producing it has been producing it for a long time. Not acrid. Not a warning smell. But not natural either.

Something is working in there. It has been working for a while.

::: {.prop-alt-ink}
**GM:** The chemical smell is the Transfer Apparatus — specifically the lux-conducting elements of the array, which oxidize slightly under sustained activation. It is not dangerous at this concentration. It would be dangerous if the apparatus were running at full test capacity with no ventilation. It is not.

Players who ask what the smell reminds them of: DC 12 Arcana or DC 14 Nature — it is consistent with sustained amber-lux discharge, the kind produced by Spire research equipment. It should not be in a rented room in the Ashring district.
:::

</details>

<details><summary>✋ Touch: The Stair, the Card, the Door</summary>

The stair rail is worn smooth. Not polished — the kind of smooth that comes from hands, over time, the finish long gone and the wood beneath it compressed to something almost glassy at the grip points. You know, without thinking about it, that many people have climbed these stairs.

The landing boards are solid underfoot. No give. The building is old but well-built at the bones.

The card pinned to the door: if you reach for it, it is slightly waxy under the fingertips. Coated paper — the kind used for technical documents that might be handled repeatedly, that needs to resist moisture. This is not scrap paper. Someone chose this paper specifically.

The door itself, if you lay your hand flat against the iron-banded wood: warm. Not the warmth of recent sunlight — there is no sunlight here. The warmth of a room where something has been running, generating heat, for hours or days. Just enough to notice if you press your palm against the wood and hold it.

::: {.prop-alt-ink}
**GM:** The door's warmth is the final sensory hint before players engage with the puzzle. It confirms that the room is occupied and active. A DC 8 Perception — effectively automatic for a player who describes touching the door — gives them: *"It's warm. Not hot. The kind of warm that takes hours to build up."*

Do not volunteer the warmth. Give it only if a player describes touching or pressing against the door. It rewards physical engagement with the environment.
:::

</details>

<details><summary>📄 Handout: The Riddle Card — Full Text</summary>

*Read this aloud when players examine the card, or hand it to them as a physical prop if playing with print materials.*

---

> *I am the same at the beginning and end of a prayer.*
>
> *I live between noise and music.*
>
> *You cannot hold me, but you can give me away completely.*
>
> *What am I?*

---

*Below the riddle, in smaller handwriting — a later addition, slightly different ink:*

> *Please don't misfire it. The neighbours have complained twice.*

---

::: {.prop-alt-ink}
**GM:** The card is meant to be readable, not puzzling in format. Isolde did not make this obscure. She made it functional. The riddle is for filtering — it is designed to let in people who think laterally, not people who pick locks.

The addendum ("Please don't misfire it") is the only moment in this scene where Isolde is something other than precise. It reads slightly exasperated. Someone has misfired it. Possibly more than twice. She has been in this room for three years; the neighbours have complained; the evidence is here in the postscript.

If players ask what "misfire" means: DC 10 Investigation on the door and frame reveals a faint discoloration of the iron bands at the center of the door — the kind of residue left by repeated sustained-tone events. The lock has fired, at full volume, multiple times. Isolde has had this door for a while.
:::
