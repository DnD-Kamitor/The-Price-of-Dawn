## 1. Physical Description — The Restricted Stacks

### The Space

The restricted stacks occupy the Archive's fourth floor, reached through the sealed door from Scene 2. The room is long and narrow — roughly 80 ft deep, 30 ft wide — with shelves running floor to ceiling on both sides and along the far end, interrupted only by the central column and the east wall. The ceiling is twelve feet high. The shelves are close enough together that a broad-shouldered person turns sideways to pass between them comfortably. The gaps between shelves are not aisles so much as channels — straight, dark, with the sense that the shelves have always been slightly closer together than they need to be.

No one has properly worked in this room for eleven years. The dust is not catastrophic — the Archive below is well-maintained — but it has settled in a particular way: heaviest on the flat tops of volumes, lighter on spines, absent only where someone has recently moved something. The players can read the room's history from what has been touched. Almost nothing has been touched.

The light is sourceless amber, the same permanent half-dark that fills all of Varenhold. It comes from nowhere specific in this room — there are no lanterns on the shelves, no windows. The light simply exists at a low, even level, as though the room is lit from inside its own materials. Enough to read by, barely. Just enough to see that you cannot see everything.

---

### The Shelves

Each shelf section is labeled at eye level with a constellation marker: a small brass plaque, engraved with a symbol and a name below it in common script. The names are short — *The Shepherd*, *Vael's Crown*, *The Anchor*, *Trailing Light* — each paired with a line-etched symbol matching what is on the corresponding ring of the star-wheel. The arrangement is not alphabetical or chronological. The filing logic is astronomical: position in the sky determines position in the room, as though Corven built the Archive as a physical map of the sky overhead.

The shelves run floor to ceiling on iron brackets bolted to a track system set into the floor and ceiling. Looking closely at the floor, players can see the grooves — parallel channels cut into the stone, worn smooth, running the full length of the room. The shelves can move. They are designed to move. The tracks are what allow the wrong configurations to rotate sections out of reach.

Documents are stored in slotted tubes and flat cases, all labeled in the same hand: meticulous, compressed, slanting slightly to the right. Corven's handwriting. Many of the labels are faded. Several have been amended in a second hand — Theron's, neater, with small notation marks in the margins of the labels indicating cross-references to the floor below. Theron has been maintaining the outer edges of this system from a distance. He has not gone deep in years.

**Shelf 4-17-3** is at the far end of the room, against the north wall, top row, reachable only by the rolling ladder on the east track. Before the mechanism is correctly set, the shelves in the 4-17 range face away from the players — their backs visible, not their labeled faces. The shelf exists as a wooden surface and a set of document tubes visible only edge-on. The ladder rolls past a blank wall. There is no obvious way in.

---

### The Ambient Light and the Stars

Here is what is different about this room, and what the players may or may not notice immediately: the dome above the restricted stacks has a skylight. Opaque glass, thick, ribbed — it does not function as a window in any meaningful sense. But the permanent amber twilight of Varenhold means the sky outside is always dim enough that stars are visible. Through the ribbed glass, faintly, the sky shows. The stars Corven used as his filing system are not historical reference. They are present. Above this room, visible through the skylight, the sky shows what it has shown every hour of every day for fifty years: the stars of a permanent astronomical dusk.

Players who think to look up will see them — blurred through the glass, but real. The Dawnmark constellation is among them. Corven did not design his system from memory. He designed it so that in this room, the answer is literally overhead.

## 2. The Star-Wheel Mechanism

### Location and Appearance

The central column runs floor to ceiling at the midpoint of the room — a structural pillar of dark stone, roughly 18 inches in diameter, wrapped at chest height in a collar of tarnished brass. The collar is the mechanism. It protrudes about 4 inches from the column face and is divided into three distinct rings stacked vertically, each about 3 inches tall, separated by thin ridges of plain metal.

The rings are heavy. Not immovably heavy, but heavy enough that turning one requires deliberate effort — two hands, a grip, a sustained push. The kind of weight that communicates that what you are doing has consequence.

Each ring is engraved around its full circumference with constellation symbols, spaced evenly, twelve in total per ring. Each symbol is a simple line etching: a few connected dots and curves, clearly representational once you have the star chart to compare against, cryptic without it. Below each symbol, in very small common script, is a name. The engraving is precise and dense. The brass has darkened with age in the incised lines, which makes the symbols easier to read than they would be on clean metal.

A narrow indicator needle is fixed to the column face above each ring — a simple pointer, immobile, pointing at whichever symbol is currently aligned. At rest (the configuration when the players enter), the three rings show:

- Ring 1: aligned to *The Anchor*
- Ring 2: aligned to *The Anchor*
- Ring 3: aligned to *The Anchor*

All three pointing to the same symbol. This was Corven's resting state — the configuration that locks everything in its most neutral position.

---

### How the Rings Feel

Each ring turns smoothly in one direction (clockwise) and with more resistance in reverse. The mechanism was maintained at some point — there is no grinding, no grit. It clicks faintly as each constellation symbol passes the indicator needle: a small, precise sound, like a well-made lock engaging. Turning past one symbol to the next requires about 30 degrees of rotation. A deliberate push. One click.

Twelve symbols per ring. If a player counts: they can reach the next symbol with one push; the wrong symbol requires either continuing forward through the remaining ten or reversing with more effort. The mechanism is not punishing about exploration. It will let you turn to anything. It only punishes commitment.

---

### What Happens on a Correct Configuration

All three rings set correctly — Ring 1 to Vael's Crown, Ring 2 to the Shepherd's Eye, Ring 3 to the Trailing Light — produces a sound that is different in quality from the wrong-answer tone. Not louder. Lower. A resonance that seems to come from the floor rather than the column, as though the mechanism connects to something underneath the stone.

A pause of about three seconds. Then the shelves move.

The 4-17 section rotates — smoothly, slowly, over about fifteen seconds — turning to face the room. The labeled faces come around. The documents are now accessible. The rolling ladder on the east track is in position. Shelf 4-17-3 is at the top of the accessible face: a row of seven slotted document tubes, one flat map case, and one smaller case, narrower, sitting slightly apart from the others as though placed last.

The smaller case is where Corven's notes and the Notation Key fragment are.

---

### What Happens on a Wrong Configuration

The wrong-answer tone is mid-range, clear, and carries. It is the sound of a mechanism doing exactly what it was designed to do — a functional sound, not a warning sound, which makes it feel stranger. It resonates for about two seconds and then stops.

The shelves rotate.

This is not violent. It is methodical. The 4-17 section (and two adjacent sections) turn away from the players with the same smooth motion that the correct answer would produce, except that the destination is worse: the labeled faces rotate out of sight and the shelf backs come around, presenting blank wood. The room reconfigures around the players while they watch. The rolling ladder rolls to a new resting position on the track. The paths that were open are now closed. New gaps appear between other sections. The room is the same room, but differently organized, and the reorganization takes long enough to complete that watching it is slightly disorienting.

The operator — whoever was turning the rings at the moment of commitment — feels the mechanism's resistance shift for a moment before the tone sounds. Not a warning exactly. A change in texture. If a player specifically says they are paying close attention to the feel of the ring, they can notice this shift before the tone completes (Passive Perception 14, or active DC 12 after the first failure) — not enough to prevent the rotation, but enough to inform future attempts.

**Exhaustion:** The rotation does not cause exhaustion from the mechanism itself. The exhaustion comes from the physical effort of turning the shelves back — or from the time cost of navigating the newly reconfigured layout to reorient. After each wrong configuration, the operator needs 30 minutes to restore the room to a workable state. The first 30 minutes is disorientation and navigation. It wears on a person. One level of exhaustion per full wrong configuration after the first.

## 3. The Star Chart

### Location and Description

The east wall of the restricted stacks is largely clear of shelves — a deliberate gap, roughly 15 ft wide, left open as a reference station. The star chart is mounted here at eye level, framed in dark wood, behind glass. It is large: approximately 3 ft wide by 4 ft tall, drawn on heavy paper in precise ink, with constellation lines in dark blue and individual stars marked as small circles graded in size by apparent brightness.

The chart shows the full night sky visible from Varenhold, oriented with north at the top. The bottom of the chart is labeled: *Varenhold Sky, as observed from the Archive Tower, Night of the Ritual of Eternal Dawn — Year 203 of the City's Foundation.* Below that, in smaller script: *Commissioned by Archmagister Corven. Drawn by S. Pallart.*

Every constellation on the chart is labeled in common script. Lines connect the component stars. The relative positions of the labeled constellations correspond exactly to the shelf sections around the room — a player who looks from the chart to the plaque labels on the shelves can see the system immediately. The chart is the answer. Corven mounted it on the wall because the answer was never meant to be hidden. The system was meant to be navigable by someone who knew the sky that night.

---

### The Dawnmark Constellation

The Dawnmark is marked on the chart differently from the others. Where every other constellation is in dark blue ink, the Dawnmark is drawn in reddish-brown — the same color as old amber, if amber were rendered in ink. The label reads: **THE DAWNMARK** in slightly larger lettering than the surrounding labels, with a small notation beside it: *Zenith position at hour of first light, Night of the Ritual.*

The constellation has nine stars. Three are significantly larger circles than the others: one on the left, one center, one right. These three are labeled:
- *Vael's Crown* (leftmost, medium-large circle)
- *The Shepherd's Eye* (center, smaller circle — the "pivot")
- *The Trailing Light* (rightmost, largest circle — the brightest)

The annotation beneath these three reads, in Corven's compressed hand: *Rise order, west to east. As above, so the key turns.*

A player reading that annotation and looking at the three-ring mechanism immediately understands the solution. No check required. The chart contains the instruction in plain language. The only barrier is finding and reading it.

---

### Condition of the Chart

The glass is slightly dusty on the outer face. The paper underneath is in good condition — preserved by the sealed room and the Archive's general care. The ink has not faded significantly. The chart is legible at a normal reading distance. A torch or hand-lantern brought close would reveal the finer notation marks without difficulty.

Theron, if present, knows about the chart. If asked, he confirms: *"Corven mounted that himself. On the day he finished the system, before the room was ever restricted. He said the answer should always be in the room."*

## 4. Shelf 4-17-3

### Before the Mechanism is Solved

The 4-17 section is at the north end of the room. Before the correct configuration is set, it presents its back to the players: blank wood, iron brackets, no labels. The rolling ladder sits on the east track but rolls past a featureless surface. There is nothing to reach for. A player who runs a hand along the back of the shelf can feel the document tubes in their slots on the far side, through the thin wood — cylindrical bumps, regular spacing — but cannot access them.

The shelf back has a faint smell: paper and something drier underneath it. Cedar, maybe. The document cases on Corven's personal shelf have been stored with cedar shavings to discourage insects. It is the only shelf in the room that smells this way.

### After the Mechanism is Solved

When the 4-17 section rotates to face the room, it presents a full working face of labeled document slots. The labels are in Corven's hand, organized by date and subject. Most slots hold cylindrical document tubes sealed with wax. A few hold flat map cases. The shelf is full — this section was heavily used before the restriction.

Shelf 4-17-3 is the top row, third section from the left. Seven standard document tubes, one map case, one smaller case set slightly apart. The smaller case is about 10 inches long, 3 inches square in cross-section, sealed with a simple brass clasp rather than wax. On its face, in Corven's handwriting: *Notation. Personal. Do not file separately.*

The document tubes in the row are labeled with dates spanning the last three years before the Ritual. The one immediately to the left of the small case is labeled: *Public Summary — Ritual Architecture and Sympathetic Mechanism — For Archive Record.* This is the public notes document: the one that mentions "distributed sympathetic anchors" and "the living conduit mechanism" without naming what the conduits are or who they are.

The small case beside it holds Fragment 1 of the Notation Key: a single sheet of heavy paper, folded once, covered on both sides in a cipher Corven designed for his personal journals. The cipher is not random — it has a visible internal structure, enough that a player can recognize it as intentional encoding rather than shorthand — but it cannot be read without the other fragments. What is visible without decoding: Corven's name at the top in plain script, a date (three months before the Ritual), and at the bottom of the second side, a single line in plain common: *When you have all three, begin at the first mark.*

## 5. Three Read-Aloud Variants

### A — Players Entering Immediately After the Door Opened

> The room beyond the door is dark at first — not completely, but darker than the stairwell, because the light in here is doing something different. It settles. It sits at a level just above what you need and does not move. The shelves are close together and tall, floor to ceiling, and they run the length of the room in parallel channels. The smell reaches you before your eyes adjust: old paper, cedar somewhere in the back, and under both of those, the specific stillness of a room that has not had to accommodate anyone in a very long time.
>
> The ceiling has a skylight. Through ribbed glass, dim and textured, you can see the sky above Varenhold — and the sky, as it always is, shows stars. Blurred through the glass, scattered, but present. The same sky that has been overhead for fifty years.
>
> At the center of the room, a brass collar wraps the structural column at chest height. Three rings. Each engraved with symbols you can almost read from here. And on the east wall, in a dark wood frame behind glass, something large and carefully drawn: a map of everything above you.

---

### B — Players Who Had to Wait an Hour (Door Locked on Third Attempt)

> The hour has made the silence feel deliberate. The Archive below did not change — there were sounds, the ordinary sounds of a building with clerks in it — but up here, through the door, nothing. When the door finally opens, the room beyond has the quality of something that was waiting.
>
> The shelves are tall and close. The amber light in here is low and sourceless. The dust on the shelf tops is light, even — eleven years of it, undisturbed. The smell is paper and cedar and a stillness that was here before you and will be here after.
>
> The central column's brass collar catches your eye immediately: three rings of engraved symbols at chest height, currently aligned to the same mark on each ring — some default resting position, like hands folded. On the east wall, a large chart behind glass shows a night sky in dark blue and amber-red ink, labeled in common, with three stars in one constellation drawn differently from all the others.
>
> You have had an hour to prepare. The room, at least, is ready to be read.

---

### C — Players Entering with Theron as Guide

> Theron goes in first. Not because he is certain — you can see from the set of his shoulders that he is not — but because this was his room too, once, and the particular discomfort of returning to it is his to carry. He touches the door frame briefly as he crosses it. He does not explain the gesture.
>
> The restricted stacks are long and narrow and darker than the rest of the Archive. The shelves run close together, floor to ceiling, labeled with constellation marks at eye level — small brass plaques, symbol and name in common script. Theron does not explain the system. He moves toward the center of the room and stops at the column with the brass collar.
>
> "He mounted the chart himself," Theron says, without looking at you. He is looking at the east wall, where the star chart hangs behind glass, large and precise, the Dawnmark constellation drawn in reddish-brown where everything else is blue. "On the day he finished the system. Before the restriction. He said —" Theron stops. Starts again. "He said the answer should always be in the room."
>
> He steps back from the column. The three rings are there. The chart is there. The ceiling skylight shows the actual sky — stars visible through ribbed glass, real and overhead, the same stars that have been above this city for fifty years.

## 6. Five Senses — Expanded

### The Restricted Stacks at Rest

**Sight**
- Amber light sitting flat and low across the floor, catching no dust motes because the air has not moved in hours; the shelves casting long lateral shadows between the channels
- Brass constellation plaques at eye level catching the light slightly — the only bright surfaces in the room
- The star chart on the east wall: dark blue ink, one section in reddish-brown, the frame casting a rectangular shadow on the wall behind it
- Through the ribbed skylight glass above: a smear of stars, blurred but persistent, the same stars visible every hour of every day in Varenhold's permanent dusk

**Sound**
- Silence that has texture — not empty, but layered. The faint settling of wood in old brackets. The very distant sound of the Archive below, filtered through the floor and two closed doors, reduced to something below language.
- When a player steps forward: the sound of their boot on stone is precise and slightly too clear, the way sounds are in rooms where nothing else is happening
- The slight creak of a document tube shifting in its slot when someone brushes a shelf — and then the silence that follows it, which is louder than the creak

**Smell**
- Paper: old and dry, the particular flatness of documents that have not been opened recently
- Cedar: faint, coming from the north end of the room — Corven's personal shelf, the only one that was stored with cedar shavings
- Dust: not heavy, not choking — just present, the texture of still air
- Underneath all of it, very faint: something that might be lamp oil from the brass fixture of the mechanism's collar, residual from whenever it was last cleaned

**Touch**
- The ambient cold of a sealed room — not damp, just cool; the Archive is stone throughout and the restricted stacks are the least-used floor
- The smooth face of the brass collar when a hand rests on it: cold metal, worn slightly at the grip points of the rings from previous use, the engraved symbols slightly rough under a fingertip
- The resistance in the rings when a player first tries to turn one — heavier than expected, yielding slowly to sustained pressure

**Taste**
- Dry air. The mineral taste that fills sealed buildings in Varenhold. Old paper has a specific dry-flat quality to it that the air here carries. Nothing sweet. Nothing bitter. The taste of a room that has been closed for eleven years and remembers it.

---

### The Shelves Rotating on a Wrong Answer

**Sight**
- The mid-range tone completes; then, after a pause of about two seconds, the shelves begin to move — smoothly, slowly, the 4-17 section (and two adjacent sections) turning on their floor and ceiling tracks with the calm deliberateness of machinery that has done this before
- The labeled faces rotate out of sight; blank wooden backs come around; the rolling ladder slides on its track to a new resting position; the gap where shelf 4-17-3 was accessible is now a wall of featureless wood
- Two new gaps appear elsewhere in the room — sections that were previously facing away are now forward, labeled, accessible, none of them the right one
- The rotation takes roughly fifteen seconds. Long enough to watch in full. Long enough for it to feel like the room making a considered statement.

**Sound**
- The tone itself: mid-range, clear, resonant, two seconds — then silence
- Then the mechanism engages: a low rumble conducted through the floor rather than the air, felt in the soles of the feet before it is heard; the sound of iron track and shelf bracket and stone all moving together
- The click of brackets resetting at the end of travel; a soft thud as each section reaches its new position; then the rolling ladder sliding on the east track, a soft metallic whisper
- After: silence again. The same silence. As though the room did not notice.

**Smell**
- A faint stir of air as the shelves move — the first air movement in the room since the players entered; it carries a brief concentrated smell of old paper and cedar from the 4-17 section as it passes, and then that faint scent settles again as the room goes still

**Touch**
- The operator feels the mechanism's resistance shift in the final click before the tone sounds — a brief change in texture, a slight easing, as though the ring was briefly uncertain before committing
- Vibration through the floor during the rotation: low, steady, lasting the full fifteen seconds; felt most clearly if a hand is on a shelf

**Taste**
- The stir of air brings a brief taste of cedar and dust — gone before it registers fully

---

### The Moment Shelf 4-17-3 Opens

**Sight**
- The lower tone sounds from the floor; then the pause — three seconds, slightly longer than the failure pause, which a player who has heard a wrong answer will notice
- The 4-17 section rotates forward: labeled faces emerging from shadow, document tubes in their slots becoming visible as the shelf turns, the cedar-scented section coming around to face the room
- The rolling ladder slides into position on the east track, smooth and automatic, as though it knew
- At the top of the accessible face: shelf 4-17-3. Seven document tubes, one map case, one smaller case set slightly apart, its brass clasp catching the amber light. Everything exactly where it was placed, undisturbed, waiting without urgency.
- In the skylight above: the stars through ribbed glass, the same stars Corven charted, present and overhead as they always are

**Sound**
- The lower tone: felt in the floor before it is heard; two or three seconds; then the mechanism engaging — the same rumble, but the destination is different; the room is opening rather than closing
- The ladder on its track
- Then: quiet. The small sound of settled wood. The document tubes in their slots. The particular silence of a question that has been answered.

**Smell**
- Cedar, released as the shelf face turns toward the room — clear and sharp and slightly sweet, the preserving smell of Corven's personal storage; it is the only part of the restricted stacks that smells like something was cared for
- Old paper from the document tubes as they come accessible; the dry flat smell of documents stored in wax-sealed cases

**Touch**
- The floor vibration completing and stopping — the absence of it is more noticeable than its presence was
- The brass clasp of the small case when a hand closes around it: cool, smooth, slightly different from the rest of the room's surfaces — this was handled more carefully than the surrounding materials

**Taste**
- Cedar on the air, brief, as the shelf turns. Then the same dry mineral taste of the room, but something is different in it — the cedar note persists slightly longer than the air stir should carry it, as though the room itself is exhaling something it has held for eleven years.

## 7. What NOT to Describe Yet

**The contents of the public notes document**
The players can see the label clearly once shelf 4-17-3 is open — *Public Summary — Ritual Architecture and Sympathetic Mechanism — For Archive Record.* Do not narrate what it contains until a player picks it up, opens it, and reads it. The phrases "distributed sympathetic anchors" and "the living conduit mechanism" carry weight precisely because the players encounter them themselves, not because the GM read them aloud.

**The meaning of the Notation Key**
The fragment's physical appearance — paper, folded, cipher writing, Corven's name and date in plain script, the instruction at the bottom — can be described in full once a player examines it. What cannot be described yet: what the cipher encodes, what "begin at the first mark" means, how many fragments remain. Fragment 1 is legible as an artifact. Its significance as a key is withheld until Session 2.

**The cipher content**
The plain-text lines on the fragment (Corven's name, the date, *"When you have all three, begin at the first mark"*) can be read by anyone who opens the case. The cipher text filling the rest of the sheet is visible as dense, structured writing that is clearly not shorthand — players may recognize the structure as intentional encoding with a DC 12 Arcana check — but its content is not available yet. Do not hint at what it says.

**The footsteps from the lower floor (if they occur)**
If the party triggers a second wrong configuration, footsteps become audible from below. Do not narrate their source or identity. The sound is: feet on stone stairs, deliberate, unhurried, climbing. Who is coming, from where, and what they will do when they arrive is information the players do not have. Let the footsteps be footsteps.

**The cedar smell's significance**
It tells a careful player that Corven's personal shelf was stored with more care than the surrounding materials — that someone, at some point, took the trouble of cedar preservation. This observation is freely available and can be noted. What it does not tell them yet: why. Do not connect it forward to Corven's habits, his relationship with these documents, or what it means that the cedar smell is still present after eleven years in a sealed room.

**The other shelves in the 4-17 range**
Shelf 4-17-1 and 4-17-2 are accessible if the players get two of three rings correct (partial success). Their contents — earlier drafts of Corven's ritual summaries, administrative correspondence, archive acquisition records — are present and can be examined, but contain nothing that advances the plot this session. Do not narrate their contents unless a player specifically searches them. They are context, not revelation.
