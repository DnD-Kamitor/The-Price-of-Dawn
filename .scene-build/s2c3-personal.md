## Per-Archetype Engagement

Each archetype has a natural entry point into this scene. The box is not a generic puzzle — it is a specific artifact carrying specific weight, and different characters will engage with it from different angles. Give each player something personal to interact with.

---

### The Investigator / Scholar

**Their instinct:** Read the documents first. The colophons are exactly what an investigator looks for — the provenance trail, the witnesses, the administrative record of who was present when. They will find the names before anyone else because finding names in documents is what they do.

**Their specific hook:** The colophon on Bundle 3 has two names. The annotation on Bundle 1 has a third, written in a different ink and slightly rushed. An investigator character will notice this ink discrepancy without a roll. Why was WREN's name added separately? Was she not part of the original witness group? Or was her name added to match the other colophon after the fact?

Ask the player: "Looking at the three names in context — two on Bundle 3, one added separately on Bundle 1 — what does your character read into that arrangement?"

There is no definitive answer. But the question rewards the investigator's attention and may prompt them to ask Theron about Wren specifically. Theron's response: "Wren was present. She signed later — the others couldn't wait for her. I don't know where she went afterward. I've never been able to find a record of her." (This is a Session 3 thread.)

**The anonymous sentence:** This is the investigator's other gift. *"He told me what it meant. I don't think he knew I would write it down."* An investigator will want to know who wrote this. Reward the attention: a DC 16 Investigation confirms it is neither Corven nor any of the three documented witnesses. The handwriting is practical, not scholarly. Someone else was present that night. Someone with enough access to write in Corven's sealed documents — possibly after the sealing, possibly before. The investigator cannot identify the writer in this scene. But they should be asking the question by the time they leave.

### The Cleric / Religious Character

**Their instinct:** The word "release" in the key passage.

> *"The completion requires their release."*

For a religious character — particularly one devoted to any of the six gods in the pantheon — the word "release" has specific weight. In common theological usage, "release" refers to the transition at death: the soul released from the body, the energy released from the vessel. Corven is using it in a technical ritual context. But the theological resonance is exact.

**Their specific hook:** The Cleric hears this passage and understands, immediately, what it means in the language they know. The Dawnborn are not being "freed" in any metaphorical sense. The word in ritual notation means what it means in theology: the living vessel ends, and whatever is inside it passes on.

What does their god say about willing sacrifice? About the relationship between a vessel and what it holds? This is a moment to connect the campaign's central dilemma to the character's faith.

**Ask the player:** "Your character understands 'release' in a way the others might not. Does your character say what they've understood, or sit with it for a moment?"

**Secondary religious hook:** The box itself predates the ritual by thirty years (see s2c3-mechanics.md, DC 14 Arcana note on construction period). It was repurposed. Someone built this box for something else, and Corven adapted it. A Cleric with History or Religion may know that resonance-locked reliquaries of this period were originally made for sacred-object preservation — typically housing relics of holy figures. This box was made to hold something precious and irreplaceable. It still is.

### The Martial Character (Fighter, Ranger, Barbarian, Paladin)

**Their instinct:** There is a door to the study. Someone should be watching it.

**Their specific hook:** Position the martial character at the door — or near the window if they want a view of the street below. While the rest of the party works the puzzle, the martial character is doing the thing they are good at: making sure no one interrupts this. This is not a lesser role. It is the role that makes the rest possible.

**What they hear from the door:** The Archive is silent. Eleven years of a building that has held this secret are now very quiet around them. If the martial character is attentive, they will notice: the street below is also unusually quiet. The Reckoning soldiers who retreated — they went somewhere. They have not come back. Yet.

Ask the player at the door, while the puzzle is being solved: "What do you hear from the street?" They hear nothing. That is the answer. After the combat in Scene 1, the area around the Archive should have some ambient noise — passersby curious about the disturbance, servants from neighboring buildings. There is none. Someone cleared the area. Someone is waiting.

This does not pay off in this scene — it pays off in Scene 5. But the martial character gets to carry that information forward. They noticed what the scholars did not.

**The box as object:** Martial characters often want to try physical approaches. If a player reaches for Thieves' Tools or talks about breaking it: see s2c3-mechanics.md for the full resolution. Do not punish this. The box has a force-open option because some people approach locked objects that way, and that is a valid approach. The consequence (scorched edges, Theron's reaction) is texture, not punishment.

**If they succeed at force-open:** the martial character gets to see Theron's expression — not anger, but grief at evidence of damage to the last thing that mattered. That look is memorable. It tells the martial character something about what this box has meant to Theron without any words being said. Some martial players will respond to that more directly than any dialogue.

### The Dawnborn-Adjacent Character

If any player is playing a Dawnborn character (one of the ten), or a character who has a meaningful personal connection to the Dawnborn (a family member, a protector, someone who has formed a genuine bond with one of the ten in prior sessions), this scene hits differently.

**Their specific hook:** When the key passage is read aloud — when the player hears *"ten living vessels born at the moment of inversion"* — the description is about specific people. Not a historical event. Not an abstract mechanism. People. Someone they know.

The Dawnborn player hears this passage as a description of themselves. Or of someone they love.

Do not call this out mechanically. Do not ask for a saving throw or a check. Ask the player, quietly: "How does your character receive this information in this moment?"

Give them space to react. Do not rush to the next piece of information. The passage ends with "the completion requires their release." The Dawnborn character — or the character who loves a Dawnborn — now knows what that means. Let them sit with that for as long as they need.

**The warmth of the box:** When the Dawnborn character first touches the box, describe the warmth. Then mention: it is the same quality of warmth as — and let the player fill in the comparison. If they have noticed that a Dawnborn NPC runs slightly warm to the touch (this is true of all ten of them; it is a faint trait of the Lux Anchor state), the connection is there. They may not say it aloud at the table. They may save it. Either way, they have it now.

**If the Dawnborn character asks to speak the names into the box:** Yes. Absolutely yes. Let them. The box responds identically. But the act of a Dawnborn speaking CORVEN's name — the name of the man who redirected the ritual into them — into a box sealed by that act has a weight that no other character possesses. If this happens, pause. Describe the indentation's warmth carefully. Give this player the ceremony the scene is designed to have.

### The Charlatan / Social Character

**Their instinct:** Watch Theron, not the documents.

A social character will read the room before they read the colophons. They will see a man standing behind a desk with a box he placed there himself, in a room that smells like one person, waiting. They will notice the shelf gap. They will notice his hands.

**Their specific hook:** Theron is performing control. He has arranged this room, arranged the chairs, arranged himself — and it is all slightly too composed. The Charlatan recognizes this because they have done the same thing: organizing external space to manage internal chaos. The box on the desk is not a neutral act. It is the most important thing he could have done before they arrived, and he did it, and now he is standing there hoping they will do the rest.

DC 12 Insight: Theron is not just waiting. He is relieved. Something about having the players in this room — with the box on the desk, with the scene finally happening — has reduced his anxiety rather than increased it. He was more afraid of another day with the box on the shelf.

**The secondary objective:** The social character is the one most likely to ask Theron why he never opened it himself. Give them the full answer. Let the table hear it through their attention.

**Post-opening:** The social character will also be the one who notices what happens to Theron after the box opens. He stands. He stays at the back of the room. He does not approach. What the social character sees is a man who has handed something over and does not yet know what to do with his hands. This is the most human moment in the scene, and the social character will catch it.
