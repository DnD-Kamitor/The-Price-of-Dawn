22  Session 1 — Into the Dark (Level 4)

Dilemma: Is hope worth chasing when you don’t yet know what it costs?

📖 The Story — What Is Happening & Why

The World

Varenhold has been in permanent twilight for fifty years. A ritual meant to fix the sun in the sky redirected instead — and the ten children born that night became the anchors. The Dawnborn carry the sun’s energy in their bodies. They have been carrying it since birth. They are now adults in their fifties. The energy is still there.

To restore the sun, the ritual must complete. Completing the ritual releases the energy. Releasing the energy means the Dawnborn die.

Grey sickness is spreading. Food stores are failing. Varenhold has three to five good harvests left before the food system collapses. The city is running out of time.

The Job

Chancellor Mira Ostenveld has hired the players to investigate why the Ritual of Eternal Dawn failed fifty years ago. She knows something went wrong. She suspects the answer is terrible. She has arranged not to know it directly — she hired investigators instead of reading the files herself. That is not cowardice. It is strategy. She cannot act on what she doesn’t officially know.

Players take the job before understanding what they’re walking into. That mirrors the Chancellor’s position exactly. The symmetry is intentional.

The Opposition

Someone already knows they’ve been hired. The players haven’t left the Chancellor’s office when the intelligence reaches Brother Edoran, leader of the Restorer movement — a theological faction who believe the Dawnborn’s death would be meaningful, not wasted. Edoran sent people to slow the investigation. Not to kill. To warn. The Restorers had the players’ route before the players knew it themselves.

Keseph Vyne (Warden of the Amber Compact) is the other opposition force. He doesn’t appear this session, but his fingerprints are on the Archive’s suppressed documents. Players won’t know his name until Session 2.

What Session 1 Establishes

By the end of this session, players know: - The ritual used “distributed sympathetic anchors” and a “living conduit mechanism” - Corven built consent into the ritual as an engineering requirement, not a philosophical nicety - Theron Waide (Master Archivist) has been sitting on something for eleven years - Someone tried to stop this investigation — twice, from two different directions

What they don’t know yet: - What “living conduit” means (it means people) - That the Dawnborn are the anchors - That completing the ritual requires the Dawnborn to die - Who Edoran actually is, or why he watched the fight instead of intervening

They’re entering a conversation that has been happening without them for fifty years.

22.2 Opening Beat — The Chancellor’s Office

22.2.1 Scene Setup — Read This Before Running

Where players are coming from: Session 0.5 just ended. Players stopped the Restorer ritual attempt beneath the Dawnhall and survived. They have had whatever short debrief or rest made sense at the end of that session. They do not yet know each other’s names or history (unless they made connections in Session 0.5). They have heard of the grey sickness, been inside a Dawnhall, and encountered Restorer zealots.

What this beat is: A summons. Chancellor Mira Ostenveld has heard what happened beneath the Dawnhall — not all of it, but enough. Word of the stopped ritual reached the Spire Quarter within hours. She has had players identified, located, and summoned. Her aide meets them at a specific address in the Spire Quarter (not the Council chambers — her private office, in a building that could be a law firm). They are not under arrest. They are being offered a job.

How to run this beat: This is a conversation, not a scene with combat or puzzle. Keep it to 15–20 minutes. The Chancellor does most of the talking. Players ask questions. She answers some of them honestly and some of them carefully.

Who the Chancellor is: Mira Ostenveld, Chancellor of Varenhold. Late 50s. Efficient, precise, the kind of person who has been making difficult decisions long enough that she no longer agonises over them in front of people. She is not cold — she is controlled. She has arranged not to know the full truth of what the ritual requires. That is not cowardice. If she officially knows, she must officially act. She has hired investigators instead.

What she tells them:

“Fifty years ago, the Ritual of Eternal Dawn failed. The sun did not return. Grey sickness has been spreading for thirty of those fifty years. Our food stores have three to five good harvests remaining before rationing becomes mandatory.

I need to know why it failed. I need to know if it can be completed. I am not asking you to find a solution — I am asking you to find the truth. Whatever that truth is.

The Civic Archive holds Corven’s original research in a restricted collection on the third floor. Archivist Theron Waide will grant you access with this letter. After the Archive, there is a woman named Sera Voss at the Dawnhall on Crestwick Lane in the Lowmark. She knows the Dawnborn community. She will know you are coming.

I suspect the answer is not easy. That is why I am not reading the files myself.”

She does not say what she suspects the documents contain. She does not explain why she is hiring people who stopped a Restorer ritual last night, instead of established Spire researchers. If asked: “Because Spire researchers have institutional entanglements I cannot control. You do not.”

What players can ask: - What are the Dawnborn? → “Ten people born the night of the ritual. They carry what we understand to be residual ritual energy. They live in Varenhold. Several run or work in the Dawnhalls.” - What do you think went wrong with the ritual? → “I don’t know. That is what I am paying you to find out.” - Why does it matter now? → “The food assessment. Three to five harvests. After that, rationing. After rationing, something I would rather avoid.” - Who else knows about this investigation? → A pause. “My aide and myself. As of this morning.” She does not say what that pause means.

What players know when they leave: - They have a job: find the truth about the ritual - They have a destination: the Civic Archive, then Sera Voss at the Dawnhall - They have a letter of introduction in the Chancellor’s handwriting - They do not yet know that someone already knows about the investigation

Transition to Scene 1: Players leave the Chancellor’s building heading south toward the Spire Quarter’s outer edge, then down into the Lowmark toward the Archive. The walk takes fifteen to twenty minutes. The Lowmark Junction is on the route. The food queue is there. So are the zealots.

22.3 Scene 1 — Opening Combat: The Restorer Ambush

Coming from the Chancellor’s office. Walking to the Archive. They cross the Lowmark Junction — ambush is waiting.

Players know: hired to investigate the ritual, heading to the Archive, letter of introduction, Sera Voss is a contact. Players don’t know: anyone is watching them.

Open with: describe the walk south from the Spire Quarter — amber twilight, streets narrowing, food queue ahead. Then pick a read-aloud below. Fight starts on initiative.

🎯 Scene Purpose, Peak Moment & GM Notes

PURPOSE STATEMENT: This scene exists to establish that someone already knows what the players are doing — and has decided to stop it before it starts. The ambush is not about violence; it is about the weight of organised opposition, and about planting the first seed of doubt: the people trying to stop this investigation may not be wrong.

EMOTIONAL TARGET: Players should feel watched — not just in this scene, but retroactively. The sense that they were already being tracked before they even started should linger past the fight and into the Archive.

DRAMATIC QUESTION: Will you take the thing that tells you who sent them, or will you let it burn?

SURPRISE: The Enforcer does not want to hurt anyone. He’s good at his job, which is intimidation, and intimidation only works if the subject stays alive. Players expecting a murder attempt find instead a coordinated warning delivered by professionals — which is more frightening, not less, because it implies resources, advance knowledge, and restraint. The Restorers had the players’ route before the players left the Chancellor’s office.

DESIGNED PEAK MOMENT: The note-burning attempt.

→ Round 2 of combat has begun. The players are mid-fight, the crowd is scattering, and the zealots are pressing hard.

→ GM narration (delivered flat, mid-round, before the zealot’s turn): “The zealot nearest the market crate crouches without breaking eye contact with you — and holds the folded paper to the lantern flame.”

→ The players must choose, right now, between the fight in front of them and the intelligence burning in the corner. They cannot do both at full effectiveness. Whatever they sacrifice to save the note — or fail to save — defines how much they learn about Edoran this session.

This is what players quote after the session: “The one with the note just crouched down and held it to the flame like he had all the time in the world.”

PREP vs IMPROVISE:

Must prep: - The note’s physical description (sealed, belt pouch, visibly present before round 1 — plant it in the scene-opening narration) - The Enforcer’s two-line yield speech (silence, then hands up — he has been instructed to say nothing, so he says nothing; this is more unnerving than a speech) - Edoran’s exact position in the alley and his sightlines (30 ft, south alley mouth, can see the whole junction — confirm this on your map before play)

Safe to improvise: - How many civilians catch a stray elbow and react (crowd chaos is texture, not plot) - The zealots’ individual fighting styles and banter (they’re cultist stat blocks — make them feel fervent, not tactical) - Whether the queue disperses into the Archive side-street or the Lowmark market (either works for Scene 2 transition)

🌍 Environment & Setup

1. Tactical Map Description

The Lowmark Junction — ~50×50 ft

A cramped four-way street junction in the lower residential Lowmark, roughly 50 ft across where two cobblestone lanes intersect. The streets are older than the rest of the city and show it: worn stone, iron lantern posts bolted into the corners of buildings, second-story washing lines overhead. The permanent amber twilight is thickest here — lamplight pools low on the cobbles and does not reach the upper storeys.

2. Three Read-Aloud Variants

A — Cautious (Party scanning, moving carefully)

The junction is busier than it should be at this hour. The food queue stretches from the distribution cart all the way to the east wall — fifteen people, maybe more, heads down, breath fogging in the amber half-light. You’re tracking the lanterns, the exits, the shadows, which is why you see it first: four figures who are not in the queue, not moving toward it, not doing anything a person in the Lowmark has any reason to do. They are waiting. They are waiting specifically for you.

B — Rushing (Party moving fast, focused on the Archive)

The quickest route through the junction cuts past the food queue — you can see the distribution cart, the crates, the north lane that leads straight up toward the Archive. You’re three steps into the intersection when you hear it: boots on cobblestone, fast and deliberate, from three directions at once. The crowd in the queue goes still. Someone grabs a child by the arm. A figure in the center of the north lane spreads his hands slowly, palms out, blocking the road. He is not asking.

C — Split Party (Party spread across the junction)

The junction opens in front of you — some of you near the distribution cart, some cutting wide around the queue’s end, someone already past the crates on the eastern side. Which is why you all notice it at slightly different moments. The stillness at the west alley mouth. The man near the lantern post who is not looking at the queue. The figure in the center of the road north who has been there since before you arrived. The gap closes differently for each of you. There is nowhere convenient to regroup.

3. Five Senses — Expanded

Before the Ambush: The Mundane Scene

Sight - Amber lanternlight lying flat on wet cobblestones, stretching long shadows from the cart wheels and the queue’s shuffling feet - Bundled figures in the food line — coats, scarves, the particular hunched patience of people who have been waiting in cold air for a long time - The NE lantern post flame moving slightly in a draft from the east alley, catching the metallic clasp of a belt pouch on a figure not standing in the queue

Sound - Low murmur from the line — not conversation exactly, more the sound of people keeping quiet together - A vendor’s voice calling quantities: “One allocation per name — show your registry mark at the cart” - Cobblestone echo from the north lane carrying traffic noise from the wider city somewhere beyond

Smell - Lamp oil, thick and slightly sweet — the particular smell of amber lantern fuel that is everywhere in the Lowmark - Old bread and something that might be preserved root vegetables from the distribution cart - River cold from the lower streets, carrying the iron smell that clings to the Lowmark in all seasons

Touch - Cold air on exposed skin — the permanent damp of a city at artificial twilight, never fully warm - Cobblestones underfoot, worn smooth in the center, rough at the edges where cart wheels don’t reach - The slight press of passing foot traffic from the queue on the players’ south side as they enter

Taste - Dust and cold air, the particular mineral dryness that fills every sealed building in Varenhold — the taste of a city that has been indoors for fifty years, even when you’re outside

The Moment It Starts: The Threat Shift

Sight - The queue goes still — not all at once, but like a wave passing through the line, people stopping mid-shuffle — because the people not in the queue are moving - The figure at the north lane spreads his hands: no weapon visible, which is worse than a weapon - A glint at the NE lantern post as the note-carrier’s hand drops to his belt pouch

Sound - The vendor voice cuts off mid-call - Boot leather on cobblestone — fast and deliberate — from west, south, and north simultaneously; the sound of people who know where they’re going - A child in the queue says something; the parent pulls them close and says nothing

Smell - Sharp sweat, the kind that comes from bodies moving fast through cold air — the sweat of people doing something they have been waiting to do - A trace of torch oil, different from lamp oil — the zealots carry their own light

Touch - The food queue crowd closes behind the players as the south zealot moves up, not aggressively but completely: there is nowhere to back into - The cold air on the back of the neck that says every exit has something in front of it

Taste - The dry metal taste of adrenaline, and underneath it, the same dust — the city always tastes like this; the difference is now you notice

4. Pre-Scene Setup

What Players Know (Coming from the Chancellor’s Briefing)

  • They have been hired by Chancellor Ostenveld to investigate the Ritual of Eternal Dawn — specifically, whether restoration is possible and what it would cost
  • They are heading to the Varenhold Civic Archive in the Spire Quarter, which holds Archmagister Corven’s documentation
  • They have the name Theron Waide — the Master Archivist — as their contact
  • The commission is meant to be low-profile; the Chancellor has not publicized it

What Players Are Looking For

  • Corven’s original ritual documents (or access to them via Theron Waide)
  • Any physical evidence of what the ritual actually did and whether it can be undone
  • The Archive is their next immediate destination — they are crossing through the Lowmark to reach it

What Players Do NOT Know

  • Someone already knows their route
  • Edoran exists, knows who they are, and has sent people to intercept them
  • The Restorers have any operational capability in the Lowmark at street level
  • The note exists; the note contains Edoran’s handwriting; Edoran is in the alley watching

What Should Be Visible Before Combat Triggers

No roll required: - The sealed note in Z4’s belt pouch — describe it as a rolled document with a dark wax seal, carried in a visible open-top pouch on Z4’s belt. Any player who scans the group before the fight starts can see it without a roll. - The group arrangement itself: four people who are not in the queue, positioned at exits. A DC 10 Perception (passive) notices this feels wrong. DC 12 registers “these are exits.”

DC 15 Perception (active, or passive 15+): - A figure 30 ft into the east alley, not moving, watching the junction. Difficult to see because they are standing completely still in shadow and the players are not expecting a watcher. Description if spotted: “A person in a plain dark coat, hood up, standing with the particular stillness of someone who is not lost. They have been there for some time.” Edoran does not react to being noticed. He watches.

5. Cinematic Framing

GM Opening Sentence (Before Read-Aloud)

Deliver this aloud before reading any variant:

“You’re one junction from the Archive road. The Lowmark is doing what it always does at this hour — feeding itself, slowly, in the amber half-dark. Let me tell you what you see as you turn the corner.”

Then deliver the appropriate read-aloud variant based on the party’s approach.

What NOT to Describe Yet

Do not mention until the relevant moment occurs:

  • Edoran — not unless a player makes the DC 15 Perception check or actively looks into the east alley. He is the scene’s secret. Describing him too early collapses a reveal that pays off in Scene 5.
  • The scripture on the note — the note is visible, but its contents (“The willing heart gives freely what the bound heart cannot”) are only revealed when a player reads it after recovery. Do not describe the inscription in any opening pass.
  • The note-burn mechanic — do not announce the secondary objective; let players discover it when Z4 starts moving toward the lantern post at the start of round 2. The movement telegraphs intent without explanation.
  • The Enforcer’s origin or name — he will not say who sent him, even if captured. Do not give him identifying details that feel like a faction marker. He is professional and silent and that is the point.
  • The exit route zealots take — they scatter into the food queue crowd when the Enforcer goes to half HP. Do not describe where they go; let them simply be absorbed by the crowd, which is its own statement about how the Restorers operate in the Lowmark.

The Scene’s Core Tension

This is not a fight about survival. The players are almost certainly going to win. The fight is about:

  1. The note — can they get it before it burns?
  2. Edoran — is anyone watching the alleys?
  3. The crowd — how do they handle a public ambush in front of fifteen hungry civilians?

Players who engage all three layers leave this scene with a richer picture than players who only fight. Reward attention over aggression.

DC 15 The group of five is moving against the flow of the queue, not with it
DC 17 The Enforcer’s chain mail creates a faint sound under his coat at every stride
Automatic Any player who explicitly says they are watching the crowd before the scene description ends

If surprise is broken, the players act on the first round. If not broken, the Restorers get a free Round 0 before initiative.

A player must: 1. Reach Zealot A — requires sufficient movement to close the distance this turn (or the previous turn if repositioning). On a zealot success: the paper corner catches. The player may attempt one more contest next turn with disadvantage (the note is partially burning). On a second failure, the note is destroyed.

1. Restorer Enforcer

Role in Scene: Field commander. He controls the tempo — when it escalates, when it stops.

Stats: Use Veteran (MM p.350). HP 52. Does not draw weapons until initiative is rolled.

Physical Description

40s. Stocky. Jaw scar, old dock dispute, healed badly. He stands slightly sideways — the posture of someone who has managed crowds, not charged them. Weapons are present but not visible until he needs them. He moves the way furniture moves: deliberate, unhurried, occupying space without announcing it.

OGAS

Occupation Restorer field commander. Former city guard, fifteen years service. Resigned when the administration cut district patrols at the peak of the grey sickness outbreak. His district paid for that decision in bodies.
Goal Execute Edoran’s order exactly: deliver a warning serious enough to end this investigation, without causing a death that brings the city guard down on the Restorers. It must be surgical. Clean. The players must leave this junction shaken, not martyred.
Attitude Professional contempt — not personal. These people are meddlers who don’t yet understand what they are meddling in. He doesn’t hate them. He was one of them once, before his wife died of grey sickness in Year 42. He watched the district heal centers fill up and the patrol routes disappear, and he drew a conclusion about where loyalty belongs.
Secret He does not fully believe Edoran’s theological position. He follows Edoran because grief demanded a direction and Edoran provided one. Loyalty, not faith. If asked — really asked — what he personally believes about the Dawnborn, he cannot articulate an answer. He has not tried. The question feels dangerous to look at directly.

Voice

Measured. Economical. Deliberate silences where other people would fill space with words. He says the exact thing he means to say, nothing more, and then he stops talking.

Dialogue

Opening line (before initiative, blocking the players’ path):

“You should go home. Whatever you were hired to find — it’s not worth finding.”

During combat — professional, never personal:

“Stand down and walk away. Last time I’ll say it cleanly.”

“You’re making this harder than it needs to be.”

“I’ve broken up worse than this. Don’t make me prove it.”

If captured (grappled/restrained):

Complete silence. Name: nothing. Who sent him: nothing. He holds eye contact and waits. He is not afraid. He is calculating whether this silence is still the correct tactical position.

GM note: This silence is itself information. A player who makes Insight DC 12 reads not defiance — patience. He has been in custody before. He knows how this goes. He is waiting for the players to decide what kind of people they are.

If players open non-violently before initiative:

He pauses. A genuine pause — something lands differently than expected. Then:

“You want to talk? Then talk. You have until my patience runs out.”

He means it. He will listen for up to 30 seconds of real table time before the situation resolves or deteriorates. He will not confirm who sent him. He will not confirm the Restorers. But he will listen, and that listening is visible to anyone watching.

Attitude Shift Triggers

Reduces hostility (do not escalate; he may choose to let them go): - Players mention Edoran with any genuine respect, rather than as a name to leverage - Players demonstrate they understand the grey sickness is personal, not abstract — that they know who has been dying - Players make clear they are not instruments of the Chancellor’s administration — that they have their own stake in the truth - Players ask about his scar, his time in the guard, or show any curiosity about him as a person rather than an obstacle

Increases hostility (escalates immediately; combat resumes if paused): - Players threaten people in the food queue or use bystanders as leverage - Players taunt about the ritual, the Dawnborn, or the sun — anything that sounds like mockery of grief - Players use the names of the Dawnborn dismissively, as if they are objects rather than people - Players invoke the Chancellor’s authority as a shield

2. Restorer Zealots (x4)

Stats: Use Cultist (MM p.345) for all four. HP 9 each. They fight with the flinching energy of people who have talked themselves into this.

The Archetype

These are not soldiers. They are believers — and between them, they carry enough grief to fill a room. A child lost. A parent lost. Two siblings. Edoran gave that grief a direction and a name, and they followed it here because following it feels better than sitting with it.

They are two women, two men. Ages 20s to 40s.

Occupations: - Dockworker, early 30s, man. Lost his younger brother to grey sickness last winter. Still has the habit of leaving food portions in twos. - Baker, late 40s, woman. Lost her mother at the grey sickness peak in Year 42. Has attended every Restorer meeting for three years. Has never missed one. - Former Spire Quarter clerk, late 20s, woman. Lost her position when the Spire cut administrative staff. Found the Restorers six months later. The theology was secondary; the community was not. - Neighborhood healer’s assistant, early 20s, man. The youngest. Has not lost anyone yet. Joined because his mentor did. Is here because he did not know how to say no to someone he admired.

Body Language in Combat

Nervous energy. They hold weapons like people who practiced the grip but have rarely needed it. Their footwork is too wide. They breathe audibly. They fight like people who have made themselves do something that frightens them — which is exactly what they are doing.

Identifying mark: A small sun-disk is stitched inside the collar of each zealot’s coat. It is not visible at a distance. In a grapple, or if a collar opens during struggle, it becomes visible. Religion DC 12 identifies it as a Restorer devotional symbol — the Wounded faction’s version of the Auris sun, stitched in grief-thread (undyed linen, the traditional mourning fiber).

If isolated and spoken to:

“We’re trying to save everyone. You don’t understand what’s at stake.”

They will not elaborate. Not because they have been told not to — because they are afraid of how much they themselves don’t know. They believe. They are not certain. The gap between those two things is visible if anyone looks for it (Insight DC 10).

The youngest zealot (healer’s assistant):

Before the first attack — before initiative is even rolled, in the moment of stillness right before movement — he flinches. Not toward. Away. It is a small motion, barely a shift of weight, but it is visible.

Insight DC 10 reads it correctly: this is grief, not violence. He is here because grief organized itself into this moment and brought him with it. He has not been in a fight. He is terrified. He will fight anyway, because that is what the others are doing, and because he has nowhere else to put what he is carrying.

GM note: If a player speaks directly to him before or during combat — not at the group, at him specifically — he hesitates for one round. He does not defect. But he hesitates, and that hesitation is real.

3. Brother Edoran — Alley Profile (Scene 1 Only)

Full OGAS in npcs.md. This entry covers Scene 1 only: his presence, what he is doing, and what happens if reached.

Position: 30 feet away, at the mouth of a side alley off the east end of the junction. Not hidden. Not moving. Standing in the open in plain wool coat, hood up against the cold.

Physical

60s. The coat is worn at the elbows. He stands the way a person stands who has been waiting a very long time and has stopped expecting anything to arrive on schedule. He is not watching the fight. He is watching the players.

What He Is Doing

Calibrating. He wants to know what kind of moral agents the players are. He is not assessing whether they are a threat — he has already decided they are not a serious physical threat. He is watching whether they fight immediately, try to negotiate, pursue him, or do something he did not anticipate. He finds the unanticipated interesting.

He does not move toward the fight. He does not intervene. He does not signal the Enforcer. This is not detachment — it is the particular stillness of someone who has already done everything he could do before arriving here, and now has to watch and see whether it was enough.

If Reached

A player who breaks away from the combat and crosses 30 feet to the alley reaches him. He does not run. He turns to face whoever arrives. He looks at them for a moment — a genuine look, taking stock — and then he speaks:

“You have no idea what you are looking for. When you find it, you will understand why I did not want you to.”

Delivery notes for the GM: This is not a threat. It is not a warning in any tactical sense. It is a statement of fact from someone who has already walked the road the players are about to walk, and who knows where it goes, and who sent men to stop them anyway — not because he thinks they can be stopped, but because he felt obligated to try. There is no menace in it. There is something close to sorrow.

He does not answer follow-up questions. He does not give his name. He does not confirm the Restorers. He takes one step back into the alley, and then he is gone — not with urgency, not running, just gone the way someone is gone when they knew the exact moment to leave.

What he does NOT do: Threaten. Explain. Justify. Linger.

2. Terrain Mechanics

Food Queue Crowd (~50 people, north side of junction)

Baseline state (Round 1): - The crowd occupies a 20 ft × 40 ft zone along the north street edge, tightest near the distribution cart. - Difficult terrain for anyone moving through it. - Creatures inside the crowd zone gain half cover (+2 AC, +2 Dexterity saving throws) from ranged attacks originating outside it.

Density zones: - Within 10 ft of the distribution cart: heavy difficult terrain (every foot costs 3 ft of movement). AC bonus applies to attackers outside this zone. - Street edges and alley mouths: lighter crowd; treat as standard difficult terrain only.

Round 2 trigger (Initiative Count 20, start of Round 2): The crowd breaks. Civilians scatter in all directions — move them off the map. Crowd cover is gone. The junction becomes sparse difficult terrain (overturned baskets, dropped packs, scattered grain) rather than dense crowd. GM may place 1d4 prone civilians in random squares as obstacles.

Civilian injuries: - Each civilian is AC 10, 4 HP. - If a player’s AoE or attack strikes a civilian, note it. Two or more civilian casualties triggers a City Watch investigation within 24 hours (see Section 4D below).

Market Crates (east end of junction, stacked 3–4 high)

Default state: Half cover (+2 AC, +2 Dex saves) for creatures crouching behind them.

Upending a crate (Action): - DC 10 Athletics. - Success: crate falls; creates a 5 ft radius difficult terrain patch at the landing square. Creatures in the path make a DC 12 Dexterity saving throw or take 1d6 bludgeoning and are knocked prone. - Failure: crate shifts but does not fall; no terrain change.

Improvised weapon (pushing a crate onto an adjacent creature): - DC 12 Athletics check (not an attack roll). - Success: target takes 2d6 bludgeoning and must succeed on a DC 13 Strength saving throw or be knocked prone. - Failure: crate tips but target steps clear — no damage, but crate now counts as half cover for them.

Lantern Posts (spaced ~15 ft along street edges, 6 ft tall iron posts, open-flame oil lamps)

Passive hazard: - Any creature pushed, shoved, or knocked into a lantern post must succeed on a DC 12 Acrobatics saving throw or take 1d6 fire damage as the lamp tips and splashes burning oil. - The lantern post itself is AC 13, HP 8; destroying it removes the hazard and plunges a 15 ft radius into dim light.

The burn zone: - A zealot holding the note who is adjacent to a lantern post has an opportunity action available on their turn: hold the note to the flame (no action cost if already adjacent; uses their Action if they must move to reach it). - Trigger condition: Round 2 or later, note still in zealot’s possession. - Contesting the burn: a player within reach can use their Reaction to make an Athletics or Dexterity (Acrobatics) check (DC 13) to knock the zealot’s hand aside before the note catches. - If no player contests: note ignites at end of zealot’s turn. It burns to ash by end of next turn unless smothered (DC 10 Dexterity as a bonus action while adjacent).

Distribution Cart (centre of junction, north edge)

Object stats: Large object, AC 11, HP 27, immune to psychic and poison damage.

As blocking terrain: Blocks movement and line of sight for Medium and smaller creatures crouching behind it. Standing creatures are visible above the cart bed.

Pushing the cart (Action): - DC 15 Athletics. - Success: cart moves 10 ft in a straight line. Any creature in its path must make a DC 13 Strength saving throw or be knocked prone and take 1d8 bludgeoning. - Failure: cart shifts 2 ft; no terrain change. - The cart cannot be pushed through walls or other Large objects.

East Alley Mouth (10 ft wide, runs east from junction)

Lighting: Dim light (vs. amber street lanterns). Perception checks made from the street into the alley have disadvantage.

Edoran’s position: 30 ft into the alley from the junction mouth. He does not move toward the street under any circumstance.

Time window: Edoran waits until end of Round 2. If no player has entered the alley by then, he takes a step back into the darkness and is gone. Players who move into the alley on Round 1 or early Round 2 can reach him before he withdraws.

Dim light in the alley: Creatures inside the alley making Perception checks back toward the lit street have advantage (bright light behind the subjects).

3. Crowd Mechanics (Detailed)

Pushing Through

  • Check: DC 12 Athletics or Acrobatics (player’s choice).
  • Success: Move through the crowd zone at half speed (difficult terrain, every foot costs 2 ft). The crowd parts grudgingly.
  • Failure: The crowd presses back. No movement through the crowd zone this turn. The player can still move in open areas outside the crowd zone.
  • Special: A player who declares they are forcing through (not navigating) can push through automatically but displaces 1d3 civilians who stumble — roll to see if any are in a hazard square.

Crowd as Cover

  • A player who moves into the crowd zone and stays still (does not attack) may attempt to blend in.
  • Check: DC 13 Stealth, rolled with disadvantage (active combat, enemies are actively searching).
  • Success: Player gains half cover from ranged attacks this round. Enemies must guess which figure in the crowd is the player; melee attacks against the player have disadvantage until the player attacks or casts a spell.
  • Failure: No benefit. The player is conspicuous.

AoE Spells / Indiscriminate Force Into Crowd

  • If a spell or effect’s area of effect includes crowd squares:
    • Each civilian in the blast is AC 10, 4 HP. GM resolves their saves normally.
    • On any civilian being reduced to 0 HP: one civilian is visibly injured (screaming, down). Track this.
    • Two or more civilians at 0 HP: witnesses flee and report to City Watch. Investigation arrives in 1d4 hours.
    • Player-character reputation: the Dawnhall, Sera Voss, and any Lowmark faction contact hears about this within the session.

4. Creative Play Responses

A. “I try to negotiate before any combat starts”

Check: Persuasion DC 16 (Enforcer is under orders; persuasion is fighting his chain of command, not his personal feelings).

Approach matters: - Threatening: disadvantage on the roll. - Appealing to his fear (requires DC 13 Insight first, revealing he’s afraid not angry): advantage on the roll. - Offering to leave the district immediately and abandon the investigation: automatic success — the Enforcer signals a hold. The zealots stay blocking. Players get 2 minutes to leave before the hold breaks.

Success: Enforcer holds position; says nothing. A tense standoff. He gives players 1 minute to leave the district. If they leave, he withdraws. If they don’t, combat starts on his initiative.

Failure: Enforcer shakes his head and signals his people. Roll initiative. Players who attempted negotiation have already declared their position; they do not get a free action before Round 1.

B. “I run directly for Edoran without fighting”

No check required to run. Movement is automatic. But:

  • The crowd is difficult terrain (costs double movement, or DC 12 Athletics/Acrobatics to push through at full speed).
  • Two zealots will use their Reactions to make opportunity attacks if the running player passes within 5 ft.
  • The Enforcer may use his turn to Dash and grapple the running player (Athletics +3 vs. player’s contested roll).

Reaching the alley (30 ft to Edoran): - Player needs ~30 ft of movement after clearing the crowd zone. With difficult terrain, a 30 ft speed character needs 2 turns minimum unless they succeed on the crowd push check. - A player with 35–40 ft speed who clears the crowd with a successful DC 12 check can reach Edoran in 1 round.

What happens: Edoran does not flee immediately. He says: “You have no idea what you are looking for. When you find it, you will understand why I did not want you to.” Then he steps back into the dark. He cannot be grappled or detained — if a player attempts it, he simply is not there when they reach out. He is already gone.

C. “I try to turn the crowd against the zealots”

Check: Persuasion or Performance DC 14, rolled with disadvantage (the crowd is frightened, not angry; they want to flee, not fight).

Success: 1d6+2 civilians throw debris (sacks, baskets, produce) at the nearest zealot. Each hit deals 1 damage and imposes disadvantage on that zealot’s next attack roll. The Enforcer turns his attention to crowd control for 1 round (loses his Action).

Failure: The crowd starts to panic early — treat Round 2 crowd scatter as happening at the end of Round 1 instead. The early scatter removes cover and exposes civilian AoE risk sooner.

Critical success (rolled 5+ above DC): A civilian with some physical presence (broad shoulders, dock worker build) physically grabs a zealot and bear-hugs them — the zealot is grappled until it succeeds on a DC 10 Athletics check on its turn.

D. “I cast an area spell into the crowd / use indiscriminate force”

No check — this just happens. Resolve the spell normally.

Civilian consequences: Per Section 3. Two civilian casualties = Watch investigation. Three or more = the scene ends with players fleeing the district, not completing it cleanly.

Faction consequences: - Sera Voss hears. Scene 4 (the Dawnhall) begins with her standing with her arms crossed. She still talks to them. She is not warm. - The Chancellor hears. She does not comment. She files it.

Combat consequence: The Enforcer will not yield to players who harmed civilians. Persuasion to end the fight becomes DC 20 for the rest of the scene.

E. “I try to knock out all zealots non-lethally”

Rules: Melee attacks only. When a melee attack would drop a creature to 0 HP, the player declares non-lethal. The creature is unconscious, not dead. Ranged attacks and spells (without specific “non-lethal” flavour) cannot be declared non-lethal under 2014 rules without GM agreement.

Practical note: Cultists have 9 HP each; they drop fast. The Enforcer has 52 HP — non-lethal is viable but he fights to half HP before the surrender trigger anyway.

Restraint check: Each non-lethal KO costs a bonus action to secure (tie hands, etc.). If players don’t secure them, unconscious zealots regain consciousness in 1d4 minutes (1 HP) and may flee or alert others.

Reward: Players who knock out all zealots non-lethally and secure them can interrogate. The zealots know: they were paid, not devout (despite the pins); the Enforcer is the only one with a name to give (he won’t); they met their contact in the Ashring, not the Lowmark.

F. “I try to intimidate everyone into leaving immediately”

Check: Intimidation DC 18 to affect the Enforcer (he’s seen worse). Cultists break at DC 13 — they’re hired, not fanatics.

Split resolution: Roll once. On a 13–17, the zealots flinch and hesitate (disadvantage on their next attack), but the Enforcer holds and they hold with him. On an 18+, the Enforcer takes a half step back; the zealots break and run; the Enforcer fights alone (reducing the combat to 1v party).

Failure (below 13): The Enforcer smiles, briefly. His people step forward. Roll initiative with players at disadvantage on their first initiative check (they overplayed their hand).

G. “I try to bribe the Enforcer”

No check required — the offer is made. But:

  • If the bribe is less than 50 gp: Enforcer ignores it. Proceed to check.
  • If 50–100 gp: DC 16 Persuasion to make the bribe work (he’s taking a professional risk).
  • If 100+ gp: DC 12 Persuasion (the money is real and significant to him).

Success: Enforcer pockets the coin without acknowledgment. He calls a tactical retreat — “nothing to be done here.” Zealots withdraw. No combat.

Failure: Enforcer glances at the coin. Does not take it. Says: “Put that away before someone else sees it.” He does not elaborate. This is not a refusal on principle — it is a refusal of this bribe in this public place. A private approach (alley, later, DC 12 Persuasion) might work. The current scene proceeds to combat.

H. “I try to grab a zealot as MY hostage / use them as leverage”

Check to grab: Athletics or the Grapple action (contested Athletics, zealot +2). On success, player has zealot grappled.

Check to use as leverage against Enforcer: Intimidation DC 15 (Enforcer is calculating whether this zealot is worth more than his contract).

Success (Intimidation 15+): Enforcer freezes. Standoff. He has 1 round to decide. If player maintains grapple and holds position, Enforcer signals withdrawal at the start of his next turn. He says nothing.

Failure (Intimidation below 15): Enforcer gestures to the remaining zealots. They move to flank the player with the grappled zealot — they are going to take their colleague back. The grappled zealot fights to break free (Athletics +2 on its turn).

Special case: If the player explicitly threatens to kill the hostage and the Enforcer believes them (DC 14 Intimidation with a credible weapon at the zealot’s throat): automatic withdrawal. But word travels — the players threatened to murder a captive in the Lowmark. Someone saw.

5. Rulings Cheat Sheet

Most Likely Edge Cases at the Table

1. “Does the crowd count as cover for the zealots / Enforcer too?”

Yes, while they are inside the crowd zone. Half cover for everyone, both sides. Once the crowd scatters (Round 2 trigger), cover is gone for all. The GM does not need to track individual crowd figures — zones are enough.

2. “Can I delay my turn to contest the note burn?”

Yes, but only if the player has already declared before the zealot’s turn that they are readying an action to intercept. A Readied Action (DC 13 Athletics or Acrobatics as the Reaction trigger) is the correct mechanic. Players cannot retroactively contest after seeing the zealot succeed — they must declare the ready before the zealot acts.

3. “I want to shove the Enforcer into a lantern post.”

Shove action: Athletics vs. Enforcer’s Athletics or Acrobatics (Veteran Athletics +5, Acrobatics +2 — player rolls against whichever is better, so +5). On success, Enforcer moves 5 ft in a straight line. If that line ends at a lantern post square, the GM calls a DC 12 Acrobatics save for the Enforcer or he takes 1d6 fire. The shove itself requires the player to be within 5 ft and uses their Attack action (or one attack if multi-attack).

4. “The zealot dropped the note — it fell on the ground. Who owns initiative on picking it up?”

The note is a loose object on the ground. Picking it up costs a free object interaction (no action required) on a player’s turn if they are in or adjacent to the square it occupies. If two creatures want the same object, the one whose turn comes first gets it — no contested roll needed. If both are within reach on the same initiative count, the player wins the tie (friendly GM ruling; players are protagonists).

5. “Can I use magic to solve the crowd problem non-violently?” (Charm Person, Sleep, Calm Emotions, etc.)

Charm Person / Calm Emotions: Valid. Each zealot is a valid target (humanoid). The Enforcer has Veteran stats, which include no special save bonuses beyond Wisdom (modifier unknown — treat as +1 Wisdom for MM Veteran). The DC is the caster’s spell save DC (typical Gestalt L3: 13–14). On success against the Enforcer, he is charmed — he will not attack but also will not order a withdrawal (the charm makes him friendly, not obedient). This effectively ends the threat while leaving the Enforcer present and awkward. DC 13 Insight for other zealots to notice the Enforcer’s behaviour has changed — if they notice, they may break and run on their own.

Sleep: Cultists (9 HP) go down easily. The Enforcer (52 HP) is almost certainly above the Sleep pool at L3 — he will not be affected unless he is already heavily wounded. Casting Sleep into the crowd risks civilians — GM applies the civilian HP rules per Section 3 for civilians caught in the burst radius.

GM Craft

1. Guy Sanders Techniques — Applied to This Scene

SECONDARY OBJECTIVE: How to Make the Note Feel Urgent Without Explaining It

Do not tell players the note is important. Show them the zealot is protecting it.

Delivery technique — pre-initiative, in the scene-opening narration:

“One of them — the shortest, nearest the crates — has a sealed packet tucked visibly into his belt. He clocks your eyes on it. His hand moves across it once, a small deliberate gesture, then drops back to his side.”

That gesture is everything. It tells players: he knows you noticed. It tells them: he is prepared to do something about it. It creates a question — what is in that packet, and why does he care whether you see it? — without answering it.

Then, in round 2, the burning attempt answers the question by threatening to remove it permanently. The urgency is retrospective: the moment the flame appears, the pre-initiative gesture snaps into meaning.

Do not name the note. Do not say “clue.” Say only what a character in the scene would see.

FIVE SENSES — Timing

Sense Content When to Deliver
Sight Amber lanternlight on cobblestones, food queue stretching the block, bundled figures, the zealots cutting through the crowd with deliberate speed Pre-initiative — first paragraph of scene narration, before any roll is called
Sound The queue’s low murmur, a vendor calling quantities, then boots on stone — fast, deliberate, wrong for the context Pre-initiative — second sentence; the sound shift is what signals threat before sight confirms it
Smell Lamp oil and old bread from the distribution cart; the sharper sweat of bodies moving to cut off exits Round 1, after initiative — give this as the crowd compresses. It arrives with the physical press of the fight
Touch The crowd closing behind players, no clean retreat route Round 1 — narrate after first enemy move, when players feel the tactical trap. “The queue has thickened behind you — there’s nowhere to back into.”
Taste Dust and cold air, the dryness of a city that hasn’t had open sky in fifty years Round 2 or transition out — this one is atmospheric and elegiac, not tactical. Use it as the fight peaks or as the scene closes. It reminds players what is at stake beneath the brawl

Rule: Sight and sound belong to the approach. Smell and touch belong to the fight. Taste belongs to the pause after.

NPC QUICK REF — The Enforcer

WANTS: To complete this job without injury to his people or the targets — a clean warning, nothing that brings the City Guard down on the Restorers tonight.

FEARS: That one of the zealots loses nerve and actually hurts someone, escalating this into something Edoran can’t contain.

LIES: That he is simply a hired enforcer following instructions he doesn’t understand. He has read the note. He knows what Edoran is asking. He believes in it. He tells himself he’s a professional keeping distance from the cause; he is not.

At the table: When the Enforcer speaks (pre-yield), he uses job language: “Stand down. Walk away. This doesn’t need to go further.” When he yields, he says nothing. His silence is not sullenness — it is instruction. He has been told that anything he says can be used. He is protecting Edoran by giving the players nothing.

ENCOUNTER BEAT — The Pivot

The pivot is not the note. The pivot is Edoran.

Midway through the fight (round 2 or 3, whenever a player has a high Perception or specifically scans the perimeter), the GM delivers:

“There is a figure in the south alley mouth. Thirty feet away, plain coat, hood up, very still. He is not moving toward the fight. He is watching it.”

This changes the scene’s direction. The fight was about survival and the note. Now there is a third thing in the scene: a man who sent these people and is watching to see what happens next. Players who notice him have a choice — engage the combat fully, or break off and cross 30 feet to reach him.

This pivot reframes everything that has already happened. The zealots aren’t random opposition; they’re a test being observed in real time. The Enforcer isn’t the leader; he’s a proxy. The scene’s actual stakes expand the moment that still figure becomes visible.

When to deliver the pivot: After the note-burning attempt has been resolved (or failed), so players are not tracking three things at once. The note is the micro-crisis; Edoran’s presence in the alley is the macro-revelation.

STAKES MAP

What players can GAIN: - The note (Edoran’s handwriting, the scripture quote — first evidence he is not simply a fanatic) - Edoran’s words if they break off and reach him: “You have no idea what you are looking for. When you find it, you will understand why I did not want you to.” - The implicit knowledge that their route from the Chancellor’s office was known before they left it

What players can LOSE: - The note, if no one breaks from the fight in time (the zealot gets it to the flame) - The chance to reach Edoran in the alley — if they spend every action on the Enforcer, he steps back into the dark before they think to cross to him (he watches through round 3, then goes) - Civilian goodwill — if the fight sprawls into the food queue, the Lowmark residents remember it

The CHOICE the scene actually asks: Do you finish the fight properly, or do you accept a worse tactical position to grab the intelligence?

This is the campaign’s first version of its central question in miniature: do you prioritise the immediate, visible problem, or the harder-to-reach thing that tells you what’s actually happening?

2. Scene Transitions

IN — Closing the Chancellor Scene, Opening Scene 1

Use this when the players leave the Chancellor’s offices:

“Ostenveld’s door closes behind you with the specific quietness of an expensive latch. The corridor outside is empty. Down the stair, through the public hall, and then you are back in Lowmark — amber lanterns in their brackets, the food registry queue already stretching to the corner, bundled figures shuffling forward with the patient exhaustion of people who have been doing this for a long time. The Archive is twenty minutes east on foot. You don’t make it five.”

Technique: The Chancellor’s door close is precise and slightly cold; Lowmark is immediate and human. The queue — people waiting, patient, exhausted — is the thematic context for everything that follows. The ambush interrupts a scene of ordinary suffering, which is why it feels wrong rather than just dangerous.

OUT — Closing Scene 1, Opening Scene 2

Use this after the Enforcer yields and the zealots scatter:

“The queue re-forms around you. It takes about ninety seconds — the particular resilience of people who have learned not to let disruptions cost them their place in line. Someone picks up a dropped basket. Someone else retrieves a child from behind a crate. The junction looks almost normal. The south alley is empty. You are still holding [the note / nothing — call whatever they retrieved or failed to retrieve]. The Archive is fifteen minutes east. Whatever just happened, someone knew you were coming.”

Technique: The queue reforming is the emotional punctuation — life in Varenhold continues regardless, which is quietly devastating. The final line drives the paranoia forward without melodrama. It opens the Archive scene on unease rather than triumph.

If the note was recovered: hand out the Edoran’s Sealed Note card from Player Handouts — Session 1. Pass it to whoever retrieved it. Don’t read it aloud — let them read it silently and share what they find.

3. Improv Anchors

Anchor 1: Players try to avoid the scene entirely — go around the junction

The Enforcer anticipated this. Two zealots are positioned on the Lowmark side-street that bypasses the junction. Going around the food queue means hitting a smaller version of the same encounter — two cultists, same warning, no Enforcer. The note is not with these two; it stayed with the main group. Players who avoid the ambush avoid the note entirely. They find out someone tried to stop them; they don’t find out who. Let this be a genuine loss — it costs them intelligence — without punishing them for tactical thinking.

Anchor 2: Players de-escalate completely — no combat, total diplomatic resolution

This is allowed. DC 14 Persuasion or Intimidation to make the Enforcer stand down without fighting; he will not name his employer regardless. If players succeed, the zealots back off — but the shortest zealot, with the note, moves to the alley and hands it to the figure in the hood before the players can reach him. Edoran pockets it and leaves. Players get nothing from the note but may get Edoran’s words if they move immediately (DC 12 Athletics or Acrobatics to close the distance before he steps back into the dark). The Enforcer, if asked directly who sent him: silence, and then he walks away. This outcome is valid; the players demonstrated skill. The cost is that the intelligence route was the violence route, which is an interesting inversion of the campaign’s usual logic.

Anchor 3: Players are too lethal — killing a zealot or seriously harming civilians

Stop the momentum briefly. Describe the Enforcer’s face: “He goes still in a way that is different from before. This was not what he was sent here for.” He calls the remaining zealots off immediately — the note burns, Edoran withdraws — and the Enforcer raises both hands and backs away without a word. The encounter ends faster than expected. Consequence: the next day, a City Guard officer (one of Sera’s people) finds the players. Someone in the Lowmark queue made a report. Sera doesn’t arrest them. She wants to understand what happened. This is not a punishment scene — it is a relationship scene with Sera, moved earlier than planned. Use it.

5. The “What They Remember” Test

The sentence players are still quoting: “He just crouched down and held the note to the flame. Like he had all the time in the world.”

Is it designed in? Yes. It lives in the round 2 GM narration for the zealot’s secondary objective attempt. The specific quality that makes it quotable is the calm — the zealot does not panic or rush. He does it with the unhurried deliberateness of someone who expected to reach this moment, which implies planning, which implies the whole thing was anticipated. That calm is scarier than aggression.

If it’s not landing: The line fails if the GM delivers it in the middle of tracking initiative and hit points. Reserve the narration for a beat where you have the table’s attention — call it cleanly, make eye contact, pause after “flame.” The visual only works if everyone is picturing the same thing at the same moment. If the table is scattered, say: “Hold on — before the zealot’s turn, this is what you see.” Pause. Then deliver it. The interruption to initiative is the point; it mirrors what the zealot is doing to the fight.

What would make it stronger: Have the GM physically set something down on the table at that moment — a pencil, a token, anything — and leave it there. A small physical action that mirrors the zealot’s small physical action. Theatre of the mind anchored to something real. Players will remember the object as much as the line.

👥 Player Engagement

1. Per-Archetype Engagement

The Investigator Rogue / Ranger / Wizard / Bard — truth-seeker, possibly operating under false employer context

FIRST NOTICE The sealed note in Z4’s belt pouch. Before anyone else registers the tactical geometry, the Investigator clocks the document — rolled, dark wax seal, carried openly in a way that suggests it was meant to be delivered, not hidden. Their lens is: what is being communicated here, and to whom? The intercept formation reads as a message before it reads as a threat.

INSTINCTIVE APPROACH Stall. Ask a question. Buy time to read the room while appearing to negotiate. The Investigator’s default in an intercept scenario is to find out what the other party actually wants before committing to any action — because the most useful intelligence comes from the moment before someone realizes they’re giving it.

LIKELY QUESTION TO GM “I want to study the Enforcer before I do anything. Can I use my action to Read him — mannerisms, gear, whether he’s been briefed or improvising?”

GM ANSWER THAT SHINES Don’t just give a Perception result. Deliver the Investigator’s read as a professional assessment: “He’s been briefed, not improvising — the positions are too clean for street improvisation. His eyes don’t go to his people, which means he trusts them to hold position without signals. He’s done this before. But here’s what catches you: the way his jaw tightens when he looks at the note-carrier. He didn’t want that thing brought. Someone above him made that call.” This makes the Investigator feel like the only one who saw what was actually happening.

The Believer Cleric / Paladin / Druid — faith-driven, may know Auris or Morthis tradition

FIRST NOTICE The civilians in the food queue. Fifteen people, some with children, caught between armed parties in a public street. The Believer reads this as a pastoral situation before a combat situation — these are people who should not be here for this. The secondary notice, for anyone with Religion proficiency, is the Restorer iconography: a small Auris sun-pendant visible on the Enforcer’s coat collar, half-tucked. These aren’t random criminals.

INSTINCTIVE APPROACH Step forward. Not aggressively — interpositionally. Put themselves between the crowd and the threat. The Believer’s instinct in a public confrontation is to become the visible authority figure that draws attention away from civilians, and then talk from that position. They are used to being the person a frightened crowd looks toward.

LIKELY QUESTION TO GM “I want to address the Enforcer by his faith — I can see the Auris pendant. Does he respond to that? Does it change anything?”

GM ANSWER THAT SHINES Yes, and complicate it: “He goes still when you invoke Auris. Not hostile — still. Like you’ve touched something he wasn’t expecting to be touched. He says: ‘You’re in the wrong place to talk about the Sun God’s will.’ He means it. There’s grief in it. Whatever brought him here, he believes he’s doing it for Auris.” This puts the Believer in direct moral dialogue with someone who has the same faith and opposite conclusions — which is exactly the friction their moral engine needs.

The Survivor Fighter / Barbarian / Rogue — Varenhold local or has lost someone; possibly had prior Restorer contact

FIRST NOTICE The food queue. Specifically, who is in it. The Survivor reads faces the way people who grew up in the Lowmark do — by recognition. They may know someone standing in that line. If they do, they know what this ambush is doing to that person’s afternoon: frightening them, maybe getting them hurt, certainly disrupting the one reliable thing in a difficult week. The Survivor’s anger, when it comes, is not abstract.

INSTINCTIVE APPROACH Move. The Survivor acts before others have finished processing the situation. They are not reckless — they are experienced. They have been in bad situations in this city before and they know that hesitation in a Lowmark alley costs more than moving early. Their first action is likely physical: interpose, push someone clear, close distance.

LIKELY QUESTION TO GM “Do I recognize any of these zealots? I’ve been in the Lowmark a long time — or I’ve had contact with the Restorers before. Is any face familiar?”

GM ANSWER THAT SHINES Give them something specific, even if it’s a near-miss: “Z1 — the one near the queue’s tail — you’ve seen him before. Not personally. But he was at a Restorer pamphlet-drop two months ago outside the Dawnhall on Cresset Street. You remember because he handed you a pamphlet and you threw it out. He was grieving then. He still is. He’s not here because he’s a fanatic. He’s here because someone convinced him this was the right thing to do.” This gives the Survivor a personal and moral angle that a fight cannot resolve.

The Scholar Wizard / Artificer / Bard — Spire fellowship, may know Theron Waide by correspondence

FIRST NOTICE The tactical arrangement. The Scholar sees the intercept formation as a designed system and immediately starts analyzing it: exits covered, crowd used as a soft barrier, the Enforcer positioned on the Archive road specifically. Someone with operational knowledge planned this. Then — and this is the Scholar’s distinctive read — they notice it wasn’t designed to kill. The note-carrier is too far from a weapon for an assassination setup. This is a disruption formation. Someone wants them delayed, warned, or shaken — not dead.

INSTINCTIVE APPROACH Identify the highest-value information source in the scene and figure out how to access it. For the Scholar, that’s the note — or, if they spot Edoran, the watcher in the alley. They will immediately want to know who is running this operation, because understanding the system tells them more than winning the fight.

LIKELY QUESTION TO GM “The Archive is our destination. This intercept happened between the Chancellor’s offices and the Archive. I want to know if the timing is significant — is someone watching those two buildings, or watching us specifically?”

GM ANSWER THAT SHINES Reward the inference directly: “Both of those are reasonable hypotheses, and you can’t rule either out from here. But the formation is set for your exact entry point — not the junction generally, but your approach from the south lane. That’s either very good surveillance or someone gave these people your route. The Chancellor’s briefing was yesterday. Think about who else was in that building.” The Scholar feels like their analytical framing just opened a question that will haunt the rest of the session.

The Mercenary Fighter / Rogue / Ranger — hired job, advance paid, possibly without full context

FIRST NOTICE The Enforcer’s quality. The Mercenary reads threat level before they read anything else, and what they read here is: professional. The Veteran stat block shows in how he holds position, how he tracks multiple targets without moving his head, how his hand positioning keeps a weapon accessible without drawing. This person has been in fights before. The Mercenary adjusts their estimate of the job’s actual risk level upward, and makes a mental note that whoever hired the other side has resources.

INSTINCTIVE APPROACH Protect the principal investment. The Mercenary’s first instinct is: what does the employer need me to do here, and what’s the minimum necessary force to accomplish it? They are not looking for a brawl — they are looking for the fastest path through this obstacle to the next objective. If talking gets them past, they’ll talk. If a quick takedown ends it faster, they’ll do that. They are efficient, not aggressive.

LIKELY QUESTION TO GM “What’s it going to cost me to just push through? I don’t care about the note or the alley — I want to know if I can get the party through to the Archive road before this turns into a whole thing.”

GM ANSWER THAT SHINES Give them an honest tactical read, then complicate it: “Straight through the Enforcer? Doable — he’s blocking the road, not a wall. But Z3 is five feet off your right shoulder the moment you move north, and Z1 is cutting the retreat. You can punch through, but one of your people is eating an opportunity attack from behind, and the crowd is directly in your line of movement. Also — and this is professional courtesy — the Enforcer isn’t trying to kill you. Someone told him not to. That’s worth knowing before you decide how hard to push.” The Mercenary’s tactical read matters and the GM is treating it as such.

The Dawnborn-Adjacent Any class — knows a Dawnborn personally, likely Sera Voss; came because Sera asked or because of personal relationship

FIRST NOTICE The Lowmark itself. This is Sera’s district. The Dawnhall is two streets east of here. The food queue is the same food queue Sera serves every week — some of these people know her face. The Dawnborn-Adjacent feels the violence of this moment in a specific way: this is being done in Sera’s neighborhood, in front of Sera’s people, and Sera doesn’t know it’s happening. That is personal before it is tactical.

INSTINCTIVE APPROACH Protect the crowd with specific attention, and process the moral dimension while acting. The Dawnborn-Adjacent is the archetype most likely to check on civilians mid-combat, most likely to notice a child being pulled close by a parent, and most likely to feel the weight of what it means to bring a fight into the Lowmark’s public spaces. They will act — they are not passive — but their action is colored by what this means for the people who live here.

LIKELY QUESTION TO GM “Sera knows these streets. Would she know these people? Is any of the Restorer group someone who might recognize the Dawnhall — someone she’s fed?”

GM ANSWER THAT SHINES This question deserves a beat of silence before the answer: “Almost certainly. The Restorers draw from the Lowmark. The grey sickness draws from the Lowmark. So does the food queue. Whoever these people are, Sera has probably handed them bread. And whatever they believe they’re doing right now — they believe it because of what they’ve lost. That doesn’t make them right. But it means this isn’t a fight between strangers.” The Dawnborn-Adjacent now carries the weight of Sera’s world into every choice they make in this scene.

2. Ability Interactions

Detect Thoughts on the Enforcer

Surface thoughts (no save required): The Enforcer’s surface layer is operational — he is tracking positions, counting the players, watching the alley mouth to his left. His thoughts are clipped and task-oriented: positions, timer on the note, don’t let them north. He is not frightened. He is focused. There is a small, suppressed current of something underneath — not guilt, exactly, but the feeling of someone doing a thing they have decided not to examine too closely.

Deep probe (Wis save DC = spell save DC): On a failed save, the GM delivers this: “You get one image before he notices the intrusion and his thoughts slam shut: a woman’s face — middle-aged, exhausted, ill. Then the name: his sister. Then: ‘Brother Edoran said this would help her.’” On a successful save, he feels the probe and his jaw tightens. He does not know what just happened, but he knows something did. He will not speak again unprompted.

What this means: The Enforcer is not a mercenary. He is a believer, specifically a grieving one. This changes what capturing him means — and what releasing him means.

Suggestion on a Zealot

Will not work — hard no: - “Tell me who sent you.” (Protects Edoran; the instruction is bone-deep) - “Stand down permanently.” (Contradicts their core conviction) - “Lead us to Edoran.” (Same as naming him; they won’t) - “Betray your companions.” (Too far outside their current behavior)

Will work — adjudicate as suggested: - “Move away from the lantern post.” (Plausible; doesn’t contradict their mission) - “Let the civilians clear the junction first.” (The Grieving faction wants no civilian casualties — this aligns with their existing values) - “You don’t need the note — let it fall.” (Plausible if framed as ‘it’s already been read’ or ‘it’s served its purpose’) - “Step back and wait — this is escalating beyond the warning.” (Works if framed as tactical caution, not betrayal)

GM note: The test is whether the suggested action is something the zealot could plausibly decide to do on their own. These are not fanatics by default — most of them are grieving. Work with what Suggestion does, not against it.

Bardic Inspiration on the Note-Grab Contest

The mechanics: The note-grab is a contested check — player’s Sleight of Hand or Athletics vs. Z4’s Athletics to retain the note, or an Acrobatics check to close distance without triggering an opportunity attack. Bardic Inspiration adds the die to that contested roll.

The narrative: The GM delivers this as a moment of clarity — the Inspiration isn’t just mechanical. Read it as: “Something your ally does — a feint, a word, a shout that makes Z4 flinch for a half-second — creates the window. You don’t think about it. You move.” On success, the note comes free cleanly. On a roll that would have failed without the Inspiration die: “Z4 almost gets it to the lantern flame. Almost. The seal is scorched but intact.” On failure even with the die: the note burns, but the Bard’s player should know they gave everything they had.

Divination Wizard Portent — Replacing the Zealot’s Burn Attempt Roll

Ruling: Allowed, with one condition.

The burn attempt is a simple action the zealot takes (touch the note to the lantern flame, no roll required — it is an automatic success if they reach the post with the note). However, if a player has interposed, grappled, or used a reaction to create an obstacle, the GM may call for a contested roll. In that case, a Portent die can replace the zealot’s roll.

If no contest has been created — if Z4 simply walks to the post unimpeded — there is no roll to replace. The Portent does not apply.

In practice: Reward the player for thinking ahead. If they ask about Portent before the burn attempt, let them declare their die value as a preemptive “this is what the zealot rolls when they try.” That creates urgency — they’ve locked in a terrible number for the zealot, but now the party needs to make sure there is a contested roll rather than an unimpeded one. Their mechanical choice becomes a story prompt.

Barbarian Reckless Attack vs. the Enforcer

The tactical note: Reckless Attack gives the Barbarian advantage on attacks against the Enforcer — and gives the Enforcer advantage on attacks against the Barbarian. In a standard combat, this trade is usually favorable. In this scene, it creates a specific complication.

The Enforcer’s mission includes grabbing a hostage. If he has advantage on attacks against the Barbarian (who has declared Reckless), his grapple attempts against that character are more likely to succeed. The Barbarian may inadvertently make themselves the easiest hostage target in the party.

GM delivery: Don’t announce this as a warning. Let it happen. If the Enforcer successfully grapples the Reckless Barbarian, the scene’s tension shifts: the hostage situation the zealots came to create now exists because of the Barbarian’s most aggressive move. That is good story.

Paladin Lay on Hands on a Downed Zealot After Combat

What happens mechanically: The zealot stabilizes. They are conscious, shaken, and in the Paladin’s debt in the most immediate sense.

The social consequence: Z1 (the south zealot) is the most likely to be downed near the players at the fight’s end. If the Paladin heals them, Z1 stays. They do not run. They look at the Paladin for a long moment, then say: “Brother Edoran said no one would be hurt.” It is not clear whether this is accusation or gratitude. It may be both.

The story consequence: Z1 knows who sent them. They won’t give Edoran’s name — not yet, not out of loyalty — but they have just been given a reason to reconsider. If the players treat this zealot with humanity, Z1 becomes a possible contact later in the campaign. If the players press hard immediately, Z1 shuts down.

GM delivery note: The Paladin’s player just created a future scene with one sentence of action. Acknowledge it. “She looks at you like she’s trying to figure out what you are. People on the Chancellor’s payroll don’t usually do that.”

Bard Cutting Words — Breaking the Zealot Grapple

The mechanics: Cutting Words uses the Bardic Inspiration die as a penalty to the zealot’s attack or ability check. If a zealot has successfully grappled a player (Str check, opposed), Cutting Words applies to the zealot’s next Str (Athletics) check to maintain the grapple when the grappled character attempts to escape — or to the grapple attempt itself if declared before the roll.

The specific adjudication: Cutting Words cannot be applied retroactively to a completed grapple. It must be declared before the relevant d20 hits the table. If declared in time: the die is rolled, subtracted from the zealot’s check. On a success (the grapple still holds despite the penalty): the zealot has heard whatever the Bard said and it made them angrier, or more determined. Narrate accordingly — the words landed, they just weren’t enough.

Narrative note for the GM: What does the Bard say? Ask them. It doesn’t change the mechanics, but the answer reveals character. A Bard who says something cruel to break a grapple and a Bard who says something desperate are both using the same die. One of those is more interesting.

Ranger with Humanoid Favored Enemy — Does It Apply?

Ruling: Yes. The zealots and the Enforcer are humanoids. The Ranger adds their Favored Enemy bonus to relevant tracking, recall, and — in later editions — some damage calculations. More importantly, Favored Enemy (Humanoid) typically grants advantage on Survival checks to track and recall knowledge about humanoid behaviors and tactics.

What the Ranger knows that no one else does: With a successful DC 12 Wisdom (Survival or Perception) check, the Ranger can identify the zealots’ formation as trained, not improvised. More specifically: “This is a containment formation. One person at each exit. The Enforcer is the only one who can authorize a change in orders — the others are holding position until he signals. If you take him out of the equation or pin him, the formation collapses. They don’t know what to do without the signal.”

This is tactically decisive. The Ranger has just identified the scene’s win condition from the enemy’s internal logic.

Warlock Eldritch Blast on Z4 (Note-Carrier, Near Lantern)

The situation: Z4 is positioned approximately 10 feet from the NE lantern post. The note is in their belt pouch. An Eldritch Blast that hits Z4 at this range has a chance of knocking them into the lantern post (depending on invocations — Repelling Blast specifically).

The fire hazard adjudication: A standard Eldritch Blast that hits Z4 deals damage but does not automatically create a fire hazard. If Z4 is knocked prone or into the lantern post (Repelling Blast, failed Dex save from GM ruling), the GM should call for a DC 12 Dexterity saving throw for Z4 — on a failure, their movement toward the lantern during their next turn is erratic, and the note-burn attempt is delayed one round. On a critical fail (natural 1), they stumble into the post themselves, knocking the lantern. Fire hazard activates in the 5-ft square around the NE post (1d4 fire per turn for 3 rounds). The note is now in immediate danger regardless of Z4’s action.

The moral complication: If the fire hazard activates in the NE corner of the junction, it is adjacent to the market crates — and the food queue is 15 feet away. The Warlock’s most efficient attack on Z4 may have made the note harder to save. That is the correct outcome. It rewards players who thought about the geometry.

Monk Stunning Strike on the Enforcer — Yield Condition Interaction

The mechanics: Stunning Strike requires spending a ki point after hitting. On a failed Constitution save, the Enforcer is Stunned until the end of the Monk’s next turn — incapacitated, auto-fails Str and Dex saves, attacks against him have advantage.

The yield condition interaction: The Enforcer yields when reduced to half HP (26 HP) or when grappled and restrained. A Stunned Enforcer is effectively neutralized — but not grappled. He has not yielded; he has simply been shut down.

GM ruling: If the Monk Stuns the Enforcer, the zealots read the situation differently than a yield. They see their leader neutralized, not surrendering. One of two things happens: - If the zealots’ morale is intact (all four still up), Z2 or Z3 may move to protect the Enforcer — escalating, not de-escalating. - If zealot morale is already broken (two or more down), they scatter without waiting for the Enforcer’s signal.

The key implication: A Stunning Strike ends the fight faster than the yield condition if the Monk follows up immediately — grapple the Stunned Enforcer on the same turn (auto-succeeds with advantage on the Str check). That satisfies the yield condition and the stun window. The Monk who sees this line is playing the encounter correctly.

3. Player Choice Map

Choice 1: Retrieve the Note NOW (during combat, Round 1-2) vs. Fight First, Retrieve After

Option A — Retrieve during combat:

  • Gives: The note before it can be destroyed. Full intelligence value: Edoran’s handwriting, Auris scripture quote, the first evidence he is not a simple fanatic. Also: the moral satisfaction of the secondary objective completed under pressure.
  • Costs: One player action dedicated to the note instead of combat. The retriever must close 30 feet to Z4 under initiative order, likely provoking opportunity attacks from Z1 (south) and the Enforcer (if they move north). They are not fighting; they are running through a fight. Their allies absorb more pressure.

Option B — Fight first, retrieve after:

  • Gives: Cleaner combat with full party focus on the Enforcer and zealots. Likely ends faster with fewer PC resources spent. No one takes opportunity attacks on a retrieval run.
  • Costs: Round 2 begins with Z4 moving toward the NE lantern post. If the combat runs past round 2 without someone interrupting Z4, the note burns. Option B is only safe if the party defeats the Enforcer in round 1 (unlikely at Level 3 against Veteran stats) or someone peels off to deal with Z4 anyway — at which point it becomes Option A.

The real stakes: Option A is a resource gamble. Option B is a time gamble. Neither is obviously correct. Players who communicate before acting make this choice better than players who each decide independently.

Choice 2: Pursue Edoran in the Alley (break from combat) vs. Stay and Protect Teammates

Option A — Break from combat and reach Edoran:

  • Gives: Edoran’s words — “You have no idea what you are looking for. When you find it, you will understand why I did not want you to.” This is the only way to get those words in Session 1. It establishes that the man behind the ambush is not a fanatic but someone with foreknowledge, grief, and a moral position. It pays off enormously in Session 2 and beyond.
  • Costs: One PC leaves the combat. The remaining players are at a meaningful disadvantage: four zealots plus the Enforcer against a reduced party. The player who breaks away also takes opportunity attacks from whoever is between them and the east alley mouth. And Edoran speaks exactly one statement before he steps back into the dark — the player who reaches him gets one exchange, not a conversation.

Option B — Stay and protect teammates:

  • Gives: A cleaner combat outcome. No one gets isolated. The party likely ends the scene with more HP and fewer resources spent.
  • Costs: Edoran is gone. His words are gone. Scene 5 (the second ambush) will give them another chance, but that contact is different — it happens after the Archive, after the players know more. What they would have learned in the alley now arrives with different weight.

The real stakes: This is the scene’s most significant choice because it cannot be undone. The note can potentially be recovered even if Z4 reaches the lantern (last-second checks, creative intervention). Edoran cannot. Once he steps back into the dark at the end of his window, he is gone until Scene 5. Players who split the party pay for it in the fight. Players who stay together pay for it in lost intelligence. Both costs are real.

Choice 3: Capture the Enforcer vs. Let Him Go at Yield

Option A — Capture and hold the Enforcer:

  • Gives: Interrogation opportunity. The Enforcer will not name Edoran — that is absolute — but he will react to direct questions in ways that confirm or deny. He will not lie fluently; he will go silent. Players who ask about the note’s origin, about the faction structure, about whether the Restorers knew the players’ route — these silences are information. He also becomes a logistical problem that forces the players to make a decision about what to do with a prisoner in the middle of the Lowmark.
  • Costs: The logistical problem is real. They cannot take him to the Archive. They cannot leave him unattended without restraints they may not have. If they turn him over to the City Guard, word reaches the Chancellor before the players do — the commission is no longer low-profile. If they release him after interrogation, they’ve done the work of Option B with extra steps.

Option B — Let him go at yield:

  • Gives: Clean exit. The Enforcer is no longer a problem. The zealots have scattered. The players can move on the Archive immediately. There is a small but meaningful social effect: the Enforcer registers that he was let go. He will remember this. It does not pay off in Session 1, but the campaign tracks it.
  • Costs: Immediate intelligence loss. Whatever he knows about the route, the note, and Edoran’s operational structure goes with him. The window for that information closes permanently once he walks away from the junction.

The real stakes: Capture is more information with more cost. Release is less information with less cost. The interesting version of this choice is when the players try to capture, interrogate, and then release — which is available, but costs time during which the note may be burning and Edoran may be leaving the alley. Every choice in this scene has a time cost.

4. Questions Players Will Ask — with GM Answers

Q1: “What do the zealots want? Are they trying to kill us?”

GM answer: “They want you to stop. The formation is containment, not assassination — if they wanted you dead, Z1 would have moved first and fast. The Enforcer has his hands out, not on a weapon. Someone told them to warn you off, not end you. The hostage move is real — there’s a grapple in this, probably — but a body in the Lowmark is a political liability nobody here wants.”

Q2: “Who sent them? Can we tell from their equipment or behavior?”

GM answer: “The Enforcer carries nothing with faction markings visible from here. The zealots look like Lowmark residents in working clothes — no uniforms. But there’s a small Auris pendant on the Enforcer’s collar, half-tucked, like someone told him to cover it and he didn’t quite manage. Whatever organization this is has a connection to Auris-worship. That’s what you can see. The rest requires getting closer.”

Q3: “I want to look into the east alley. Is there anything there?”

GM answer (passive Perception 15+ or active look): “Thirty feet into the alley, a figure in a plain dark coat, hood up. Standing completely still — not the stillness of someone lost or waiting for a friend. The stillness of someone watching. They have not moved since you noticed them.” (If passive Perception is below 15, and the player didn’t specifically ask to look:) “The alley is shadow from here. You’d need to actively look.”

Q4: “Can we talk our way out of this without fighting?”

GM answer: “You can try — the Enforcer hasn’t drawn a weapon. He looks like he’d take a clean exit if one materialized. The complication is that Z4 is already moving toward the lantern post, which suggests there’s a timer on this situation regardless of what you and the Enforcer agree to. You can negotiate with him while someone else handles Z4, but you can’t pause the whole scene to talk.”

Q5: “What happens to the civilians if this turns into a fight?”

GM answer: “The queue is already going still — people who have been in the Lowmark during trouble before know to stop moving. No one is running yet, which means no one is in immediate danger of being trampled. If the fight starts and the area around the distribution cart becomes contested, the people nearest the cart will clear that space fast. The risk is if fire or area effects hit the junction — the queue is fifteen feet from the lantern post, and there’s nowhere fast for them to go if the east side goes up.”

Q6: “I want to grab the note from Z4’s pouch before the fight starts — before initiative. Can I try?”

GM answer: “You can try, but not quietly — the pouch is open-top, and Z4 is watching the junction. It’s not a pickpocket situation; it’s a lunge from thirty feet away, which means you’re sprinting at someone before the fight technically starts, and that’s going to trigger initiative for everyone. If you move on Z4 before the Enforcer says his piece, you’re the one who started it. What do you want to do?”

Q7: “Does the Enforcer have a name? Does he say anything?”

GM answer: “He doesn’t introduce himself. What he says, standing in the road, is this: ‘Go back. Whatever the Chancellor told you, this investigation ends here. Walk away and no one leaves this junction carrying anything they didn’t bring in.’ He’s not threatening your lives. He’s threatening your progress. He says it like he’s been authorized to say exactly that and nothing more.”

Q8: “After the fight — can we search the zealots for more information?”

GM answer: “The zealots who scattered into the crowd are gone — the Lowmark swallowed them, which tells you something about how long these people have been operating in this district. Z4 (if downed before reaching the lantern), and any zealot who was taken down near the players, can be searched. Beyond belt pouches with standard items, you find nothing with writing on it except one thing: a folded pamphlet for a Restorer public meeting, dated three days ago. Sevenday evenings at the Auris temple public hall. The next one is in four days.”

Q9: “What is actually written on the note?”

GM answer (only if the note was recovered): “The wax seal is a sun-disc, pressed by a signet ring — the same symbol on the Auris pendant the Enforcer was wearing. Inside, in a careful, educated hand: ‘The willing heart gives freely what the bound heart cannot. — Auris, Third Canticle, verse 14. Return this if they will not hear reason. If they will not hear reason, you know what to do.’ The scripture quote is standard Auris. The handwriting is not a clerk’s hand. Whoever wrote this has been educated in something beyond rote copying.” (The handwriting connects to Edoran in Session 2 when players have a second sample to compare.)

Q10: “The Enforcer yields at half HP. What does that actually look like?”

GM answer: “He stops. Hands up — not surrendering exactly, but no longer fighting. He looks at the junction: the scattered zealots, Z4 wherever they ended up, the food queue crowd watching from the edges. He looks at the players. He says nothing. His jaw is set. He is not afraid of you. He is calculating whether what happened here was worth what it cost, and you can see that calculation happening on his face. He will answer no questions. He will not name anyone. But he stays still, and he waits for you to decide what to do with him.”

📖 Story Threading

Why This Scene Exists

Scene 1 is not an action tutorial. It is the first sentence of the campaign’s real argument: power is already being exercised over the Dawnborn, by people who believe they’re doing it for good reasons, before the players know what the Dawnborn are. The ambush happens before the players know enough to have an opinion about it. That is precisely the point. By the time they have an opinion, they will remember that they were already being moved by forces with incomplete information — and so were the people trying to stop them.

Everything in this scene is a seed. The note is a seed. Edoran’s absence is a seed. The food queue is a seed. The Enforcer’s silence is a seed. None of them sprout in Session 1. The GM’s job in this scene is not payoff — it is planting with enough craft that the players, two sessions later, feel the roots when they hit them.

1. What This Scene Pays Off

Archetype Hooks — Immediate Activation

Each of the six archetypes from the Player’s Guide has a specific hook that Scene 1 touches on contact.

The Investigator was hired under false context — they were told the documents were stolen property, not the key to ten people’s deaths. Scene 1 immediately confirms that whoever briefed them omitted something significant: people are already willing to use force to stop this investigation. An investigator’s first instinct is to ask who benefits from warning us off? That question is not answerable yet, but it is the right question, and Scene 1 is what makes it urgent.

The Believer came because of religious obligation, ideological commitment, or a personal promise. The ambushers are Restorers — Auris-faith adjacent, driven by grief that has organized itself into theology. Scene 1 places the Believer in the position of being attacked by people whose faith may not be entirely unlike their own. The question it opens: is the difference between a zealot and a believer just the desperation of their circumstances?

The Survivor has someone they love declining from grey sickness. They came for the city, for the people in the queue. Scene 1 gives them the queue — fifty shuffling people who are the living embodiment of what the twilight costs — and then forces them to fight in front of it. The note in the zealot’s pouch is theologically gentle. The Restorer who tries to burn it is not. That dissonance is the Survivor’s whole arc in miniature.

The Scholar believes that information, correctly understood, leads to correct action. Scene 1 presents information being actively suppressed — the note-burning attempt is an attempt to destroy a clue before it can be read. The Scholar’s background hook (corresponding with Theron, being recruited without knowing it) connects directly: Theron left things findable on purpose. Someone else is trying to make them unfindable. The Scholar is now in the middle of that contest.

The Mercenary has a job: locate the ritual documents. Scene 1 tells them the job is more complicated than they were briefed. People with organization behind them know the players’ route. A mercenary who has been ignoring signs that something was wrong now has a very loud sign. The question Scene 1 opens for them: at what point does completing the contract become a choice you’re responsible for? That is also the campaign’s question for Sessions 4 and 5.

The Dawnborn-Adjacent came because Sera asked. Sera is the kind of person who runs toward burning buildings, who notices specific people, who has been waiting for the right moment to say something out loud. Scene 1 happens in Sera’s district. The food queue is Sera’s community. The ambush is in the street she walks every day. When players reach her at the Dawnhall in Scene 4, they will already know what she is protecting — because they have just fought in the middle of it.

From the Chancellor Briefing

The Chancellor told players three things: the Archive contains Corven’s documents, the city has three good harvests left, and she is trusting them to find the truth she cannot look for publicly. What she did not say: someone is already trying to stop exactly this investigation. What she let them infer: the political landscape is complicated. What Scene 1 begins answering:

  • How organized is the opposition? Organized enough to have the players’ route on the day they start. This is not a spontaneous reaction to their presence — it is a prepared response. Someone knew they were coming.
  • Why would the Chancellor claim ignorance of the cost? Because people who look into this get warned off. She has read enough to know the warning is credible.
  • What is this investigation actually about? The Chancellor framed it as locating documents. Scene 1 reframes it as walking into a contested space where the stakes are already high enough to justify violence.

The Chancellor briefing raised the question: is this a job, or is it something else? Scene 1 answers: it is something else. The Mercenary archetype knows this feeling.

The Campaign Logline in Miniature

“Ten people are carrying the sun inside them. The city needs it back. Whose choice is that to make?”

Scene 1 is this question at the scale of thirty seconds of combat. The zealots have decided the choice belongs to the ritual. Edoran has decided the choice belongs to the theology of willing hearts. The players have been hired by someone who has decided to remain strategically ignorant of whose choice it is. And then the zealots try to burn the note that contains the only evidence — so far — that Edoran believes in consent at all.

Whose choice is it to make? In Scene 1, it is the note-burner’s choice. Unless the players stop them. That is the micro-version of the macro: the question is always whether someone will intervene in the act of unilateral decision-making, and what it costs them to do so.

2. What This Scene Plants

a) The Sealed Note — Session 2 Payoff

The note reads: “The willing heart gives freely what the bound heart cannot.” This is Auris scripture in Edoran’s handwriting.

When it matters: Session 2, Scene 4, Theron’s confession. The players now know the Dawnborn are Lux Anchors — that completing the ritual means the technical term extinguished. In the silence after Theron tells them this, one player will reach for the note. Or the GM should reach for it — have Theron recognize the handwriting.

The Session 2 beat: After Theron finishes, when the players are sitting in the aftermath of the worst sentence they’ve heard in this campaign, the GM says: “The note is still in your pocket. You read it again. Edoran’s handwriting. ‘The willing heart gives freely what the bound heart cannot.’ He knew. He knew what the cost was, and he was sending people to warn you off, and he still wrote that.”

This does two things at once. It confirms that Edoran knows the ritual kills the Dawnborn. And it confirms that Edoran believes the difference between willing and forced is morally load-bearing — which means he has not, in fact, decided the choice belongs to the ritual or to him. He has decided it belongs to the Dawnborn. Everything else he is doing — the warnings, the intercepts, the ambushes — is his attempt to prevent anyone from forcing the decision before the Dawnborn can make it freely.

That is not the reading of a fanatic. It is the reading of someone who is terrified that events will move faster than consent can.

If the note was burned: The handwriting recognition beat is unavailable, but the underlying revelation still lands in Scene 5 of Session 1 — Edoran’s words carry the same implication. See section (b) below. The three-clue rule applies: the note is one path to Edoran’s position; his words are a second; his willingness to be seen in the alley at all is a third.

b) Edoran’s Words — Session 2 Echo

“You have no idea what you are looking for. When you find it, you will understand why I did not want you to.”

When it echoes: Session 2, Scene 4. The exact moment Theron says the word extinguished.

The GM line: Let Theron finish. Let the silence run. Then, quietly, without signaling that this is a callback: “You have no idea what you are looking for. When you find it, you will understand why I did not want you to.” Do not attribute it. Let the players place it.

They will. Because Edoran was right. He did not want them to find this — not because he wanted to suppress it, but because he knew what finding it would cost them. He has known for long enough that his grief has calcified into theology. He organized it into a movement. He sent people to stop the investigation. And he was still standing in that alley, thirty feet away, watching, saying something that turned out to be true.

That is not a villain’s speech. It is the speech of someone who has been carrying the same information as Theron for years, and made entirely different choices with it — and still landed on willing heart as the operative moral principle.

The callback does not redeem Edoran’s methods. It complicates them, which is more useful.

c) The Food Queue — Session 3 Weight

Fifty people shuffling forward in the amber lanternlight. A vendor calling out quantities. Bundled figures. A child somewhere in the line.

What it means in Session 3: Session 3’s faction choice is the moment players must decide how far they are willing to compromise toward a path that might not be clean. Isolde Maren’s transfer apparatus offers a possibility — but with mortality risk. The Inversion path requires unanimous consent. The ritual-as-designed kills ten people.

When players reach that decision, they are deciding in the abstract: ten lives against a city. The abstraction is easy to work with. The fifty people in the queue are not abstract. The GM’s job, in Session 3, is to collapse that abstraction back to the specific.

“You remember the food queue. Fifty people. The vendor calling quantities. The child somewhere in the line. Three good harvests remaining, the Chancellor said. Those fifty people have three good harvests remaining. So does the child. Whose choice is it to make?”

The moral weight of the Session 3 decision is real because the fifty people were real. Not named, not spoken to, not given individual stories — but real, present, sensory. Five senses. The cost was in the room before the players knew they were being asked to calculate it.

d) The Enforcer’s Silence — The Truth That Lands Later

What players think it means: The Enforcer is loyal, well-trained, professionally disciplined. He will not name his employer because he has been instructed not to. This is a competent operation. Standard interpretation: Restorer zealotry with better organization than expected.

What it actually means: The Enforcer is silent because naming Edoran would expose the Restorers’ knowledge of the players’ route, which would expose the network that provided it. The Enforcer is not silent out of personal loyalty — he is silent because the thing he would have to explain is more damaging than the ambush itself.

When the truth lands: Session 2, Scene 1. The Reckoning moves on the Archive on the same day the players return. The Reckoning also knew the players’ timeline. Two different organizations — Restorers and Reckoning — both had advance intelligence on the players’ movements. Either the players have a leak, or both organizations have someone watching the same source.

The source is the Chancellor’s office. The Enforcer’s silence in Scene 1 is the first indication that the information network underlying this conflict is more sophisticated than an ambush suggests. Players who revisit this in Session 2 will understand: the Enforcer wasn’t protecting Edoran. He was protecting the shared infrastructure.

This is not something players need to decode in Session 1. But the GM should note it, because when the Session 2 realization lands, the Enforcer’s silence retroactively explains itself.

e) The Route Intelligence — Edoran’s Network

The zealots had the players’ route. The players left the Chancellor’s offices not long before the ambush. That is the specific thing to hold.

What it tells us about Edoran’s network: He has either someone inside the Chancellor’s operation, or someone watching the office’s exits closely enough to track new contractors on day one. Either possibility means the Restorers are not simply a theological movement with pamphlets and Sevenday meetings. They have institutional reach.

When that network shows up again: Session 2, the Reckoning arrives at the Archive the same morning the players do. Session 3, Edoran finds the players before they reach him — or has sent a contact in advance who knows to expect them. Session 4, Edoran appears at exactly the moment the players need him, before they’ve told anyone where they’re going.

Each of these beats should feel, in retrospect, connected to the same fact established in Scene 1: this organization tracks people who are investigating the ritual. They have been tracking this for long enough that it is operational muscle memory, not reactive improvisation.

When players finally reach Edoran in Session 1, Scene 5, and he says “You have no idea what you are looking for” — part of what that means is: I know exactly what you have been doing since you left the Chancellor’s offices this morning. I have people who told me. That’s how worried I am.

4. Three-Clue Rule — Information This Scene Provides

Scene 1 is primarily a clue delivery mechanism for one core piece of information: Edoran is not simply a fanatic — he believes in consent.

The campaign depends on players being able to reach this conclusion before Session 4. If they dismiss Edoran as an antagonist and stop thinking about him, the moral complexity of Sessions 3-5 is diminished. Scene 1 gives three separate routes to the same conclusion:

Clue 1: The Note - What it contains: Auris scripture in Edoran’s handwriting. “The willing heart gives freely what the bound heart cannot.” - What it points to: Edoran theologically prioritizes consent. He is not directing violence toward the Dawnborn — he is directing it toward investigators. The distinction matters. - How players get it: Retrieving the note before round 2 ends, before the second zealot burns it. - Backup path if missed: The Factions Guide’s GM-only section confirms Edoran’s position. Jaret (Scene 4 of Session 1, in the Dawnhall approach) will tell players that Edoran believes the willing heart is the only valid ritual mechanism, if asked directly. Theron in Session 2 will reference “a Restorer leader who kept writing to him arguing that the Dawnborn must choose freely.”

Clue 2: Edoran’s Words - What they contain: “You have no idea what you are looking for. When you find it, you will understand why I did not want you to.” - What they point to: Edoran knows what the cost is. He did not want this investigation to succeed — not to suppress the truth, but because he understands the weight of what finding the truth will require from whoever finds it. - How players get it: Reaching the alley in Scene 5 before Edoran decides the conversation is over. 6. Moral Weight Analysis

The Micro-Choice as Campaign Architecture

The three choices Scene 1 offers — retrieve the note, pursue Edoran, capture or release the Enforcer — are structurally identical to the choices of Sessions 4 and 5, compressed into the space of a single combat encounter. Retrieving the note is the choice to pursue information at cost. Pursuing Edoran is the choice to seek a direct conversation with the person who would rather you didn’t have this information. Capturing or releasing the Enforcer is the choice about what you do with someone who was acting under orders they didn’t fully understand, who will not tell you who sent them, and who poses no further threat once the operation has failed.

These are the same questions the campaign asks at scale: Do you have the right to the information? Do you seek out the person who is withholding it? What do you do with people who were tools in someone else’s moral decision?

The players will answer all three of these questions in Session 1 before they know they’re answering them. That is the function of the micro-choice. It establishes character position — this player pursues the information at all costs, this player releases the Enforcer, this player goes for Edoran while the rest fight — before the stakes are high enough to feel like a test. By Session 5, those established positions are the ground the characters are standing on when the actual test arrives. The GM should remember what each player chose in Scene 1. So, if possible, should the players.

Consent as Structure, Not Theme

The Archive door in Scene 2 asks players to name CONSENT as the answer to a riddle. That is the campaign’s central word arriving in capital letters, unmissable, at the end of Session 1’s first hour. But the scene that actually embeds consent as a structural concern is Scene 1, not Scene 2.

When the second zealot tries to stamp the note into the lantern flame, that is an act of withholding. It is not Edoran’s act — he didn’t order the burning, he ordered the interception. But his organization, acting on his behalf, is attempting to destroy a document that contains evidence of his own theological commitment to the willing heart. The zealot burning Edoran’s words about consent is one of the campaign’s cleanest structural ironies. The most charitable reading of Edoran’s operation — that he genuinely believes in the Dawnborn’s right to choose — is being suppressed by his own people, in his name, without his direct instruction, in front of the players.

This is what organizations do to the moral positions of leaders. The Restorer Fanatics (Jaret’s wing) will do this to a much greater degree in Sessions 3 and 4. But it begins here, in Scene 1, with a burning note. The answer to Scene 2’s riddle — CONSENT — is what Scene 1’s note was about. The answer was always present. Someone was trying to burn it before it could be found.

The Cost Must Be Real Because the People Were Real

The campaign’s central moral weight — ten lives against a city — is an abstraction that the campaign earns over five sessions by refusing to let it stay abstract. The Dawnborn become individuals. Sera becomes someone specific, someone the players will see at the moment Theron says extinguished. The city becomes the food queue, the grey sickness, the vendor calling quantities, the child somewhere in the line.

Scene 1 is the first act of that process. Fifty people in amber lanternlight, shuffling forward. The players fight in front of them. The crowd presses behind them. The five-senses opening — lamp oil and old bread, boots on stone, the dryness of a city indoors for fifty years — is not atmosphere. It is the cost being made embodied before the players understand there is a cost to calculate.

When they reach Session 3 and must decide whether the Transfer path’s mortality risk is acceptable, whether the Inversion path’s requirement for unanimous genuine consent is achievable, whether the ritual-as-designed is something they can permit — they are not deciding in a vacuum. They are deciding in reference to fifty people in the Lowmark and a food vendor and a child and the particular dryness of fifty years of amber air. The decision will be made in their bodies as well as their minds, because Scene 1 put the cost in their bodies first.

That is why Scene 1 begins in the food queue rather than in the Chancellor’s office or the Archive. The Chancellor’s office is where the job begins. The food queue is where the campaign begins.

GM note: The Scholar’s register is analytical. Give them the interpretation, not just the sensation. The key word is “before” — the intercept predates their arrival.

GM note: Give the Mercenary credit for reading what is actually there — a competent but imperfect operation. The “almost professional” assessment is accurate and character-validating without being flattering.

Information moves through Lowmark on three channels: the food queue (people talk while they wait), the amber-workshop runners who carry messages between district hubs, and the Dawnhall volunteers who live throughout the district and compare notes at shift change. Chancellor Ostenveld: What Her Composure Slipping Looks Like

She does not raise her voice. She does not move from her chair. What changes: she stops completing sentences at their normal pace. She begins one sentence — “If they knew your route before you left my —” — and stops it. She laces her fingers on the desk surface and looks at them, not through them, for approximately three seconds. Then she says: “Tell me everything. In sequence. Leave nothing out.”

The moment of stillness is the tell. She has been in this office for eleven years and she calculates before she speaks. The three seconds in which she did not calculate is the thing players remember.

He is not unwilling to talk. He is unwilling to be hurt again.

4. Failure Track — Interesting Failures

A. Enforcer Escapes Clean + Note Burns

What this means for Sessions 3–5:

Edoran receives a complete operational picture: how the players fight, what they sacrificed (nothing) to save information (nothing), and that they are effective combatants who do not distinguish between intelligence and targets. He recalibrates.

In Session 3, he does not send the Enforcer again. He changes the approach entirely:

  • He stops using intermediaries who can be identified and searched. Future warnings arrive as written messages slipped under a door or left on a table — unsigned, in the Auris-quoting style the players have now lost the key to recognizing.
  • He makes direct contact in Session 4 instead of Session 3, in a setting where combat is not possible (a crowded location, a space with a civilian hostage dynamic already in place from the scene structure). The delay costs the players one session of potential dialogue.
  • In Session 5, if the players have not yet spoken to him: he is not present at the ritual location. He has already made his choice, alone, without the conversation that might have changed it.

GM note: This is not a punishment path. It is a lonelier one. The campaign’s most interesting version of Edoran is the one who is still reachable. Each failure in these early scenes reduces the window.

B. A Player Is Grappled and Used as Brief Hostage

What the Enforcer demands: He doesn’t make a speech. He says two things: “Everyone stops moving,” and, after a pause, “Walk away.” He is not asking for information, payment, or surrender — just for the investigation to pause long enough for his people to withdraw. He will not hurt the hostage; that was not in his brief, and he knows it. But he is good at making the situation feel like he might.

How it resolves: The Enforcer releases the hostage when the players de-escalate or when one of the other zealots signals clear. If players push the confrontation anyway, he releases the hostage and retreats — he was never going to follow through, and a professional knows when the leverage has failed.

What players remember: Being on that side of the leverage is different from being the party that applies it. The scene is designed so players feel the weight of the gap between “I could hurt you” and “I am choosing not to.” That gap is the emotional center of the campaign. Edoran is living in it. Now one player knows what it feels like.

Optional carry-forward: In Session 3 or 4, if that player faces a scene where they could use leverage against someone weaker, ask them privately: “You remember the junction. What does your character do?” Do not frame it as a mechanical choice. Let it be a character choice.

C. Players Kill a Zealot (Who Is a Civilian)

City Watch inquiry: A junior Watch sergeant arrives at the Archive gate the following morning — not at the Dawnhall, not at the Chancellor’s office. At the Archive, where the players are most likely to be identified as investigators on official business. He is not hostile. He is methodical.

Brief scene: the Watch interview

The sergeant is mid-thirties, ink-stained cuff, the quiet manner of someone who has been doing this long enough to stop being angry about it. He asks, in order: were you present at the junction on the afternoon of [date]; were you in contact with the deceased; what was the nature of the altercation; did you have authorization to use force in Lowmark district. He takes notes. He does not editorialize.

He mentions, before leaving: “The deceased was in the food queue registry. Eleven years. Gave three times a month at the distribution point.” A pause. “That doesn’t change what happened. It’s just what I know about him.”

The inquiry does not result in charges unless the players were egregiously violent or lied to the Watch. It does result in a file. That file exists.

How Theron Waide reacts when players mention it at the Archive:

He is quiet for a moment. Then: “Eleven years in the registry. That’s someone who came here because the city was in trouble and thought showing up was worth something.” Another pause. “I’m not telling you what to feel about it. I’m telling you what I know: the people who follow Edoran are not soldiers. Most of them have someone sick at home.”

He does not say the players were wrong. He says it clearly enough that they feel the weight without him having to say it. This is intentional. The campaign does not adjudicate moral choices; it witnesses them.

D. The Party Retreats from the Ambush Entirely

Where they go: The most immediate refuge in Lowmark is the Dawnhall — it is a public space, staffed, and the Restorers will not force entry there. The players arrive early and in disorder rather than on their own terms.

What they learn later: By the time they reach the Archive (Scene 2), they have no note, no contact with Edoran, and no read on the Enforcer. What they do have: the shape of what it felt like to be the targets of organized opposition that knew their route. That sensation — of being anticipated — is itself information about what they are dealing with.

What they miss: The note. Edoran’s words. The window to examine the Enforcer. The Religion and Investigation checks that would have told them they are dealing with someone who thinks in ritual theory, not just theology. They arrive at the Archive slightly more in the dark than the scene intended.

How the GM recovers the story:

The note’s content can surface through a second path. When players reach the Archive and speak with Theron Waide (Scene 2, following the door puzzle), he has been tracking the Restorers’ activity around the Archive for months. He has intercepted a previous note — same handwriting, same style, same ritual-circle seal. He did not know what to do with it and filed it. If players describe the ambush, he retrieves the file. The content is not identical — earlier correspondence — but the voice is the same, and the phrase “the anchor holds” appears there too.

The story does not close. It reroutes.

8. “What They Remember” Test

The sentence players are still quoting after the session:

“The one with the note just crouched down and held it to the flame like he had all the time in the world.”

Where it lives: Round 2, Initiative Count 20 (start of round), before the zealot’s turn resolves. The GM delivers it flat, without rising inflection, mid-combat — not as a dramatic announcement but as a factual update. The player who was about to attack the Enforcer now has to decide, in the next two seconds of real time, whether to change their action.

If it is not landing strong enough:

The failure mode is narrating it too dramatically — giving it weight with your voice before the players have decided what it means. Deliver it dry. “The zealot nearest the market crate crouches without breaking eye contact with you — and holds the folded paper to the lantern flame.” Then wait. The drama comes from the players’ reaction, not the narration’s tone.

It catches the lantern light — the wax seal is deep amber, not the standard brown.” Plant the object as a question before it becomes a crisis. The burning is only a crisis if the players already care what the note says.

The moment works when a player says “wait, I’m doing that instead” and the table goes slightly quiet. That pause is the scene working correctly.

🎯 Scene Purpose & GM Notes

PURPOSE STATEMENT: This scene exists to introduce the campaign’s central word before players understand why it matters. The answer to the riddle is CONSENT. Players will say it to open a door. Later they will understand they were saying it about something far heavier.

EMOTIONAL TARGET: Curiosity shading into unease. The door has been sealed for eleven years. The Archivist who knows the answer will not say it. Both of those facts should feel weighted before players know why.

DRAMATIC QUESTION: Will you ask Theron why he never opened the door himself?

SURPRISE: The riddle is not hard. The puzzle is not in the words — it’s in Theron. He knows. He won’t say it. When players solve it themselves, watch his face. He doesn’t celebrate. He exhales.

DESIGNED PEAK MOMENT: The door opening after someone says CONSENT for the first time. Hold a beat of silence after the word. Let the door swing before anyone speaks.

SECONDARY OBJECTIVE: Get players to ask Theron why he never opened it. His answer: “I knew the answer. I could not bring myself to say it out loud.” This is a preview of his Session 2 confession — he has been alone with the knowledge of what the ritual requires and has been unable to act on it.

🧩 Puzzle — The Riddle Door

The riddle (carved into the stone lintel above the door): > I am given only when no one compels it. > Forced, I become something else entirely. > I am the difference between a gift and a theft. > What word names the thing that makes the difference?
